A deck that looked great ten years ago can send mixed signals today: a few soft boards here, a wobbly post there, and suddenly you're not sure whether a quick fix is enough or whether you're throwing money at a losing cause. That question comes up constantly among Sioux Falls homeowners, especially those with decks that are 15 to 20 years old and starting to show their age. Before you call a contractor, it helps to understand what actually determines that answer. For the full picture on building new, see [Building a Deck in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/deck-guide-sioux-falls).

## The Frame vs. the Boards: Why It Matters

The single most important factor in a repair-or-replace decision is the condition of the structural frame, not the surface you walk on.

Boards are the skin. The frame, which includes posts, beams, joists, and the ledger board where the deck meets the house, is the skeleton. You can resurface a deck for a fraction of the cost of a rebuild, but only if the frame underneath is solid.

A contractor should probe every joist and post with an awl or screwdriver. Soft spots, crumbling wood, or wood that collapses under light pressure are signs of rot that goes deeper than the surface.

Ask specifically:

- Are the posts sitting in direct contact with soil or concrete? Ground contact accelerates rot dramatically.
- Is the ledger board flashed correctly, or has moisture been getting behind it for years? The [ledger board is one of the most common failure points on older decks](/guides/deck-ledger-flashing).
- What wood species was used originally? Some species resist rot longer than others.

If a contractor looks only at the decking boards and gives you a resurfacing quote without lifting boards to check the frame below, that is a red flag worth noting.

## Signs That Point Toward Replacement

Some findings push the math strongly toward a full rebuild, even when it stings to hear it.

Widespread rot in the joists or beams means you would essentially be building a new frame anyway. At that point, resurfacing costs added to frame repair costs can exceed the cost of starting fresh, often with better materials and a longer result.

Frost heave is another signal worth taking seriously. When footings were not dug deep enough to stay below the frost line, the posts move with the ground through freeze and thaw cycles. That movement stresses every connection in the frame. According to City of Sioux Falls Building Services, footing depth requirements exist for exactly this reason, and you should confirm the required depth directly with Building Services before any new footings are poured. If an old deck shows obvious lean or bounce that traces back to footing movement, a repair-only approach rarely holds up.

Other signs that often point to replacement:

- Multiple posts with rot at or below the base
- Ledger damage that has compromised the house's band joist or rim board
- Hardware (joist hangers, post bases, lag bolts) that is heavily corroded or pulling loose
- A deck that was built without a permit and has no record of inspection, leaving structural quality unknown

If you are unsure what you are looking at, [the spring safety checklist for older decks](/guides/old-deck-safety-check) walks through each structural component and what to look for.

## Signs That Point Toward Repair or Resurfacing

Not every aging deck needs to come down. Sometimes the frame is genuinely sound and the surface is what has failed.

If joists and beams are solid, posts are plumb and firm, and hardware is intact, replacing the decking boards and railings can give you a deck that feels and functions like new. This is especially common on decks built with pressure-treated framing, which often outlasts the decking boards laid on top of it.

Composite decking is a popular choice when homeowners resurface an existing sound frame, and it tends to hold up well through Sioux Falls winters without the annual sealing that wood requires. The tradeoff questions around material choice are covered in depth in [Wood or Composite Decking in Sioux Falls](/guides/wood-vs-composite-deck).

Spot repairs (a single rotted board, one loose post base, a stair stringer that cracked) are reasonable when damage is genuinely isolated and the rest of the structure checks out. The mistake is treating spot repairs as a reason to avoid a full inspection. Isolated rot is often not isolated.

## Permit and Licensing Facts You Need to Know

Whether you repair or replace, local rules apply and they are worth understanding before you sign anything.

According to the City of Sioux Falls, decks require a building permit. That applies to new decks and to work that qualifies as a remodel or addition. Painting and similar finish work do not require a permit, but structural repairs and full replacements typically do. Confirm the specifics of your project with City Building Services before work begins.

The City also requires a Residential Building Contractor's License for any contractor working on residential one and two family homes or townhomes within city limits. The City does not accept out-of-state licenses. You can verify any contractor's license on the Currently Licensed Contractors list at siouxfalls.gov/licensed-contractors before you hire.

Homeowners who own and live in a single-family home can pull a separate homeowner permit for their own work, but structural deck work is technical and the permit inspection process exists for real reasons.

If a contractor discourages you from pulling a permit or says it isn't necessary for a deck rebuild, that is a serious warning sign.

## Questions to Ask Before You Decide

A good contractor will help you understand the decision rather than push you toward the more expensive option automatically. Sioux Falls homeowners have noted, in public reviews and project requests, that the contractors they trusted most were the ones who explained their findings clearly and showed up when they said they would.

Here are the questions worth asking at the estimate stage:

- Did you probe the joists and posts, or only look at the surface?
- If you found rot, how far does it extend and what would full frame repair involve?
- Are the footings deep enough to meet current City of Sioux Falls requirements?
- If I resurface rather than replace, what is the realistic lifespan of the result given the frame condition?
- Will you pull the permit, and are you licensed with the City of Sioux Falls?
- What does the quote include: demolition, haul-away, permit fee, and cleanup?

Getting two or three written quotes that answer these questions in detail will tell you far more than any single contractor's verbal opinion. For a full breakdown of what to compare across quotes, see [How to Compare Deck Quotes](/guides/deck-quotes).

The honest answer to repair versus replace is almost always: it depends on the frame. Any process that skips a real structural inspection before giving you an answer is guessing, and that guess is expensive either way.
