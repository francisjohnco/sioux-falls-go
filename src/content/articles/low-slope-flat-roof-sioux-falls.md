Flat and low-slope roofs show up more often in Sioux Falls than many people realize. You find them on additions, garages, older bungalows in neighborhoods like McKennan Park and All Saints, and commercial-style homes with modern architecture downtown. They work differently from a steep shingle roof, they fail differently, and they need a contractor who actually knows them. For the full picture of roof repair and replacement in this city, start with the [Roof Repair and Replacement in Sioux Falls, the Complete Guide](/guides/roof-repair-replacement-sioux-falls).

One of the most common complaints from local homeowners is getting the run-around when they call for a flat roof quote. Many roofers who handle shingles all day will simply decline. That is not them being unhelpful. It reflects the fact that low-slope systems are a genuinely different trade.

## What Makes a Roof "Low-Slope" and Why It Matters in Sioux Falls

A low-slope roof is generally anything with less than a 3-in-12 pitch, meaning it rises less than 3 inches for every 12 inches of horizontal run. A true flat roof has almost no pitch at all, just enough to drain.

That matters here because Sioux Falls gets plenty of snow every winter, and a low-slope roof cannot shed that snow the way a steep pitch can. Snow sits. When temperatures swing above and below freezing repeatedly through a South Dakota winter, that snow melts and refreezes in cycles that push water under seams and around drains.

Ponding water is the defining problem on low-slope roofs. If water pools and sits for more than 48 hours after a rain or melt event, it accelerates the breakdown of almost every membrane material. A well-designed low-slope roof should drain completely. If yours does not, that is worth addressing before any repair or replacement.

## The Four Main Low-Slope Systems, in Plain Terms

**Tar and gravel (built-up roofing, or BUR)**

This is the oldest system and what a lot of Sioux Falls additions and older homes have. It is built up in layers: alternating plies of felt or fiberglass and hot-mopped asphalt, finished with a layer of gravel.

Done well, it is durable and provides decent insulation value. Done poorly or left without maintenance, the gravel hides cracks and blisters until water is already inside. Finding the actual leak source under gravel is harder than it sounds.

**Modified bitumen**

Think of this as the modern evolution of tar and gravel. It is factory-made asphalt modified with either APP or SBS polymers, which makes it more flexible and resistant to cracking in cold temperatures. SBS-modified bitumen in particular handles freeze-thaw cycling better than older BUR systems, which is relevant given Sioux Falls winters.

It is applied in sheets, either torch-applied, cold-adhesive, or self-adhering. Torch work requires a licensed and experienced hand. Done right, it lasts 15 to 25 years under typical national estimates. Ask your contractor which modification type and application method they are proposing and why.

**EPDM (rubber roofing)**

EPDM is a synthetic rubber membrane, typically black, that comes in large sheets. It is widely used on residential low-slope roofs because it is relatively affordable and handles temperature swings well. The vulnerable points are the seams and any penetrations (vents, drains, curbs). Most flat roof leaks in this system trace back to a failed seam or flashing detail, not a hole in the field of the membrane.

EPDM can last 20 to 30 years under normal national estimates, but that depends heavily on how well the seams were originally bonded and how often someone checks them.

**TPO (thermoplastic polyolefin)**

TPO is a white or light-colored membrane that reflects heat, which is one reason it became popular on commercial buildings. On residential low-slope roofs it is a solid option, and the heat-welded seams are generally considered stronger than the adhesive seams used with EPDM.

It is relatively newer than EPDM, so the long-term track record is shorter. Installation quality matters a great deal. A bad TPO install with poorly welded seams will fail faster than a good EPDM install. Ask any contractor quoting you on TPO how they handle the seams and what equipment they use to weld them.

## Why Flat Roof Repair in Sioux Falls Is Harder to Schedule

If you have called several roofers for a flat roof quote and gotten no response or a vague "we don't really do those," you are not alone. Homeowners across Sioux Falls describe exactly this experience, especially with smaller repairs on existing low-slope sections.

There are a few reasons. First, low-slope work requires different tools, materials, and training than shingle work. A crew that runs architectural shingles all week is not set up for a torch-applied modified bitumen repair. Second, the market here skews toward residential shingle replacement, so fewer contractors specialize in low-slope systems. Third, small repairs on flat roofs are genuinely harder to price because finding the actual source of a leak under a built-up roof takes diagnostic time.

The practical upshot: when you call, ask specifically whether the contractor has done low-slope work before and whether they can show you examples or references in Sioux Falls. A roofer who handles both steep and low-slope regularly is a different profile from one who is willing to give it a try.

Also check licensing. According to the City of Sioux Falls, any contractor doing roofing work on a home needs a Residential Roofing and Repair Contractor license or a broader Residential Building Contractor license, backed by a $20,000 compliance bond and at least $300,000 in general liability insurance. You can verify this by calling Building Services licensing at 605-367-8670. This requirement does not go away because your roof happens to be flat.

## Snow Load, Ponding, and the Sioux Falls Winter Problem

Sioux Falls homes with flat or low-slope roofs face a real structural question every winter: how much snow is too much?

Roof load capacity depends on how the structure was designed and built. Most residential roofs are engineered to handle the local snow load, but a flat roof accumulates snow faster than a steep one, and wet spring snow is far heavier than dry winter snow. If you have ever looked at your garage roof after a heavy March storm and felt uneasy, that instinct is worth acting on.

The fix is not always climbing up there yourself. Roof rakes designed for flat or low-slope sections exist, though they work best from the ground on shallower angles. For a heavily loaded roof, calling a professional is the right move, especially given that in January 2023, after a series of winter storms, roofing experts in Sioux Falls specifically advised homeowners to call a pro rather than attempt ice or heavy snow removal themselves, according to Dakota News Now.

Ponding after snowmelt or rain is a separate issue. If water sits on your low-slope roof for more than 48 hours, check whether the drains or scuppers are blocked first. A clogged interior drain is one of the most common and easiest-to-fix causes of ponding. If the drainage is clear and water still sits, the roof may not have been installed with adequate slope, and that is a conversation for a low-slope roofing contractor.

## Leaks on Low-Slope Roofs, Where to Look and What to Ask

A leak on a flat roof is often not where you think it is. Water that enters at a failed seam near a parapet wall may travel several feet horizontally along the roof deck before showing up as a stain on your ceiling below. This is the same principle that makes leak diagnosis on steep roofs tricky, only the horizontal distance can be much greater on a flat system.

Common sources to flag when talking to a contractor:

- Seams and laps in the membrane, especially near edges and penetrations
- Flashing around vents, curbs, HVAC equipment, or skylights
- The perimeter edge where the membrane meets the fascia or wall
- Interior drains where the drain flashing has separated from the membrane
- Blisters or bubbles in the field of a BUR or modified bitumen roof (trapped moisture that has caused the material to separate from the layer below)

If a roofer quotes you a flat roof repair without walking the whole roof and probing any suspect areas, ask why. A thorough diagnosis on a flat roof takes more time than it does on a steep shingle roof, and a contractor who is not spending that time may be quoting a repair that misses the actual source.

## Questions to Ask Before Hiring for Low-Slope Work

When you get a contractor on the phone or on-site, these questions separate someone who actually knows flat roofing from someone who is willing to take your money and figure it out:

- How many low-slope projects have you completed in Sioux Falls in the last two years, and can I contact a reference?
- Which system are you recommending and why, given my specific roof situation?
- How will you identify where the leak is actually entering before you start the repair?
- If you are doing torch-applied work, what are your fire safety protocols?
- Is the work you are proposing a repair or a full replacement, and what is the expected lifespan of each option?
- Will you pull the required permit from the City of Sioux Falls?
- What is your warranty on the workmanship, and is it in writing?
- If you are replacing the system, what happens if you find damaged decking once the old material is removed?

That last question matters because low-slope roofs trap moisture more readily than steep roofs, and rotted decking is a common finding on a tear-off. Get a clear answer about how additional decking repairs are handled and priced before the work starts, not after.
