A window well leak is one of the more frustrating basement water problems because it looks so small from outside but delivers a surprising amount of water below grade. Many Sioux Falls homeowners notice it first as water in one corner of the basement every spring, often assuming the foundation is to blame. Usually the well itself is the culprit, and fixing it correctly is a lot cheaper than a full waterproofing system. Before diving into window wells specifically, the [A Dry Basement in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/basement-waterproofing-guide-sioux-falls) gives you the full picture of how water finds its way into a basement and how the fixes connect to each other.

## Why Window Wells Flood in Sioux Falls

Window wells are set into the ground, which means they collect everything that runs toward them: rain, snowmelt, and water sheeting off the house.

The basic mechanics are simple. The well is a pocket. When water enters faster than it can escape, the pocket fills. Once it fills past the window frame, water presses against the glass and frame and finds its way inside.

A few specific conditions make Sioux Falls wells especially prone to this:

- **Clay-heavy soil** does not drain freely. Water sits in and around the well rather than percolating down.
- **Freeze-thaw cycles** shift the well's metal or plastic liner over time, opening gaps between the liner and the foundation wall.
- **Settled grading** near the foundation can redirect water from the yard straight into the well. The article on [grading and downspouts](/guides/grading-and-downspouts) covers this in more detail if that is what you are dealing with.
- **Downspout discharge** landing near a well is one of the most common causes homeowners miss.

None of these require a complicated fix on their own. The problem is that most wells are built with just enough gravel to feel like a drain system, and that gravel compacts or clogs over time.

## The Four Things That Go Wrong (and What to Check)

Walk outside and look at your window well before calling anyone. Most of the diagnosis is visible.

**1. No drain, or a clogged drain**

A properly installed well should have a drain at the bottom connected to a gravel bed or, in some homes, to the footing drain system. If yours has no drain at all, water has nowhere to go except up and in. If there is a drain, probe it with a screwdriver. Compacted dirt, leaves, and root debris clog well drains regularly.

**2. Insufficient gravel depth**

Gravel below the well acts as a reservoir that holds water while it slowly drains into the soil. A shallow gravel bed, or one filled with fine sediment, loses that capacity. When you are outside, you can often see whether the gravel has sunk or turned dark with organic material.

**3. Gaps between the liner and the foundation wall**

Run your hand along the edge where the metal or plastic liner meets the concrete. Any gap there is a direct water path. Freeze-thaw movement widens these gaps season by season if they are not sealed.

**4. No window well cover, or a cover that fits poorly**

This is the simplest fix and the one most homeowners skip. A good window well cover sheds rain and snowmelt before it ever enters the well. A loose, cracked, or absent cover turns every rain event into a flooding risk. Covers are sold at hardware stores to fit standard round and rectangular wells, and they are worth buying before anything else.

## What a Proper Fix Looks Like, Step by Step

If you have ruled out a quick cover fix, here is the sequence a thorough repair follows. You can do parts of this yourself; others benefit from a contractor.

**Start with the cover.** A properly fitting window well cover costs very little and eliminates a significant portion of the water entering the well. Make sure it overhangs the edges and sheds water away from the liner.

**Next, clean and restore the gravel bed.** Remove debris from the top of the gravel. If the gravel has compacted or silted over, dig it out and replace it with clean washed gravel, usually to a depth of 12 inches or more below the well's base. This restores drainage capacity.

**Check the drain if one exists.** Flush it with a hose. If water backs up, the drain is clogged or the pipe has separated. A contractor will need to dig to inspect the pipe connection to the footing drain.

**Seal the liner-to-wall gap.** A hydraulic cement or appropriate masonry sealant fills gaps along the foundation wall edge. This is a surface fix, not a structural one, but it stops direct infiltration at that joint.

**Check grading within a few feet of the well.** The ground should slope away from the well, not toward it. Even a modest low spot nearby can funnel water in during heavy rain.

If you work through all of these and still get water during major rain events, the problem may be hydrostatic pressure coming through the foundation wall itself, not the well. That is a different conversation and the [interior vs. exterior waterproofing](/guides/interior-vs-exterior-waterproofing) article explains what your options look like at that stage.

## Egress Wells and Why They Have Extra Risk

If your window well is an egress well, meaning it serves a bedroom window that is sized as a code-required emergency exit, the stakes are higher in two ways.

First, egress wells are bigger. A larger opening collects more water and needs a more robust drain system beneath it. A standard residential drain often undersizes these wells.

Second, the window itself is larger, which means more frame, more sealing surface, and more opportunity for water infiltration if the frame weathers or shifts.

If you are finishing or have finished a basement bedroom with an egress window, make sure the well drainage was part of the original installation scope. Many homeowners discover years later that it was not. The City of Sioux Falls Building Services issues permits for egress window installations, and work on a finished basement carries its own permit requirements. Checking with Building Services before starting any structural work in the well area is worth the call.

Egress wells also tend to need covers rated to support weight, since a person may need to stand in one during an emergency exit. Standard decorative covers are not built for that. Ask a supplier specifically about egress-rated covers.

## When to Call a Contractor and What to Ask

You can handle covers, gravel, and basic sealing yourself. Call a contractor when:

- The well drain connects to the footing drain system and you are not sure if that system is functional.
- Water is still entering after you have fixed the cover, gravel, and gaps.
- You see cracks in the foundation wall within or near the well opening.
- The liner has pulled significantly away from the wall and cannot be simply resealed.

When you do call someone, the City of Sioux Falls requires a Residential Building Contractor's License for work on residential homes within city limits, and the City does not accept licenses from other states. You can verify any contractor on the currently licensed contractors list at siouxfalls.gov/licensed-contractors before you sign anything.

Questions worth asking before work begins:

- What is causing the water to enter, and how did you determine that?
- Does the well drain connect to the footing drain, and will you inspect that connection?
- What specifically will you do to prevent recurrence?
- Is a permit required for this scope of work?

Sioux Falls homeowners who have had the best experiences with waterproofing contractors consistently mention one thing: the contractor explained the cause before proposing a fix. If someone quotes you a full interior drainage system for a window well problem, ask them to show you exactly why the well drain and cover are not the right starting point. That is a reasonable question and a good contractor will answer it directly. The [questions to ask a waterproofing contractor](/guides/choosing-waterproofing-contractor) article has a fuller list if you are heading into that conversation.
