You turn the thermostat up, the rest of the house feels fine, and that one room stays cold no matter what. It is one of the most common complaints from Sioux Falls homeowners, and the good news is that a cold room in a house almost always has a traceable cause. The bad news is there are several possible causes, and guessing at the wrong one wastes money.

This checklist walks you through how to narrow it down. For the full picture on insulation in Sioux Falls homes, start with [Insulating a Sioux Falls Home, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/insulation-guide-sioux-falls).

## Start by Ruling Out the Heating System

Before you pull up any flooring or poke around the attic, check whether the room is actually getting conditioned air.

Go to the room on a cold day when the heat is running. Hold your hand near every supply register and feel for airflow. Then check the return register (the larger grille that pulls air back to the furnace) if the room has one.

A few things to check:

- Is the register damper open all the way?
- Is there a register that was furniture-blocked or carpet-covered for years?
- Does the room have a return air grille at all, or just a supply?

Rooms without returns create pressure imbalances. The conditioned air gets pushed in but has no path back, so it leaks out through gaps instead of circulating. This alone can make a room feel noticeably colder than the rest of the house.

If the register has strong airflow and the room still stays cold, the problem is almost certainly the building envelope, and you keep going down the checklist.

## Rooms Colder Than Others: Check What Is Above and Below

The room's location in the house tells you where to look first.

**If the cold room is on the top floor or has attic space above it**, heat is escaping upward. Attic insulation depth matters, but air leaks matter more. Even a well-insulated attic floor loses most of its effectiveness if there are gaps around recessed lights, plumbing chases, or the top plates of interior walls. Sioux Falls homeowners have reported wet spots on upstairs bedroom ceilings in January and ice on attic framing, both signs that warm air is punching through the ceiling and hitting cold surfaces.

Start by going into the attic above that room on a cold morning. Look for frost or moisture on the framing directly above the cold room. That pattern points to exactly where air is escaping.

**If the cold room is above an attached garage**, that is its own situation worth reading about in detail at [Warming a Room Over the Garage](/guides/room-over-garage). The garage floor is essentially outdoor air. The room above needs an insulated, air-sealed floor assembly to perform, and the duct serving that room often runs through the unheated garage space, which strips heat from the air before it arrives.

**If the cold room is on the main floor or basement**, look at rim joists, crawl spaces, and the foundation walls near that room. Cold from the soil can transfer into the floor system directly under a cold room.

## Windows and Exterior Walls Are the Obvious Suspects (But Not Always the Problem)

It is easy to blame the windows. Sometimes they are the problem. But single-pane windows or old storm windows on one wall of a room usually create a cold draft near the glass, not a room that feels uniformly cold.

Stand near the window with the back of your hand facing the glass on a cold day. If you feel cold radiating off the glass from several inches away, the window is contributing. If the whole room feels equally cold regardless of where you stand, the window is probably not the main cause.

What to actually check on the exterior wall:

- Run your hand along the bottom of the wall where it meets the floor. Cold air infiltration often enters at this joint.
- Check electrical outlets on exterior walls. Remove the cover plate and hold a tissue near the box. Movement means air is coming through the wall cavity.
- In older Sioux Falls homes, exterior walls sometimes have little or no insulation at all. Dense-pack retrofit is an option worth knowing about through [Adding Insulation to Walls in an Older Home](/guides/wall-insulation-older-homes).

Gaps around window and door frames are also common. A thin bead of caulk that has cracked or separated lets in more air than homeowners expect.

## How to Read the Room Like an Inspector

You do not need a thermal camera to find the problem, though a camera makes it obvious. Here is what you can do without tools.

On a cold, calm day (little or no wind so you are not just chasing infiltration from pressure), walk the room slowly.

- Run your palm along every exterior wall, from floor to ceiling.
- Check corners, which lose heat from two sides at once.
- Feel around any penetrations: pipes, wires, recessed lights, ceiling fans.
- Crouch down and feel along the baseboard. Cold floors usually mean inadequate insulation or air leaks below.

Then go outside and look at the room from the street after a snowfall. If snow melts faster on the roof above one room than on the rest of the roof, heat is escaping through that section. That is a reliable field test that costs nothing.

If you find frost or moisture in the attic above this room, the article [Frost and Wet Spots in the Attic in January](/guides/frost-in-attic) covers exactly what causes it and what needs to be fixed first.

## When to Call Someone and What to Ask

Some of these fixes are DIY-friendly. Caulking around a window frame, opening a register damper, or adding weatherstripping to a door takes an afternoon.

Others are not. Air sealing inside the attic above the cold room, insulating a room-over-garage floor, or dense-packing a wall cavity requires a contractor who knows what they are doing.

When you call, ask these specific questions:

- Will you go into the attic and look at the area above this room specifically?
- Can you show me photos of what you find?
- Do you do air sealing as part of the job, or just add insulation on top of the existing gaps?
- Is the duct serving this room insulated, and can you check for leaks at the connections?

Sioux Falls homeowners consistently praise contractors who explain what they found in the attic and show photos before starting work. That is a reasonable expectation, and any contractor worth hiring should be able to do it.

If you want a whole-house view of what is happening, a home energy assessment with a blower door test will pressurize the house and reveal every leak at once. That can be a faster path to answers than chasing one room at a time.
