If you have a basement in Sioux Falls, your sump pump is probably the hardest-working piece of equipment you own and also the easiest to forget about until the moment it fails. Spring snowmelt and summer downpours can send water into your sump pit faster than a single pump can move it, especially if the power goes out during the same storm that's pushing water through your foundation. A failed sump pump is one of the most common complaints local homeowners share after a bad weather event. Before that happens to you, it's worth understanding how these systems work, how to test them, and what backup options give you a real safety net. If you haven't read [A Dry Basement in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/basement-waterproofing-guide-sioux-falls), that's the place to start for the full picture.

## How a Sump Pump Actually Works

A sump pump sits inside a pit (the crock) dug into the lowest point of your basement floor. Water flows into the pit through the surrounding gravel or through a perimeter drain system if you have one installed. When the water level rises high enough, a float switch triggers the pump, which pushes the water up through a discharge pipe and out of the house.

The key parts to understand:

- **The pit** collects water before it spreads across your floor.
- **The float switch** tells the pump when to run. If it sticks or gets tangled, the pump won't turn on.
- **The check valve** on the discharge line keeps water from flowing back into the pit once the pump shuts off.
- **The discharge line** carries water to an exterior outlet, usually a few feet from the foundation.

Most residential sump pumps are either pedestal style (motor sits above the water) or submersible (motor sits inside the pit). Submersible models are quieter and more common in newer Sioux Falls homes. The right size for your pit and water volume depends on your specific setup, so ask your contractor what GPH (gallons per hour) rating they recommend and why.

## How to Test Your Sump Pump Before Storm Season

Testing takes about five minutes and can save you a flooded basement.

1. Find your sump pit, usually covered by a plastic lid in a basement corner or utility area.
2. Lift the lid and check that the pump is upright and the float arm moves freely.
3. Slowly pour a five-gallon bucket of water into the pit.
4. The pump should kick on before the water gets close to the top of the pit, then shut off after the water drops.
5. Listen for unusual grinding or rattling, both are signs the impeller or motor may be wearing out.

Do this test every spring before the ground thaws and again before any forecast of heavy rain. If the pump runs but water rises anyway, the pump may be undersized for your pit volume or the discharge line may be blocked or frozen.

Also check that the discharge outlet outside is clear of ice, mud, or debris. A blocked outlet forces the pump to work against back pressure and can burn out the motor faster than you'd expect.

## Backup Systems, Why One Pump Is Not Enough

A single sump pump has two vulnerabilities that most homeowners don't think about until they're stacking towels at midnight.

The first is power failure. Sioux Falls storms that dump the most water are also the storms most likely to knock out electricity. A pump with no power is just a piece of plastic sitting in a rising pit.

The second is mechanical failure. Pumps run on motors, and motors wear out. If your primary pump fails mid-storm, nothing about your drainage system compensates for it automatically.

That's why a sump pump backup system is worth serious consideration for any Sioux Falls home with a finished basement or anything stored downstairs. The main options are:

- **Battery backup pump.** A second pump with its own sealed battery sits in the same pit and activates when the primary pump can't keep up or loses power. Battery capacity varies; ask how many hours of run time the battery provides at peak pumping load, not just standby time.
- **Water-powered backup pump.** Uses your home's water pressure to eject pit water through a venturi effect. No battery required, but it uses municipal water to do it and is not effective when municipal pressure drops during a widespread outage.
- **Whole-home generator.** Keeps your primary pump running through any outage. This is the most reliable option if you already need a generator for other reasons, but it's the highest upfront investment.

For most Sioux Falls homeowners, a battery backup pump paired with a good primary pump covers the realistic scenarios without the cost of a generator. Ask any contractor you speak with how the backup float is set relative to the primary float, since the backup should only trigger above the primary's shutoff level, not at the same point.

## Alarms and Monitoring

An alarm doesn't stop water, but it wakes you up at 2 a.m. before the damage spreads. A basic high-water alarm sits in the pit just above the normal shutoff level and sounds a loud alert if the water rises past that point.

More advanced options connect to your home Wi-Fi and send a phone alert even when you're away. These make a real difference for homeowners who travel or who have a vacation property in addition to their main home.

Look for a system that monitors:

- High water level in the pit
- Pump failure or no-run detection
- Power loss to the primary pump

You can also buy combination units that include an alarm, battery backup pump, and charger in one package. If you're already replacing a pump, it's worth pricing the combo versus adding pieces separately.

## Discharge Location, Where the Water Has to Go

Where your sump pump sends the water matters more than most homeowners realize. If the discharge outlet is too close to the foundation, the water soaks back in and your pump runs in circles. The standard guidance is to route water well away from the house and toward a spot where it can drain toward the street, an alley, or a low point in the yard, never toward a neighbor's property or a spot that pools.

A few things to confirm about your discharge setup:

- The outlet should terminate away from the foundation, not just past the sill.
- The line should slope downward from the pump to the outlet so water drains fully and doesn't sit in the pipe and freeze in winter.
- Discharge into a sanitary sewer is not permitted in the City of Sioux Falls. If a previous owner connected the pump to a floor drain that ties to the sewer, that's a code problem worth correcting.
- In winter, the outlet can ice over. Some homeowners use a freeze-resistant discharge fitting that allows some water to escape even when the end is partially blocked.

If your yard grading sends water back toward the house, the best pump in the world is working against the slope. Fixing the grade outside can reduce how hard your sump system has to work. ## When to Replace a Sump Pump

Sump pumps don't last forever. Most submersible pumps run reliably for somewhere in the range of 7 to 10 years under normal use, though that varies with how often the pump runs and whether it's ever run dry or handled debris-heavy water.

Signs it's time to replace rather than repair:

- The motor runs but the pump moves little or no water (worn impeller)
- The pump cycles on and off rapidly without the water level changing much
- You hear loud vibration or grinding during operation
- The pump is more than 10 years old and you've never replaced it
- It ran dry during a power failure (this can shorten motor life significantly)

If you're uncertain whether something you're seeing in the pit is a drainage problem or water coming from another source entirely, the article [Sewer Backup or Groundwater?](/guides/basement-backup-vs-seepage) can help you figure out what you're actually dealing with before you call anyone.

When you do get quotes on a replacement or a full backup system, make sure any contractor working in your Sioux Falls home holds a current Residential Building Contractor's License. The City of Sioux Falls does not accept out-of-state licenses, and you can verify who is currently licensed at siouxfalls.gov/licensed-contractors.
