If you have ever pressed your hand against the sill plate area in your basement in January and felt a rush of cold air, you have found one of the most overlooked energy problems in Sioux Falls homes. The rim joist, that band of framing that sits right on top of your foundation wall, is exposed to outdoor temperatures on three sides and is rarely insulated well, or at all, in older homes. It is a small area on a blueprint but it runs all the way around your house, and that adds up fast. For the full picture of where this fits in a whole-home strategy, see [Insulating a Sioux Falls Home, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/insulation-guide-sioux-falls).

## What a Rim Joist Actually Is and Why It Matters

The rim joist (sometimes called a band joist) is the outermost piece of framing that caps your floor joists where they rest on the foundation.

It sits right at grade level, or just above it, which means it faces outdoor air, wind-driven cold, and moisture from two directions at once.

In a typical Sioux Falls home built before the 1990s, that entire perimeter was left open or covered only with unfaced fiberglass batts that had slipped out of place over the years.

Here is why it causes so many problems:

- The wood is thin and has no thermal mass to buffer temperature swings.
- Any gap between the rim joist and the sill plate, or between the rim joist and the subfloor above, lets outside air pour straight into the floor system.
- Rooms directly above, especially on the north or west side of the house, feel the effect immediately as cold floors and drafty baseboards.

This is different from attic heat loss. Attic air sealing matters most for heat rising out of the house. Rim joist leaks matter most for cold air pouring in at floor level, which is exactly what makes first-floor rooms uncomfortable even when the thermostat is set high.

## Why Sioux Falls Homes Are Especially Vulnerable

Homes here deal with long stretches of extreme cold pressing against that thin band of framing for months at a time.

The Big Sioux Aquifer supplies Sioux Falls with notoriously hard water, and that mineral-heavy water contributes to condensation and efflorescence on foundation walls near the sill plate. Where moisture already collects, any insulation you add needs to handle that environment without trapping water against the wood.

Older neighborhoods like McKennan Park and All Saints have beautiful homes with deep character, but their basement framing reflects the building practices of their era. Rim joists were often left bare, or batts were stuffed in loosely without any air barrier, which does almost nothing against air movement.

The frost line in this region is deep, meaning foundations go down far, but the rim joist itself sits at the very top of that foundation and takes the full brunt of winter air before the soil insulation effect kicks in at all.

## The Two Materials That Actually Work Here

For rim joist insulation, two approaches dominate because they both air-seal and insulate in the same step.

**Rigid foam board (cut and cobble)**

- You cut pieces of polyisocyanurate or XPS foam to fit each joist bay precisely.
- Each piece gets pressed in and sealed around all four edges with canned spray foam.
- The foam fills the gaps, the board adds R-value, and the assembly stops air movement completely.
- This is the most common DIY approach for rim joist insulation because it requires no special equipment.

**Two-component spray foam (professional application)**

- A contractor sprays closed-cell foam directly into each joist bay, expanding to fill every crack and seam.
- Closed-cell foam adds meaningful R-value per inch and acts as its own vapor retarder, which matters given the moisture environment near a Sioux Falls foundation wall.
- This is faster on large perimeters and leaves no gaps, but it requires licensed applicators and proper ventilation during the work.

What does not work well here: loose fiberglass batts stuffed in without any air barrier. The batt may add some R-value on paper, but cold air moves right through it, carrying away heat. If you have bare or batt-only rim joists right now, that is a known weak point.

For a deeper look at how these material choices compare across the whole house, [Blown-In, Batts or Spray Foam?](/guides/blown-in-vs-spray-foam) covers each in context.

## Moisture, Mold, and What to Check Before You Insulate

The biggest mistake homeowners make with rim joist insulation is covering up a moisture problem instead of solving it.

Before any material goes in, look at the sill plate and the bottom of the rim joist for staining, soft wood, or any visible mold. Press on the wood with a screwdriver handle. It should feel solid.

Check the exterior grade around your foundation. If soil or mulch is piled against the siding and slopes toward the house, water is likely working its way toward that framing over time.

If you see white chalky deposits (efflorescence) on the inside of the foundation wall directly below the rim joist, that is a sign of water moving through the concrete. Insulating over that without fixing drainage first can trap moisture and cause rot.

Once you are confident the framing is dry and the exterior is draining away from the house, insulation can go in without worry. If anything looks soft, stained, or smells musty, get a contractor to assess it before you proceed.

## DIY vs. Hiring a Pro for Rim Joist Insulation

The cut-and-cobble rigid foam method is genuinely one of the more accessible DIY basement insulation projects for a handy homeowner.

The materials are available at local building supply stores. The work is done in a finished or unfinished basement without scaffolding or attic access. You can do one wall at a time and stop when you need to.

What you need to handle correctly:

- Measure each joist bay carefully because they vary, especially in older homes.
- Use canned spray foam generously around every edge so there are zero air gaps.
- Many fire codes require that exposed foam in a basement be covered with a thermal barrier, usually drywall. Check with City of Sioux Falls Building Services before leaving foam exposed.
- If your basement has any bulk water history, resolve that first.

Hiring a pro makes more sense when:

- The perimeter is large and you want it done in a single visit.
- You want closed-cell spray foam, which requires professional equipment and licensing.
- You are not sure whether the existing framing is in good shape.
- You are pairing the rim joist work with broader basement insulation or a whole-home energy assessment.

If you go the pro route, ask specifically whether they will air-seal the sill plate as well as the rim joist bays, and ask them to show you photos of the completed work before they pack up. ## The Payoff and the Next Step

Sealing and insulating rim joists is one of the faster wins in a home's thermal envelope because the problem is accessible and concentrated in one area.

Homeowners who address this along with attic air sealing often notice the difference in first-floor comfort within the first cold stretch after the work is done. Cold floors and drafty baseboards that felt permanent tend to ease noticeably.

The best starting point is to go into your basement on a cold day, feel along the rim joist perimeter for drafts, and look for any sign of moisture or staining. What you find in that twenty-minute walk will tell you a lot about what your house needs next.

If you are not sure how to prioritize the rim joist against other projects, a home energy assessment with a blower door test and thermal camera can show you exactly where air is moving and by how much. That kind of data makes decisions much easier. Learn what that process looks like in [What a Home Energy Assessment Shows](/guides/energy-assessment).
