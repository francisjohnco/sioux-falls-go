Overflowing gutters are one of the most common complaints from Sioux Falls homeowners, and they almost always happen during the worst possible moment: a heavy summer downpour when you can't do anything about it. The good news is that most gutter overflow has a fixable cause, and in many cases you can find it yourself before spending money on a service call. This checklist walks through every common reason, starting with the simplest and moving toward the ones that need a contractor. For background on how gutters work as a system, see [Gutters for a Sioux Falls Home, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/gutter-guide-sioux-falls).

## Start Here: Check for Clogs First

The single most common reason gutters overflow is a clog, and it doesn't have to be dramatic.

A handful of cottonwood fluff, a clump of shingle grit, or a few wet leaves packed into a downspout elbow can block enough flow to send water cascading over the front edge within minutes of a storm starting.

Before you assume anything structural is wrong, check these spots:

- The downspout opening where it meets the gutter at the bottom
- The first elbow below that opening (the most common jam point)
- Any section of gutter directly below a tree canopy
- The bottom of downspouts, especially if they discharge underground

If water pours freely through the downspout when you flush it with a garden hose, the downspout isn't the problem. If it trickles or backs up, you've found it.

A regular cleaning schedule prevents most of this. Sioux Falls has enough cottonwood, ash, and elm trees in older neighborhoods like McKennan Park and All Saints that gutters can clog between fall and spring without anyone noticing until a June rain. See [A Gutter Cleaning Schedule for Sioux Falls](/guides/gutter-cleaning-schedule) for timing that fits this climate.

## The Slope Problem (Water Sitting in the Gutter)

After a rain, go outside and look at your gutters. If you see standing water still sitting in them an hour later, the slope is off.

Gutters should pitch toward the downspout, typically around a quarter inch of drop for every ten feet of run. When hangers sag or pull away from the fascia, sections of the gutter go flat or even tilt backward, creating a low spot where water pools and then spills over the edge during the next storm.

You'll often see a waterline or rust streak inside the gutter right at the low spot.

The fix is usually resetting or replacing a few hangers, which is a straightforward repair. If the fascia board behind the hanger is soft or rotted, the hanger has nothing solid to grip and the problem will return. [Sagging or Pulling Away Gutters](/guides/sagging-gutters) covers how to tell whether it's a hanger issue or a fascia issue, and what each repair involves.

## Not Enough Downspouts, or Downspouts Too Small

A gutter is only as effective as what it drains into.

The industry standard for residential gutters is roughly one downspout for every 20 to 40 linear feet of gutter, but that number changes based on your roof's pitch and total drainage area. A steep roof sheds water faster than a low-pitch roof, so it needs more capacity.

If your home has long gutter runs with a single downspout at one end, the gutter is trying to move a lot of water a long distance before it has an exit. During a heavy storm, it fills faster than it can drain, and the overflow point is usually the far end, farthest from the downspout.

Standard residential downspouts are 2x3 inches. Upgrading to 3x4 inch downspouts moves significantly more water and is a common upgrade when overflow is chronic and no clog is found. Ask a contractor what your roof's drainage load actually requires rather than accepting the minimum.

## Undersized Gutters for Your Roof

Sioux Falls gets intense summer thunderstorms. A 5-inch gutter that would handle a moderate rain in a milder climate can be completely overwhelmed by a fast-moving storm cell dropping heavy rain in a short window.

Standard 5-inch K-style gutters handle most homes well. But if your roof has a steep pitch, a large surface area, or complex geometry with multiple planes all funneling to the same gutter section, a 6-inch gutter may be what the home actually needs.

You can't fix an undersized gutter by cleaning it. If your gutters overflow only during hard, fast rain and not during steady moderate rain, undersized gutters are worth discussing with a contractor. [Gutter Size and Why It Matters](/guides/gutter-sizing) explains how roof area and pitch affect what size gutter a home actually needs.

## Roof Valleys Are a Hidden Source of Overflow

A roof valley is where two roof slopes meet and direct water downward in a concentrated stream.

If a valley dumps directly into a short section of gutter, or if that section has only one downspout nearby, the volume of water hitting that spot during a storm is far higher than the rest of the gutter system handles.

Watch your roofline during a heavy rain if you can do so safely from a window or covered porch. You'll often see water shooting past the gutter entirely at a valley, or see that section overflow while the rest of the gutter is fine.

Solutions include adding a downspout in that section, adding a diverter inside the gutter at the valley point, or in some cases upsizing just that run of gutter. This is a situation where a site visit from a licensed contractor is worth it because the fix depends on your specific roof geometry.

## What to Try Before Calling Anyone

If you're seeing gutters overflowing and want to work through it yourself first, here's the order to follow:

1. Clean the gutters and flush the downspouts with a hose
2. Check the downspout elbows for packed debris
3. After the next rain, look for standing water in the gutters (slope problem)
4. Watch which section overflows, and whether it lines up with a roof valley or a long run with one downspout
5. Measure your downspout size (pull out a tape measure, it takes 30 seconds)

If cleaning fixes it, great. If overflow still happens after the gutters are clear, you're likely looking at a slope issue, a sizing issue, or a drainage capacity problem, all of which a contractor can assess and quote.

## When to Call a Contractor

You don't need a permit to replace or repair gutters, according to the City of Sioux Falls, which lists gutter and fascia work among aesthetic projects that don't require a permit as of April 2025.

You do want someone who is properly licensed. The City requires a Residential Building Contractor's License for all contractors working on residential homes within city limits, and it does not accept out-of-state licenses. The City publishes a Currently Licensed Contractors list at siouxfalls.gov/licensed-contractors, which is worth checking before you hire.

Call a contractor when:

- Cleaning doesn't stop the overflow
- You see soft fascia or wood rot behind the gutters
- The overflow is concentrated at a roof valley and you're not sure what the fix is
- You want an honest assessment of whether your gutters are sized correctly for your home

A good contractor will walk the roofline with you, explain what they're seeing, and give you a written quote that separates materials from labor. Ask specifically about downspout count and size, slope adjustments, and whether any fascia needs replacing before new gutters go up.
