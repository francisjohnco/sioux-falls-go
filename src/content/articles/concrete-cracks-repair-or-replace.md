Concrete in Sioux Falls takes a beating that most of the country never sees. The freeze-thaw cycle here runs dozens of swings a year, the clay soil underneath shifts with every wet spring, and hard water from the Big Sioux Aquifer leaves mineral deposits that work into surface pores over time. The result is that most driveways, sidewalks, and patios here eventually crack, and the real question is whether a crack is cosmetic or a warning sign. For the full picture on concrete in Sioux Falls, start with the [Concrete Driveways and Patios in Sioux Falls, the Complete Guide](/guides/concrete-guide-sioux-falls).

## Reading the Crack: Hairline vs. Structural

Not every crack means your concrete is failing. The first thing to figure out is what kind of crack you are dealing with.

**Hairline cracks** are surface-level, usually less than 1/8 inch wide, and run in irregular patterns across the slab. They are common in Sioux Falls concrete that cured too fast in summer heat or went through a hard first winter. They look alarming but often do not affect the slab's strength.

**Structural cracks** are wider, run all the way through the slab, or show vertical displacement, meaning one side of the crack is higher than the other. That height difference is the red flag. It tells you the two pieces are moving independently, which is a trip hazard and a sign that the base underneath has failed.

Watch for these signs that a crack is more than cosmetic:

- One side of the crack is raised more than a quarter inch above the other
- The crack is wider than a half inch
- Water pools in or near the crack after rain
- You can see daylight through the crack from below (in a raised slab or step)
- Multiple cracks are converging or running parallel across the same slab

If you see any of those, do not just fill the crack and move on.

## Why Sioux Falls Concrete Cracks the Way It Does

Understanding the cause helps you choose the right fix. Random surface cracking with no displacement is usually a curing or surface issue. Cracking at control joints (the straight lines cut into the slab) is normal and expected. That is exactly what those joints are designed to do.

The more serious patterns here are almost always caused by what is happening under the slab. Sioux Falls sits on expansive clay soil. When that clay gets wet, it swells. When it dries out, it shrinks. Over years, that movement creates voids under the concrete, and the slab sinks or tilts into those voids.

**Heaving** is the opposite problem. When water gets under a slab and freezes, it expands and pushes the concrete up. With a frost line at 42 inches in Sioux Falls, that freeze can go deep and affect even well-installed concrete. Heaving often looks like one panel pushed up relative to its neighbor.

**Settling** is when the slab sinks, usually because a void formed underneath or the base material washed away. Poor drainage is a common cause here, especially in neighborhoods where downspouts or yard grading push water toward the slab rather than away from it. ## Mudjacking and Foam Lifting: When Repair Makes Sense

If the slab itself is in decent shape but it has sunk or tilted, lifting it may be an option before you consider full replacement.

**Mudjacking** (also called slab jacking) pumps a slurry of water, soil, and cement under the slab through drilled holes to fill voids and raise the concrete back to level. It is a well-established process that many contractors in the region use. The holes are patched afterward, and the slab can usually be used within a day.

**Polyurethane foam lifting** (sometimes called foam jacking) uses an expanding foam injected through smaller holes. The foam cures quickly and is lighter than mudjacking material, which can matter on weak subsoils. Both methods are worth asking about when you get quotes.

Neither method is a permanent cure if the underlying drainage or soil problem is not also addressed. Ask the contractor directly: "What caused this to sink, and will that cause it to sink again?"

Lifting makes the most sense when:

- The slab is structurally sound (no major cracking or spalling)
- The displacement is relatively uniform across the panel
- The concrete is not too old or too thin to handle the pressure
- The surrounding slabs are in good condition

If the slab is badly cracked, spalled, or more than 20 to 25 years old, lifting a deteriorated slab usually just delays an inevitable replacement.

## Concrete Crack Repair: What It Actually Fixes

Surface crack repair, the kind you can do yourself or hire out for a small job, works best on cracks that are stable and hairline-width. Products range from polyurethane caulk to epoxy injection to resurfacing overlays.

The honest limitation: concrete crack repair seals the crack from water intrusion, which is genuinely valuable in a Sioux Falls winter. Water in a crack freezes, expands, and widens the crack fast. Sealing it early slows that process down.

What repair does not do is restore structural integrity to a slab that has lost it, bond two panels that are still moving relative to each other, or fix a base problem. If the two edges of a crack are still shifting seasonally, any filler will fail within a year or two.

Ask a contractor to probe the slab and look for hollow spots before recommending repair. A quick tap with a hammer reveals a lot. Solid concrete sounds dense. A void underneath sounds hollow.

## When Replacing Is the Right Call

Replacement costs more upfront, but it is the only real answer when:

- The slab has widespread spalling (the surface is flaking away in chunks)
- Multiple panels are heaved, settled, or cracked through
- The base has completely failed and needs to be rebuilt
- You are dealing with a sidewalk panel that is a trip hazard (the [Sidewalk Repair and Replacement guide](/guides/sidewalk-repair-sioux-falls) has more on how that works in Sioux Falls)
- The concrete is old enough that its reinforcement, if any, has corroded

When you replace, you get the chance to fix the root cause: improve drainage, build a proper base, and pour concrete at the right thickness. Skipping that work and just pouring new concrete on a bad base is a way to have the same conversation again in five years.

If you are replacing only part of a driveway or matching existing concrete, ask the contractor how they plan to match the finish and how visible the seam will typically be. Homeowners in Sioux Falls have noted after road construction work that partial replacements can be more noticeable depending on how well the contractor sets forms and matches the finish.

A written bid that spells out base prep, thickness, reinforcement, and what happens if they find a bigger problem underneath is worth asking for before any work starts.
