Hair clogs are the most common drain problem in any bathroom, and they are also one of the few you can almost always fix yourself without spending a dime on a service call. The catch is knowing which tools are safe, which shortcuts make things worse, and when what looks like a hair clog is actually something else entirely. If you want the full picture on drain problems in Sioux Falls, start with [The Sioux Falls Homeowner's Guide to Drain Cleaning](/guides/drain-cleaning-sioux-falls) and come back here for the step-by-step work.

One thing worth knowing locally: Sioux Falls water is hard, running anywhere from roughly 12 to 15 grains per gallon depending on the season. That mineral load leaves a sticky film inside pipes that hair and soap scum bond to faster than they would in a soft-water city. A clog that seems like pure hair almost always has a mineral-and-soap layer underneath it, and that matters for how you treat it.

## Tools You Actually Need (and What to Skip)

You do not need much, and buying the wrong thing can cause real damage. Here is what works:

- **Needle-nose pliers or a hair clog removal tool** (also sold as a "Zip-It" or drain snake hair remover). These thin plastic strips with barbs pull hair out mechanically and cost two to four dollars at hardware stores.
- **A standard cup plunger** for sinks. Use one with a flat bottom, not the flange style made for toilets.
- **A hand-crank drum snake** (also called a drain auger), 15 to 25 feet, for clogs that sit deeper in the drain arm.
- **Rubber gloves.** Non-negotiable. You will be touching decomposing hair and soap sludge.
- **A flashlight or your phone's flashlight.** You need to see what you are dealing with.

What to skip:

- **Liquid chemical drain cleaners** (Drano, Liquid-Plumr, and similar products). They can soften hair clogs temporarily, but they also attack older pipe fittings and gaskets, and they leave a caustic residue that can splash back at you on the next attempt. The article on [chemical drain cleaners vs. a plumbing snake](/guides/drain-cleaner-chemicals-vs-plumbing-snake) covers this in more detail if you want the full comparison.
- **Boiling water poured directly into a porcelain sink.** Hot tap water is fine. Boiling water can crack older porcelain or loosen PVC glue joints.
- **Wire coat hangers.** They scratch chrome finishes and can puncture older plastic drain arms.

## Step-by-Step: Bathroom Sink Slow Drain Fix

Work in this order. Stop when the clog clears rather than doing all of it unnecessarily.

**Step 1: Remove the pop-up stopper.**
Most bathroom sinks have a pop-up stopper that lifts and lowers with a small rod behind the faucet. Lift it all the way up, then wiggle it sideways and pull straight up. Many come out without tools. The stopper itself is often loaded with hair and is the entire source of a bathroom sink slow drain fix.

**Step 2: Clean the stopper.**
Pull off the hair, rinse it under hot water, and set it aside. Run the sink. If water drains freely now, reinstall the stopper and you are done.

**Step 3: Use the hair removal tool.**
If the drain is still slow, insert the barbed plastic strip into the drain opening and push it down slowly while rotating it slightly. Pull it back out steadily. Expect to retrieve a dense, unpleasant clump. Repeat two or three times, rotating to different angles.

**Step 4: Flush with hot tap water.**
Run the hottest water your tap produces for two to three minutes. This helps dissolve the soap-and-mineral film the hair was stuck to.

**Step 5: Use a plunger if the drain is still slow.**
Block the overflow hole (the small oval opening near the top of the sink basin) with a wet rag to create suction. Add an inch or two of water to the basin, then plunge with short, firm strokes for 20 to 30 seconds. Check the drain.

**Step 6: Try a hand-crank snake.**
If steps one through five have not worked, the clog is sitting further along the drain arm, past the P-trap. Feed the snake cable slowly into the drain until you feel resistance. Crank it clockwise, then pull back. Work patiently rather than forcing it.

## Step-by-Step: Shower Drain Clogged with Hair

Shower drains collect hair fast because the drain cover sits flat at floor level, and there is no stopper to catch debris before it enters the pipe.

**Step 1: Remove the drain cover.**
Most shower drain covers either lift straight out, unscrew with a Phillips screwdriver, or unscrew by hand. Remove it and set it aside.

**Step 2: Look before you reach.**
Shine a flashlight into the drain. In many cases the hair clog is visible within the first few inches, sitting just below the cover or on the drain basket.

**Step 3: Pull the hair out mechanically.**
Use the barbed plastic hair tool or needle-nose pliers. Push the tool down, rotate it, and pull up slowly. The clog often comes out in one large mass. This is the most effective single step for a shower drain clogged with hair.

**Step 4: Flush and re-check.**
Run the shower for a minute and watch the drain. Water should disappear immediately. If it pools slightly and drains slowly, repeat step 3 at a different angle.

**Step 5: Snake if needed.**
If water still pools, the clog is deeper. A hand-crank snake fed through the drain opening will usually reach it within the first few feet of pipe.

**Step 6: Clean and reinstall the cover.**
Scrub the drain cover with an old toothbrush before replacing it. Mineral buildup on the cover itself can slow drainage even with a clear pipe.

## Signs the Problem Is Deeper Than Hair

Hair clogs behave in a specific way: they affect one fixture, they respond to mechanical removal, and they do not come with odors beyond the smell of wet hair and soap. If your situation does not match that description, pay attention.

Call a plumber if you notice:

- **Multiple fixtures draining slowly at the same time.** A bathroom sink and a shower both running slow, or a tub backing up when you flush the toilet, points to the main line, not a hair clog.
- **Gurgling sounds from other drains** when water goes down one fixture. That is a pressure and venting problem deeper in the system.
- **Water backing up into the tub or floor drain** when the sink runs. This is a main-line symptom and needs professional attention quickly. The [emergency drain backup guide](/guides/what-to-do-when-your-drain-backs-up-sioux-falls) walks through what to do in that first hour.
- **A sulfur or sewage odor** coming up from the drain even when the fixture is not in use.
- **A clog that returns within days** after you cleared it. Recurring clogs usually mean buildup on the pipe walls, a partial obstruction further down the line, or a venting issue. The article on [why drains keep clogging](/guides/why-do-my-drains-keep-clogging) covers the most common causes.

In older Sioux Falls neighborhoods like McKennan Park and All Saints, homes with original cast-iron or galvanized drain lines often have rough interior surfaces that grab hair and mineral scale far more aggressively than modern PVC pipe. If your home is pre-1970 and clogs keep returning, the pipe condition itself may be the underlying issue.

## How to Prevent Hair Clogs Going Forward

Clearing the clog is the short-term fix. Preventing the next one takes about 30 seconds a week.

- **Install a mesh drain cover** over your shower drain. The flat, stainless-steel style costs under five dollars and catches nearly all hair before it enters the pipe.
- **Pull hair from the drain cover after every shower.** Takes five seconds. Makes a noticeable difference over a month.
- **Clean the pop-up stopper on your bathroom sink once a month.** Most hair buildup happens right there.
- **Flush drains with hot tap water for a minute or two once a week.** This is especially useful in Sioux Falls given the hard water. Mineral film is what hair bonds to, and regular hot-water flushing slows that film from building up.
- **Skip the monthly "drain treatment" products.** Most are diluted versions of the chemical cleaners mentioned above. Mechanical removal and regular maintenance outperform them without the pipe wear.
