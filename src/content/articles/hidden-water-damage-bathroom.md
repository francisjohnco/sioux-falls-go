A bathroom remodel rarely starts with the words "we found rot." It usually starts with a tile that keeps cracking, or grout that never seems to stay clean, or a floor that has a slight give right in front of the shower. By the time a Sioux Falls remodeler pulls the first tile, that soft spot has sometimes become a full subfloor replacement and a conversation about how water got all the way to the basement.

If you are planning a full remodel, the [Remodeling a Bathroom in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/bathroom-remodel-guide-sioux-falls) covers the whole process from start to finish. This article focuses on one specific part of that process: what tends to be hiding behind and beneath old tile, why it happens here, and how to handle it without losing your mind or your budget.

## The Warning Signs Before You Start Demo

The tricky thing about water damage under shower tile is that it hides well for years. The surface can look mostly fine while the structure underneath is quietly failing.

Watch for these before you sign anything:

- A floor that feels soft or slightly springy near the toilet or shower base
- Tiles that rock or hollow-sound when you tap them
- Grout that cracks repeatedly even after repairs
- A persistent musty smell that does not go away after cleaning
- Stains on the ceiling of the room directly below the bathroom
- Peeling paint or bubbling drywall near the base of the tub or shower surround

Any one of these is worth mentioning to your remodeler before demo begins. More than one of them together usually means water has been moving somewhere it should not.

A Sioux Falls homeowner shared this experience in a public review: a shower repair started as cracked floor grout and turned into leaks and rot in several places. What looked like a surface fix became a much larger project once the tile came off. That story is not unusual.

## Why Water Gets Behind Tile in the First Place

Tile and grout are not waterproof on their own. The waterproofing in a shower lives behind the tile, in the membrane or board underneath. When that layer is missing, inadequate, or installed incorrectly, water migrates through grout lines with every shower.

Over time, that moisture reaches the substrate (the board or mortar bed the tile sits on) and eventually the subfloor below.

A few things make this worse in Sioux Falls specifically. The City of Sioux Falls 2020 finished water quality report found total hardness of around 15 grains per gallon for water treated at the City's own plant, and around 10 grains per gallon for Lewis and Clark water. Hard water leaves mineral deposits in grout and at tile joints. Those deposits expand slightly as they dry, which works grout loose over time and opens pathways for water.

Freeze-thaw cycles do not directly affect interior tile, but they matter for homes with older foundations and crawlspaces where temperature swings can affect moisture levels in the structure.

Bathroom subfloor rot is almost always the result of slow, long-term water intrusion rather than a single event. By the time it is visible or soft underfoot, it has usually been happening for years.

## What Remodelers Actually Find

When tile comes off a shower surround or floor in an older Sioux Falls home, here is what turns up most often:

- Drywall used as a substrate instead of cement board or a waterproof backer (common in bathrooms built before the 1990s)
- No waterproofing membrane at all, or one that was applied only partially
- Soft or crumbling substrate that falls apart when touched
- Blackened or spongy subfloor, sometimes extending well past the shower footprint
- Mold growth on the back of the tile or on framing
- Corroded or leaking drain connections underneath the shower pan

In some cases, water has traveled further than the bathroom itself. A new shower leaking into the basement is a real pattern in Sioux Falls reviews, usually when a drain connection was not fully sealed or the pan was not properly sloped during a previous installation.

## How to Handle Change Orders for Hidden Damage

This is where homeowners feel most blindsided, and it is worth understanding before demo day.

A reputable remodeler cannot quote you for damage they have not seen yet. What they can do is tell you upfront how they handle it when they find something unexpected. Ask this question before you sign the contract.

Good answers sound like:

- "We will stop work, show you what we found, give you a written change order with the scope and cost before we continue, and you decide how to proceed."
- "We carry a contingency estimate for discovery items and here is how we calculate it."

Red flags sound like verbal estimates on the spot, pressure to approve quickly, or a vague "we'll figure it out as we go."

The [What Drives the Cost of a Bathroom Remodel in Sioux Falls](/guides/bathroom-remodel-cost-sioux-falls) article goes deeper on how to build a contingency into your budget before you start, which is the most practical thing you can do to protect yourself here. Getting a written change order for every scope addition, no matter how small, is not bureaucratic. It is the only record you have if the project ends in a dispute.

## Waterproofing Done Right the Second Time

If your remodeler finds water damage, the fix is not just replacing the rotted material. The reason the water got there has to be corrected, or the same thing happens again in ten years.

Modern shower waterproofing options include:

- Sheet membranes bonded to cement board or foam board
- Liquid-applied membranes that are rolled or painted on and cure to form a continuous barrier
- Foam shower systems that are waterproof by design and eliminate the substrate question entirely

The waterproofing layer needs to run up the walls at least to the height of the tile and wrap into the niche or shelf openings. The curb and the floor-to-wall transition are the most common places where waterproofing is cut short.

Ask your remodeler what system they use and why. Ask where it terminates and how the drain is integrated into the membrane. These are not fussy questions. They are the ones that determine whether you are having the same conversation again in a decade.

## Testing Before the Walls Close Back Up

After waterproofing is applied and before tile goes in, there is a window to verify the work. Some installers do a flood test: they plug the drain, fill the shower floor to a set depth, and check for any drop in water level over several hours.

Not every contractor does this as a standard step, but it is worth asking about.

After the remodel is complete, run every fixture for several minutes and then check:

- The ceiling or floor of the room directly below the bathroom
- The area around the drain from underneath if accessible
- The base of the walls inside the shower for any seeping at grout lines

Catching a problem the week after completion, while the contractor is still engaged, is much easier than finding it six months later.
