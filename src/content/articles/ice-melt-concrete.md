Picking up a bag of ice melt at the hardware store feels simple until you realize your driveway is flaking and pitting after a few winters. In Sioux Falls, the freeze-thaw cycles that run through late winter and early spring put enormous stress on concrete, and the wrong product makes that stress much worse. This guide breaks down how the common ice melt types behave on concrete, what to do if your slab is new, and how to protect the things you care about -- like your dog and your landscaping. For the full picture of handling snow and ice at your home, start with [Snow Removal in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/snow-removal-guide-sioux-falls).

## The Common Ice Melt Types and What They Do to Concrete

Not all ice melt products work the same way, and not all of them are equal when it comes to concrete damage. The active ingredient is what matters.

**Sodium chloride (rock salt)** is the cheapest and most widely sold option. It works well above about 20 degrees Fahrenheit but becomes much less effective in colder temperatures. It is also the harshest on concrete because it pulls moisture into the slab and accelerates the freeze-thaw cycle that breaks down the surface over time.

**Calcium chloride** melts ice faster and works at lower temperatures than rock salt. It generates heat as it dissolves, which is useful in a hard freeze. It is less damaging to concrete than sodium chloride, but it is not harmless, especially on older or already-stressed slabs.

**Magnesium chloride** is gentler than both sodium and calcium chloride and is widely marketed as a safer option for concrete and vegetation. It still contains chloride, though, so it is not damage-free. It works reasonably well in moderate cold and is a good middle-ground choice for most homeowners.

**Potassium chloride** is often marketed as pet-friendly. It is less corrosive than sodium chloride and less likely to burn plant roots, but it becomes much less effective once temperatures drop significantly, which limits its usefulness in a Sioux Falls winter.

**Calcium magnesium acetate (CMA)** and **urea-based products** are the gentlest on concrete and vegetation. They are also the most expensive and work more slowly. CMA in particular is often recommended for new concrete or decorative surfaces.

A quick reference for scanning:

- Rock salt: cheapest, most damaging to concrete and plants
- Calcium chloride: fast-acting, lower temps, moderate concrete impact
- Magnesium chloride: gentler on concrete and vegetation, good all-around
- Potassium chloride: low corrosion, limited effectiveness in hard freezes
- CMA or urea: gentlest option, higher cost, slower melt

## New Concrete Needs Special Treatment

If your driveway or sidewalk slab was poured in the last year or two, pay close attention here. New concrete is especially vulnerable to deicers because the curing process is not fully complete and the surface is more porous.

Most concrete professionals recommend keeping all deicing chemicals off a new slab for at least the first full winter. That includes products labeled "safe" or "gentle." The pores in a new slab absorb liquid more readily, and any freeze-thaw action inside the concrete causes surface scaling and spalling faster than it would on a mature, sealed slab.

For new concrete, your best options are:

- Sand or fine gravel for traction (adds grip without chemical action)
- Cat litter as a traction aid in a pinch
- A good quality concrete sealer applied before winter, once the slab has cured

If you are unsure whether your slab is cured enough to handle a sealer or a deicer, that is a good question to ask the contractor who poured it. For more on what to expect from a new slab and when it is ready for winter, the [Concrete Work in Sioux Falls](/guides/concrete-guide-sioux-falls) guide walks through the basics.

## Protecting Pets and Plants from Ice Melt

Sioux Falls homeowners with dogs know the routine: the dog comes in after a walk and starts licking its paws. Many ice melt products irritate paw pads and are toxic if ingested in meaningful quantities.

Sodium chloride and calcium chloride are the most irritating to pets. Magnesium chloride is generally considered less harmful. Urea-based products are sometimes marketed as pet-safe, though "safer" is a better word than "safe."

The practical steps most veterinarians suggest:

- Wipe your dog's paws when they come inside, every time
- Avoid letting dogs eat snow from treated surfaces
- If your dog shows signs of irritation (excessive licking, limping), rinse the paws with warm water
- Look for products labeled "pet-safer" with magnesium chloride or CMA as the active ingredient, and check the label for concentration

For plants and lawn edges, chloride-based products wash into the soil and can damage roots, especially on foundation plantings close to the driveway or sidewalk. Grass along driveway edges is particularly vulnerable.

To reduce plant damage:

- Apply ice melt sparingly and only where you need traction
- Rinse treated areas with water during a mild spell to flush chlorides away from roots
- Avoid piling treated snow against shrubs or garden beds (more on where snow should go in [Where to Put All That Snow](/guides/where-to-put-snow))

## How to Use Ice Melt Without Overdoing It

One of the most common mistakes Sioux Falls homeowners make is using too much product. More is not better with ice melt. Overapplication increases damage to concrete, harms plants and pets, and costs more money.

A few habits that make a real difference:

- **Shovel or snow-blow first.** Remove as much snow as you can before applying any product. Ice melt works on thin layers of ice, not deep snow. This also means you use far less product.
- **Apply before a storm, not after.** A light pre-treatment makes ice removal much easier because it prevents bonding between ice and the surface.
- **Use the smallest effective amount.** A light, even scatter across the surface is enough. If you can see individual granules touching each other without overlapping heavily, that is about right.
- **Sweep up leftover granules.** Once the ice has melted, leftover product sitting on a dry surface continues to work on the concrete. Sweep it away or rinse it off.

If your sidewalk still needs to be clear because of the City of Sioux Falls 48-hour rule, and ice melt alone is not doing the job fast enough, combine it with physical removal. The rule requires sidewalks cleared edge to edge within 48 hours after snow stops falling. As reported by Dakota News Now and KSOO in 2025, an uncleared sidewalk can bring a $100 penalty and the City can hire a removal company at the owner's cost. More detail on the rule and how it is enforced is in [The 48-Hour Sidewalk Rule, Explained](/guides/sidewalk-snow-48-hours).

## Protecting Your Concrete Long-Term in Sioux Falls

Ice melt choices are only part of the picture. Concrete in Sioux Falls is also dealing with expansive clay soil underneath it and hard water from the Big Sioux Aquifer area, both of which can stress slabs over years.

A quality penetrating concrete sealer applied in the fall is one of the best things you can do to reduce deicer damage. It slows moisture absorption, which is what makes freeze-thaw cycles so destructive. Sealers typically need reapplication every few years, depending on traffic and exposure.

Watch for these signs that your concrete is already under stress:

- Surface flaking or spalling (thin chips peeling off the top layer)
- Fine cracks that widen after a cold snap
- Pitting or a rough, pockmarked texture that was not there before

If you are seeing any of those, it is worth talking to a concrete contractor before next winter rather than after. Catching surface damage early is far cheaper than replacing a slab.
