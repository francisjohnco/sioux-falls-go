Water is the quiet enemy of every concrete driveway, sidewalk, and patio in Sioux Falls. Get the grading right and the slab can last decades. Get it wrong and no amount of thick concrete or good mix design will save you from heaving, cracking, and erosion eating away at your yard.

Before you hire anyone or sign a bid, the [Concrete Driveways and Patios in Sioux Falls, the Complete Guide](/guides/concrete-guide-sioux-falls) is worth reading front to back. This article goes deeper on one piece of that puzzle: how water moves across and under your concrete, and what you can do to make sure it moves away.

## Why Sioux Falls Soil Makes Drainage a Bigger Deal

Most of Sioux Falls sits on expansive clay soil. Clay does not drain freely. When it gets wet it swells, and when it dries it shrinks.

That movement is relentless. Over years, it pushes slabs up in some spots and lets them settle in others. When you combine that with the freeze and thaw cycles this city puts slabs through every winter, the stress adds up fast.

The frost line in Sioux Falls goes down 42 inches. Any water that sits against or under a slab and then freezes can expand with enough force to crack reinforced concrete. That is not an exaggeration.

The practical takeaway: drainage is not just an aesthetic question about whether puddles form on your driveway. It directly determines how long your concrete lasts before it cracks.

## The Slope Numbers That Actually Matter

Concrete needs to shed water, not hold it. Contractors and engineers use the term "positive drainage," which simply means the surface tilts away from wherever water cannot go.

For a driveway or patio next to your house, the minimum slope is typically 1/8 inch of drop per foot of run, away from the foundation. Many contractors prefer 1/4 inch per foot on flat sites with heavy clay soil.

That 1/4-inch-per-foot standard might sound small, but across a 20-foot driveway it means a 5-inch total drop. If your lot is relatively flat, that takes careful planning before the forms go in, not after.

Ask your contractor in writing what slope they are building in and in which direction. If the answer is vague, that is a problem.

Here is what to look for in a written bid:

- Stated cross-slope or pitch (such as 1/4 in. per foot)
- Direction water will travel (away from house, toward street or yard drain)
- How the contractor will handle the transition at the garage floor or house foundation
- Whether any regrading of the surrounding yard is included

## Downspouts, Erosion, and Where Water Actually Goes

One of the most common grading mistakes homeowners see after a new driveway or patio is poured is erosion showing up at the edges. Soil washes away, the slab edge is left hanging, and within a couple of seasons the edge starts to crack.

The source is often a downspout. A downspout discharging directly onto new concrete or soil near the slab edge can dump hundreds of gallons of water in a single heavy storm. That water has to go somewhere, and if grading does not direct it away clearly, it goes under the slab.

Before your project starts, walk the perimeter of the work area during or right after a rain. Watch where water actually travels. Note any downspout discharge points.

Good contractors factor this into the grade plan. You can also:

- Extend downspouts at least 4 to 6 feet past the slab edge using an extension or underground pipe
- Add a splash block or pop-up emitter so water releases away from the foundation and slab
- Ask whether the grade plan accounts for any existing drainage patterns on your lot

If erosion has already undercut a slab edge, that is worth reading about in [Cracked Concrete, Repair or Replace?](/guides/concrete-cracks-repair-or-replace) before deciding how to proceed.

## Base Prep Is Where Drainage Starts

The slope you see on the surface is only part of the story. What is under the slab matters just as much.

A well-prepared base, typically 4 to 6 inches of compacted Class 5 gravel or crushed aggregate, allows water that gets under the slab to drain laterally rather than pond and freeze. On a site with heavy clay, some contractors add a slight crown or slope to the base itself, so even subsurface water moves toward the edges.

If the base is thin, poorly compacted, or laid flat on dense clay with no path for water to escape, freeze and thaw cycles will work against you all winter. Water gets in, freezes, lifts the slab, thaws, and the slab settles unevenly.

Questions to ask before the pour:

- What base material will you use, and how deep?
- How will you compact it? (A plate compactor is standard; hand tamping on a large pour is a red flag.)
- Is there any slope or crown built into the base, given our soil conditions?
- Will you use a vapor barrier or geotextile fabric under the base?

For more on what happens on pour day itself, [What to Expect When Concrete Is Poured](/guides/new-concrete-pour-what-to-expect) walks through each step in plain language.

## Before Any Digging Starts, Call 811

If your drainage plan involves grading the yard, digging a trench for a downspout pipe, or any excavation beyond the slab footprint, you must call 811 first. That is South Dakota One Call, as required by the City of Sioux Falls, and it gets underground utilities marked at no cost.

This step is easy to skip on a project that feels mostly surface-level. Do not skip it. Underground electrical, gas, and irrigation lines are closer to the surface than most people expect.

Once utilities are marked, your contractor can grade around them safely. If a drainage solution requires redirecting water under a sidewalk or into a dry well, knowing where every line runs prevents a much more expensive problem.

## What to Watch for After the Job Is Done

A well-graded project should be obvious the first time it rains. Watch the water.

- It should sheet off the surface evenly, not pool in the middle or against the house
- Downspouts should discharge past the slab edge, not onto it
- Soil alongside the slab should be graded slightly lower than the slab edge, so water runs off rather than onto the concrete
- After a heavy rain, check whether any soil is washing from under or alongside the slab

If you see water pooling against the house or running back toward the foundation, raise that with the contractor before the job is considered complete. Fixing grade is far easier before the forms come down and the site is cleaned up than six months later.

Homeowners in older Sioux Falls neighborhoods like McKennan Park or All Saints sometimes discover that a replaced driveway or new patio has changed the drainage pattern for the whole yard. If your lot is small or the grade is subtle, it is worth asking the contractor to walk you through the drainage plan before work begins, not after.
