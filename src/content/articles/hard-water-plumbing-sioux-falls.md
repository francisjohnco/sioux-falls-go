Sioux Falls water is genuinely hard. According to the City of Sioux Falls 2020 finished water quality report, homes served by the City's own treatment plant see total hardness around 15 grains per gallon, while homes receiving Lewis and Clark water run closer to 10 grains per gallon. Which source feeds your home can vary by neighborhood and even by street. That hardness is not a health concern, but it is a plumbing concern, and understanding it can save you from repairs that sneak up quietly over years.

For a broader look at how Sioux Falls homes age and what to watch for, start with [Plumbing in a Sioux Falls Home, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/plumbing-guide-sioux-falls).

## What Hard Water Actually Does Inside Your Pipes and Fixtures

Hard water carries dissolved calcium and magnesium. When it heats up or sits still, those minerals drop out of solution and stick to whatever surface they touch.

That buildup is called scale, and it accumulates everywhere water flows or heats.

Over time, scale:

- Narrows the inside diameter of supply lines and valves
- Forms a crust inside water heater tanks and on heating elements
- Clogs aerators and showerheads until flow drops noticeably
- Leaves white or tan deposits on faucet handles, drains, and tile grout

The narrowing happens slowly, so homeowners often don't notice until a fixture barely trickles or a valve won't fully open. By then, the buildup has usually been years in the making.

Scale inside a water heater is especially costly. The mineral layer acts as insulation between the burner and the water, so the heater works harder and longer to reach temperature. That drives up energy use and stresses the tank. Sediment that settles at the bottom also creates the popping or rumbling sound many Sioux Falls homeowners hear and mistake for a failing heater. Sometimes it is a failing heater, but sometimes it is a tank that simply needs flushing. If your heater is making noise or running longer than it used to, [Water Heater Repair or Replace? How to Decide](/guides/water-heater-repair-or-replace) walks through how to tell the difference before you spend money on a full replacement.

## Sizing and Installing a Water Softener in Sioux Falls

A water softener is the most direct answer to hardness. It works by exchanging calcium and magnesium ions for sodium ions through a resin bed. The water leaving the softener is chemically soft and will not build scale on fixtures or inside equipment.

Sizing a softener correctly matters more than most homeowners realize.

An undersized unit regenerates too often, wastes salt and water, and can shorten resin life. An oversized unit regenerates too infrequently, and the resin bed can foul between cycles.

To size a softener, a plumber needs to know:

- Your household's daily water use (roughly the number of people times 75 gallons as a starting point)
- Your actual hardness level (the City report gives ranges, but a simple test strip or water test gives your specific number)
- Any iron content in your water, which affects resin capacity

Ask the plumber to explain the grain capacity they are recommending and why that calculation fits your home. A good explanation, before any work starts, is exactly what Sioux Falls homeowners say they value most when they find a plumber they trust.

Installation requires connecting to your main supply line, usually in the basement near the water meter. Any plumbing work inside city limits requires a licensed contractor, and the City of Sioux Falls does not accept out-of-state licenses. You can verify a contractor's current status at siouxfalls.gov/licensed-contractors before anyone touches your pipes.

## What a Softener Does Not Fix

A water softener solves hardness. It does not solve everything, and it is worth knowing the limits before you invest.

A softener will not:

- Remove iron above certain levels without an additional filter stage
- Improve taste or remove chlorine (a carbon filter handles that separately)
- Reverse scale that has already built up inside pipes or a water heater tank
- Protect against leaks, corrosion from other causes, or drain problems

If your pipes already have years of scale buildup, softening the water going forward is still worthwhile, but it will not dissolve what is already there. Ask your plumber whether flushing the water heater or descaling affected fixtures makes sense at the same time as softener installation.

Softened water also adds a small amount of sodium. Most people on sodium-restricted diets are advised to use a separate unsoftened line for drinking water, run to the kitchen sink or refrigerator. That is a simple plumbing addition worth discussing when you plan the installation.

## Maintaining a Softener and Watching for Hidden Problems

A water softener is not a set-and-forget appliance. It needs attention to keep doing its job.

Basic maintenance tasks include:

- Checking and refilling salt regularly (frequency depends on water use and unit size)
- Inspecting the brine tank for salt bridges, a crust that forms above the water level and blocks regeneration
- Having the resin bed checked periodically, especially if you notice returning hardness symptoms
- Testing your water once a year to confirm the softener is performing

One thing Sioux Falls homeowners sometimes discover is that a softener has been running for years on an incorrect setting, regenerating too often or not often enough. If your salt use suddenly spikes or your fixtures start showing scale again, that is worth a service call before assuming the unit has failed.

Hard water and softener bypass can also quietly contribute to hidden leak conditions when scale cracks or valve seats wear unevenly. If you notice an unexplained rise in your water bill or a musty smell near supply lines, those are worth investigating. [Hidden Plumbing Leaks and How They Get Found](/guides/hidden-plumbing-leaks) covers the warning signs and how plumbers trace them when nothing obvious is visible.

## Questions to Ask a Plumber Before You Commit

Whether you are adding a softener for the first time or replacing an old one, a few direct questions will help you avoid surprises.

Before work starts, ask:

- What hardness level are you sizing this unit for, and did you test my water or use the City report?
- What grain capacity are you recommending, and how did you calculate it?
- Will this installation require a permit, and will you pull it?
- What maintenance does this unit need, and how often?
- What does this softener not address that I should still be watching for?

A contractor who answers these questions clearly and specifically, without rushing you, is the kind of plumber worth calling back. One who cannot explain the sizing or skips the permit conversation is a signal to get another quote.

The City's list of currently licensed plumbing contractors is at siouxfalls.gov/licensed-contractors. Cross-checking a name there takes about two minutes and removes a lot of risk from an otherwise invisible process.
