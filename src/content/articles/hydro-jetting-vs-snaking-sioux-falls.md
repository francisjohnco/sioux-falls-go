Snaking and hydro jetting both clear drains, but they do different jobs. A snake pushes through a clog. A jetter scrubs the pipe clean. The right choice depends on what is blocking your line, how old the pipe is, and whether you want a quick fix or a longer lasting one. Here is how each works, what they typically cost, and which one tends to fit Sioux Falls homes. This article is part of our [complete guide to drain cleaning in Sioux Falls](/guides/drain-cleaning-sioux-falls).

## How Snaking Works and When It Is the Right Call

A drain snake, also called an auger or cable machine, is a coiled cable with a cutting head that a plumber feeds into the pipe. It spins, breaks up or hooks the clog, and opens a path. It is fast, it costs less, and it is the right tool for hair, paper, and many soft blockages in a single fixture or a short line. For a sink, tub, or toilet, snaking is usually the first thing to try.

National cost guides put snaking a main line at roughly $150 to $500 on the lower end, with higher figures for long runs. The limit of a snake is that it opens a channel without cleaning the pipe wall. Grease and scale left behind can rebuild a clog within a few months, which is why a drain that was "cleared" sometimes slows down again.

## How Hydro Jetting Works and Why It Costs More

Hydro jetting uses a hose with a special nozzle that sprays water at very high pressure to scour the inside of the pipe. It cuts through grease, flushes out debris, and can clear roots and scale that a cable only pokes at. Because it cleans the walls, results often last longer. It also takes more equipment and time, and it usually needs a cleanout for access, so it costs more. National guides commonly put a main line jetting job at about $350 to $1,000, with higher totals for long lines, a camera inspection, or a house with several problem lines.

Jetting is not right for every pipe. High pressure can make a cracked, collapsed, or badly deteriorated line worse, which is why a camera look first is strongly recommended on older lines.

## Which One Fits Sioux Falls Homes

Neither is better in every case. Match the tool to the cause.

- **Hair or paper in one fixture.** A snake is the right call.
- **A kitchen line that keeps clogging from grease.** Jetting, often after a camera look, because grease coats the walls and a snake only opens a channel.
- **Tree roots in an older line.** A cutter on a cable can clear them, and jetting can clean more of the pipe. Roots grow back either way, so ask about a maintenance interval.
- **Hard water scale.** Water from the Big Sioux Aquifer is hard, and scale builds up over the years. Jetting scours it more thoroughly than a snake does.
- **A line that clogs in the same spot again and again.** Get a camera first. Ground that shifts with freeze and thaw can leave a low spot where debris collects, and no amount of cleaning fixes a pipe that sags.

## Questions to Ask Before You Agree to Jetting

Because jetting costs more, confirm a few things before the work starts. Ask whether a camera will run first, so the company knows the pipe can take the pressure. Ask where the water will be introduced, since a cleanout is cleaner than pulling a toilet. Ask whether the quote includes a camera look afterward and a copy of the video. And ask what happens if the company finds a damaged section, so a cleaning job does not quietly turn into a repair estimate you first hear about at the end.

## Where a Camera Inspection Fits

A camera inspection runs a small waterproof camera through the line and shows what is inside. Many companies charge about $150 to $500 for a standalone inspection, and thorough recorded reports can cost more. It is worth paying for when a clog repeats, when the house is older, when you are buying a home, or when someone recommends jetting.

Ask for the recording. A video you can show a second company is worth more than a verbal diagnosis.
