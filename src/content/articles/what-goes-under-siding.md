Most of what protects a Sioux Falls home from moisture and cold is never visible once the job is finished. It lives between the wall sheathing and the siding itself, and if any part of it is skipped or done wrong, you won't know until water shows up inside a wall or your heating bill climbs for no obvious reason. Before you start any project, it's worth reading [New Siding in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/siding-guide-sioux-falls) for the full picture. This article goes deeper on one specific question: what exactly goes on the wall before the first panel, and why each layer earns its place.

## House Wrap: The Layer That Does the Most Work

House wrap is a synthetic sheet material that covers the wall sheathing from top to bottom before siding goes on. Its job is to block wind-driven rain and moisture from getting into the wall assembly while still letting water vapor escape from inside the house outward.

Think of it as a one-way membrane. Water in liquid form is stopped. Vapor from cooking, showers, and breathing can pass through, so moisture that gets into the wall can dry to the outside rather than rot the framing.

What makes installation matter as much as the material:

- Laps run shingle-style, top over bottom, so water can't run behind a seam
- Every seam is taped with compatible tape, not just overlapped
- The wrap is fastened tightly enough that it won't billow or tear before siding goes on
- Cuts around outlets, hose bibs, and vents are sealed, not left open

A crew that staples house wrap loosely and skips the tape is not doing a complete job. Ask your contractor specifically how they handle seams and penetrations. That is a reasonable question and a good contractor will answer it without hesitation.

## Flashing at Windows, Doors, and Other Openings

Flashing is thin material, usually metal or a self-adhering membrane, installed at every point where the wall is interrupted: windows, doors, hose bibs, electrical boxes, and anywhere else something pokes through or meets the wall at an angle.

Water finds openings. When it does, flashing redirects it outward before it can reach the sheathing or framing. Without it, every window and door is a slow leak waiting to happen.

Proper window flashing in a re-siding job typically involves:

- Flexible flashing tape applied to the rough opening before the window is reinstalled or the new casing goes on
- A sill pan or sloped piece at the bottom so any water that gets past the window frame drains out
- Integration with the house wrap so the two systems work together, not independently

This is an area where hail jobs get rushed. After a big storm, when siding, windows, and sometimes gutters are all being replaced at once, it is easy for a crew to focus on speed. Ask your contractor to walk you through how they handle window and door flashing specifically, before work starts.

Flashing is also required at the top of any horizontal trim band, at roof-to-wall intersections, and wherever two wall surfaces meet at a change in direction. If your contract doesn't mention flashing as a line item or a described step, ask why.

## Insulation Board: Optional Layer, Real Benefit

Rigid foam insulation board sits between the house wrap and the siding itself in some installations. It is not required, but it adds continuous insulation across the wall face and eliminates the thermal bridging that happens through wood studs.

Studs conduct cold. Standard batt insulation in the stud cavities does a good job between studs but not at the studs themselves. A layer of rigid foam on the outside of the sheathing covers the whole wall surface, studs included.

If you're curious whether this would actually improve comfort in your home, [Does New Siding Make Your Home Warmer?](/guides/siding-and-energy) covers exactly that question, including where insulation matters and where it doesn't.

A few things to know if you're considering insulation board:

- It adds thickness to the wall, which means window and door trim has to be extended to match
- Thicker walls require longer fasteners for siding, which affects both labor and materials cost
- Not every siding type is installed the same way over foam, so ask your contractor if they have experience with it
- It is a legitimate upgrade, not a sales add-on, but get a written quote that separates the cost so you can evaluate it on its own

## What Happens When a Layer Gets Skipped

This is where the stakes become clear. Each layer handles a specific failure mode. Remove one and you shift that risk directly to the structure.

No house wrap means wind-driven rain during storms can soak the sheathing directly. Over time, that leads to rot, mold, and eventually structural damage that costs far more to fix than the wrap would have cost to install.

Poor or missing flashing at windows is one of the most common sources of hidden water damage in homes. The leak often shows up years later, long after the crew is gone, as staining on interior walls or soft spots in the framing around a window.

Skipped or torn vapor management can trap moisture inside the wall rather than letting it dry. In a climate that swings between humid summers and cold dry winters, walls need to breathe in both directions seasonally.

The underlying problem in most of these cases is not that the materials are expensive or hard to install. It is that the layers are hidden once siding goes on, and a homeowner has no way to verify they were done correctly after the fact. That's why it matters to ask before the job starts, and to have the conversation be part of the written contract.

## What to Ask Before Work Begins

Re-siding is a building permit project in Sioux Falls. According to the City of Sioux Falls, re-siding requires a permit, and all contractors working on residential homes within city limits must hold a City-issued Residential Building Contractor's License or the Residential Roofing and Repair Contractor license created in 2024. The City does not accept licenses from other states. You can confirm any contractor is currently licensed at siouxfalls.gov/licensed-contractors.

The permit process also means an inspector can verify the work at certain stages. Ask your contractor when inspections happen and whether the wrap and flashing will be visible at inspection time. That is a normal, reasonable question.

Before any crew starts, here are the specific questions worth asking:

- What brand and type of house wrap do you use, and how do you handle seams?
- How do you flash windows and doors, and can you show me on a previous job or walk me through it?
- Will insulation board be used, and if so, how does that affect trim and fasteners?
- How is the wall prepared if old wrap is damaged or missing underneath the existing siding?
- Is all of this covered in the written contract, or described in the scope of work?

For a full look at how to read and compare what contractors actually put in writing, [How to Compare Siding Quotes](/guides/comparing-siding-quotes) breaks down what a thorough scope of work should include and where bids often differ in ways that aren't obvious at first glance.

The layers under your siding are not glamorous, but they are the difference between a job that holds up for decades and one that creates problems you won't find until they're expensive. Asking about them is not being difficult. It's being a prepared homeowner.
