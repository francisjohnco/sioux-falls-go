Choosing the right custom home builder is probably the single biggest decision in the entire building process, more consequential than the lot you pick or the finishes you agonize over, because a great builder can recover from a bad tile selection but no amount of nice tile covers up poor framing or a builder who disappears after closing. The Sioux Falls market in spring 2026 had 604 single family homes and townhomes under construction, according to SiouxFalls.Business, down from 821 a year earlier, which means builders have more capacity to take on new clients than they did during the peak, but it also means some shops are under more financial pressure. That context matters when you are evaluating who to trust with a project that could easily run several hundred thousand dollars. The full process of building, from design through permits and inspections, is covered in [How to Build a Custom Home in Sioux Falls (2026 Guide)](/guides/build-custom-home-sioux-falls). This article focuses entirely on how to vet and select the builder before you sign anything.

## Start With the License Check, Not the Showroom

The first thing to do with any builder you are seriously considering is verify their license with the City of Sioux Falls, before you tour a model home, before you sit down for a sales meeting. The city licenses residential, electrical, plumbing, and mechanical contractors, and you can check the city's Currently Licensed Contractors list online or call Building Services at 605-367-8672, according to the City of Sioux Falls Building Permit FAQs. The permit data site, which updates every Sunday, also lets you look up permits a builder has pulled in the past, which tells you how active they are and whether their jobs have been inspected and closed out properly.

What you are looking for is a residential contractor license in good standing, with a consistent history of permits being opened and finaled, not a string of permits with no final inspection on record. A builder who regularly leaves permits open may be cutting corners on inspections, or may be operating in a way that could create title and insurance complications for you later. If the builder tells you they work primarily outside city limits, in Harrisburg, Brandon, Tea, or Crooks for example, their permits would go through those municipalities or through Minnehaha County or Lincoln County rather than through Sioux Falls Building Services, so you would need to verify there. Do not skip this step because a builder comes highly recommended by a friend. License status is a floor, not a ceiling, but falling through the floor is expensive.

## Tour Finished Homes and Homes Under Construction

A model home is a marketing tool. It is typically a builder's best work, finished with upgraded selections, professionally staged, and photographed to look its most favorable. What you actually need to see is a home that was completed two or three years ago and a home that is currently framed and in the rough-in stage.

The finished home visit, ideally with the owners present or at minimum with their permission to look closely, tells you how the work holds up over time. Look at caulk joints around windows and doors, grading and drainage at the foundation, any visible settling or cracking in flatwork, and how the builder handled transitions between materials. Ask the homeowner directly whether the builder was responsive to warranty calls in the first year. In Sioux Falls, the freeze-thaw cycles and expansive clay soils mean drainage and foundation work either holds up or it does not, and you can tell a lot by walking around the perimeter of a two-year-old house.

The in-progress visit is even more revealing. When a home is framed but not yet drywalled, you can see the quality of the framing, how corners and headers are built, whether blocking is in place, how the rough-in trades did their work, and whether the job site is organized or chaotic. A builder who is comfortable letting you walk a job site with an experienced set of eyes is usually confident in what they are building. One who steers you away from in-progress homes and toward finished models only is worth questioning.

## Use the Parade of Homes as a Research Tool, Not Entertainment

The Sioux Empire Parade of Homes runs twice a year, in spring and fall, with homes open from 1 to 5 PM on parade weekends, according to Dakota News Now. Details and schedules are at siouxempireparadeofhomes.com, and the Home Builders Association of the Sioux Empire is involved in organizing it. For a buyer who is early in the process of figuring out which builders operate at the quality level and price point they are targeting, the parade is genuinely useful, but only if you go in with a plan.

Before the weekend, write down the two or three things that matter most to you in a home: a specific ceiling treatment, the quality of cabinet joinery, how mechanicals are run, mudroom function, whatever it is. Then look at every home through that specific lens rather than getting swept up in the staging and the stone countertops. Talk to the builder representative on site and notice how they answer questions about subcontractors, timelines, and allowances. The ones who give vague or defensive answers to basic questions in a sales setting are not going to be more forthcoming once you have signed a contract. For a deeper walkthrough strategy, [How to Use the Parade of Homes to Pick a Builder](/guides/parade-of-homes-sioux-falls-tips) covers what to look at beyond the finishes and how to compare builders after the fact.

## Call References From Owners Who Are Two to Five Years Out

Builder references from recent closings are almost always going to be positive. The builder controls who you call, and homeowners who just moved in are still in the honeymoon phase with their new house. The references that actually tell you something are from people who have lived in the home for two to five years, long enough to have experienced a South Dakota winter or five, to have called in warranty items, and to have seen how the builder responded when something went wrong.

Ask the builder specifically for references from homes completed two or more years ago and see how they respond. A confident builder with a solid track record will not hesitate. If you get pushback, that is worth noting. When you do speak with past clients, ask these questions directly: Did the final cost come in close to the original estimate? Were there surprises in allowances that pushed the budget up? How did the builder communicate during construction, and did that change after closing? Would you use them again, and if not, why not? Those last two questions tend to surface things that do not come up otherwise.

## Read the Contract Before You Fall in Love With the Plans

By the time most buyers sit down to sign a building contract, they have already mentally moved into the house. That emotional investment makes it easy to skim contract language you should be reading carefully. The two things that trip up custom home buyers most consistently are allowances and change order policies.

Allowances are the per-item dollar amounts a builder sets aside in the contract for things you have not yet selected, things like cabinets, light fixtures, flooring, plumbing fixtures, and appliances. If the allowance for kitchen cabinets is set at a number that only covers builder-grade boxes, but you want painted inset cabinetry, every dollar over that allowance comes out of your pocket on top of the base contract price. Some builders set allowances deliberately low to make their headline price look competitive. Ask the builder to show you what their standard allowance for each category actually buys at a local supplier, not in theory but in practice.

Change orders are the other lever. Understand exactly what triggers a change order, how they are priced (markup on materials, labor, or both), and when they must be signed before work proceeds. Contracts that allow the builder to proceed on verbal authorization for changes and bill you later are a source of serious disputes. For a full breakdown of how fixed-price and cost-plus contracts work and what to have an attorney review, see [Fixed Price or Cost Plus, Home Builder Contracts Explained](/guides/fixed-price-vs-cost-plus-home-contract).

## Communication Style Is a Preview of the Whole Experience

A custom home build in Sioux Falls will typically take somewhere between nine and eighteen months from permits to move-in, depending on complexity, weather, and how quickly you make selections. That is a long time to be in a working relationship with someone, and the communication habits a builder has during the sales process are almost always the same habits they will have during construction.

Notice whether they return calls and emails within a reasonable window, whether they give you direct answers or vague ones, and whether they proactively share information or wait for you to ask. Ask them specifically how they communicate with clients during the build: Is there a weekly update? A project management platform where you can see inspection results and upcoming decisions? Or is it informal and phone-based? Neither approach is automatically wrong, but you want it to match how you operate. A buyer who wants detailed written updates every week should not sign with a builder whose style is a friendly phone call when something important happens.

One practical step: toward the end of your vetting process, send each finalist builder a detailed email with four or five specific questions about their process. The speed, completeness, and tone of the response will tell you more than a polished sales presentation ever will.

## Red Flags to Take Seriously

A few patterns should stop a conversation or at least dramatically slow it down. A builder who cannot or will not provide their license number for verification, or who suggests you do not need to check, is a red flag. A contract that requires a large non-refundable deposit before plans are finalized or permits are pulled should prompt questions and possibly an attorney review. A builder who discourages you from getting an independent inspection at any phase of construction is worth being cautious about. Unusually low bids sometimes reflect lower-quality subcontractors, missing scope items, or a plan to recover margin through change orders later.

Going into a final builder meeting with those questions in hand, written down, will give you a much cleaner basis for comparison than relying on your impression of the showroom.
