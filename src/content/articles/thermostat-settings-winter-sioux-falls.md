Getting your thermostat settings right matters more in Sioux Falls than in most cities. When January temperatures drop to single digits or below, the difference between a good setting and a careless one can mean frozen pipes, a furnace running nonstop, or a heating bill that hurts. This article focuses entirely on thermostat strategy, so for the bigger picture of how your whole heating system fits together, start with [Heating and Cooling in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/hvac-guide-sioux-falls).

## Comfort vs. Savings: Finding Your Winter Setting

The question every Sioux Falls homeowner asks is some version of: how low can I set the thermostat without being miserable or causing a problem?

There is no single right answer, but here is a reasonable framework most households land on:

- **When home and awake:** 68 to 70 degrees works for most people. Some households with young children or older adults prefer 70 to 72.
- **When sleeping:** 65 to 68 degrees. Many people sleep better slightly cooler, and your furnace works less overnight.
- **When away during the day:** 62 to 65 degrees. This is where the real savings come from.

The key word is "setback," meaning you intentionally lower the temperature during predictable stretches when no one needs full comfort.

A bigger setback saves more energy, but in a Sioux Falls winter there is a floor below which you should not go, and that floor is not about comfort. It is about your pipes.

## The Pipe Safety Floor: Never Go Below This in a Sioux Falls Winter

Sioux Falls homes sit on expansive clay soil with a 42-inch frost line. That means exterior walls and uninsulated spaces can get genuinely cold even when your furnace is running.

If your thermostat setback goes too low, areas near exterior walls, in crawl spaces, or along poorly insulated garage walls can drop to freezing. Pipes in those areas are at real risk.

**The rule most plumbers and building professionals repeat: never set your thermostat below 55 degrees in an occupied home, and 60 degrees is safer if you have any pipes near exterior walls or in unheated spaces.**

If you have ever had a pipe freeze in a cold snap, go even more conservative. The [What to Do When Your Furnace Quits in a Sioux Falls Cold Snap](/guides/frozen-pipes-and-furnace-failure-cold-snap) article covers exactly what to do if your heat goes out, but the best move is prevention through a sensible setback floor.

One other thing: a Sioux Falls winter does not give you a warning day before a cold snap. Temperatures can fall 30 degrees in a few hours. Your thermostat setting at 9 p.m. on a mild January evening may be tested by minus-10 by morning.

## Smart Thermostats: What They Actually Do Well

A smart thermostat is not magic, but it does one thing that manual programmable thermostats rarely get done: it actually runs on a consistent schedule because you set it once from your phone and mostly leave it alone.

Most Sioux Falls homeowners who upgrade to a smart thermostat say the same thing: they like being able to check and adjust the temperature remotely, especially before coming home in winter.

Here is what a smart thermostat does well:

- Learns your schedule and adjusts automatically (on models with that feature)
- Lets you raise the temperature remotely before you arrive home
- Sends alerts if the temperature drops unexpectedly (useful for catching a furnace problem early)
- Shows you how many hours your system ran, which helps spot efficiency problems

Here is what it does not fix:

- A furnace that is too small or too large for your home
- Leaky ducts or uninsulated spaces
- A system that needs maintenance

If your home has cold rooms or uneven heat, a smarter thermostat will not solve it. See [Cold Rooms and Uneven Heat, Causes and Fixes](/guides/uneven-heating-cold-rooms) for what actually will.

**One installation note:** a smart thermostat needs a "C-wire" (common wire) for consistent power. Many older Sioux Falls homes, especially those with furnaces installed before the mid-2000s, do not have one run to the thermostat location. An HVAC technician can add it, or some smart thermostats include an adapter. Ask before you buy.

## Vacation Settings and Keeping Your Home Safe

Leaving for the holidays or a winter trip is when thermostat settings become a safety decision, not just a comfort or savings decision.

**Never set your thermostat below 55 degrees when leaving for an extended period in winter.** Most professionals recommend 60 degrees as the safer target for a Sioux Falls winter absence, especially in homes with plumbing near exterior walls, in basements with little insulation, or with an attached but unheated garage.

A few other steps that work alongside your thermostat setting:

- Open cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls so warm air can reach the pipes
- Know where your main water shutoff is in case a pipe does burst
- Ask a neighbor or friend to check the house every couple of days
- If you have a smart thermostat, set up a low-temperature alert so you get a text if the heat fails

A furnace that quits while you are in Phoenix for two weeks is not just an inconvenience. With Sioux Falls temperatures, you can have frozen and burst pipes within 24 hours.

## Heat Pump Thermostats: Different Rules Apply

If your home has a heat pump, either a standalone or a dual-fuel setup paired with a gas furnace, your thermostat strategy is a little different.

The most important thing to know: **do not use large setbacks with a heat pump.**

Here is why. When a heat pump thermostat calls for a big temperature recovery (say, jumping from 60 back to 70 after an overnight setback), many systems will trigger the backup electric resistance heat to catch up quickly. That electric resistance heat is expensive to run. You can end up spending more than if you had just held a steady 68 degrees all night.

The general guidance for heat pump homes:

- Keep setbacks small, 2 to 3 degrees rather than 8 to 10
- Use "smart recovery" or "intelligent recovery" mode if your thermostat has it, so the system ramps up gradually
- Check that your thermostat is actually programmed for a heat pump (some standard thermostats are not, and running the wrong thermostat can short-cycle the system)

If you are not sure whether your home has a heat pump or a traditional furnace, or whether your current thermostat is compatible, an HVAC technician can check it in a few minutes.
