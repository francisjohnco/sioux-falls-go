Water finds basements. It seeps through cracks, pools at the cove joint, backs up through window wells, or wicks through poured concrete walls that look perfectly solid. In Sioux Falls, the combination of heavy clay soil, freeze-thaw cycles, and snowmelt gives that process a head start every spring.

This guide walks through each piece of the puzzle, from where water enters to how to safely finish a basement once it's dry. Each section links to a deeper article when you're ready to go further. If you're staring at a wet corner right now, start at the top and work your way down.

## Where Water Actually Comes From in a Sioux Falls Basement

Before spending anything, you need to know which of these three sources you're dealing with:

- **Surface water** running toward the house from poor grading, clogged gutters, or low window wells
- **Lateral groundwater** pushing through the wall or the cove joint (where the wall meets the floor) under soil pressure
- **Condensation** forming on cool walls when warm, humid air enters the space

Most homeowners who report water in one corner every spring are dealing with surface water first. That's the cheapest problem to fix, and fixing it sometimes eliminates the problem entirely.

Lateral groundwater and condensation require different answers. A contractor who diagnoses the cause before proposing a solution is worth your time. One who leads with a full interior drain tile system before ruling out grading is worth a second opinion.

For a full breakdown of what's happening in spring specifically, read [Why Your Basement Gets Wet in Spring](/guides/wet-basement-spring).

## Start Outside: Grading, Gutters, and Downspouts

The most cost-effective basement waterproofing in Sioux Falls happens above ground.

The ground around your foundation should slope away from the house. If it's flat or tilting toward the wall, water pools at the foundation during rain and snowmelt and slowly works its way in.

Gutters matter as much as grading. A single downspout that empties two feet from the foundation can deliver hundreds of gallons per storm directly against your wall. Extensions that carry water at least six feet out and away are a simple fix that many homeowners skip.

Check these things after a heavy rain:

- Does water pool anywhere within four feet of the foundation?
- Are downspout extensions intact and aimed away from the house?
- Is mulch or soil banked against the siding, trapping moisture?

These fixes cost little compared to any interior system and sometimes solve the problem entirely. For a step-by-step checklist, see [Grading and Downspouts, the Cheapest Basement Fix](/guides/grading-and-downspouts).

If water has already entered and caused damage to floors or contents, that's a separate problem that needs prompt attention. The [Water Damage in Your Sioux Falls Home, What to Do](/guides/water-damage-guide-sioux-falls) guide walks through the immediate steps.

## Foundation Cracks: Which Ones Actually Need Attention

Not every crack is a crisis, but some are.

Hairline cracks in poured concrete walls are common and often just shrinkage from curing. A crack that is wider at the top than the bottom, or that has changed width since you first noticed it, is telling you something different. Stair-step cracks in block walls, horizontal cracks anywhere, or any crack that is actively leaking under pressure all deserve a professional look.

Sioux Falls homes built on clay soil are especially prone to differential settlement and lateral pressure as soil expands and contracts with moisture. That pressure can widen a crack over years without any visible sign at the surface.

Things to track on any crack you're watching:

- Mark the ends with a pencil and date it
- Measure the width at the widest point
- Note whether it leaks after rain or only in spring

A structural engineer, not just a waterproofing salesperson, is the right call for a crack that is growing, horizontal, or causing doors to stick. Read [Foundation Cracks, Which Ones Matter](/guides/foundation-cracks) to understand what each type usually means before making a call.

## Bowing Walls and What They Signal

A wall that bows inward is under lateral soil pressure. In Sioux Falls, the expansive clay soil that surrounds most older foundations absorbs moisture and swells, pushing against the wall. This is not a cosmetic problem.

Signs to look for:

- A wall that curves or tilts visibly inward
- Horizontal cracking running across a block wall
- Doors or windows on the floor above that suddenly stick or gap
- A crack that is wider at the bottom than the top

The earlier this is caught, the more options exist for repair. Walls with minor bowing may be stabilized with carbon fiber straps or wall anchors. Walls with significant movement may require more involved foundation repair in Sioux Falls, and in some cases an engineer's assessment is required before any contractor can responsibly quote the work.

If you are seeing any of these signs, do not wait until you finish the basement or sell the house. For a detailed explanation of causes and repair approaches, read [Bowing Basement Walls](/guides/bowing-basement-walls).

## Interior Drainage, Drain Tile, and Sump Pumps

When water is coming through the wall or the cove joint and outside fixes won't reach it, an interior drainage system manages the water rather than stopping it at the source. A perimeter drain tile channel is cut along the floor, gravel is packed in, and water is routed to a sump pit where a pump pushes it outside.

This is not a small project, but it is often the right answer for lateral groundwater intrusion, especially in older Sioux Falls homes where exterior waterproofing would require excavating a full foundation perimeter.

Your sump pump is the last line of defense in that system, and it can fail. A pump that runs continuously during a storm and then burns out, or one with no battery backup, can leave you with a flooded basement. The questions to ask about any sump system:

- Is there a battery backup in case of power outage?
- Where does the discharge line terminate, and is it far enough from the foundation?
- Is there a water alarm on the pit?

For everything about how sump pumps work, testing them, and what backup options exist, read [Sump Pumps, Backups and Storm Season](/guides/sump-pump-guide).

## Window Wells That Leak

Egress windows are code in finished basements and common even in unfinished ones. The wells around them are a frequent water entry point that homeowners overlook.

A window well that drains poorly fills with water and pushes it against the window frame or directly through. Gravel at the bottom of the well is meant to drain that water down and away, but if the drain is clogged or the well was never connected to a drain at all, the gravel just holds water longer.

Covers help but don't solve a drainage problem. If your window well fills with leaves and debris, it fills with water too.

Check your window wells for:

- Standing water after rain
- Rust or water staining on the window frame
- Rotted wood or efflorescence on the wall just inside

For a complete look at window well drainage, covers, and egress requirements, see [Window Wells That Leak](/guides/window-wells).

## Radon: What Sioux Falls Homeowners Need to Know

This one is easy to skip because you can't see or smell radon. Don't skip it.

According to the South Dakota Department of Agriculture and Natural Resources, eastern South Dakota is EPA Radon Zone 1, the highest-priority zone. Dakota News Now reported in April 2023, citing state data from 2008 to 2017, that Minnehaha County had an estimated mean radon level of 6.5 pCi/L across 1,920 tests. The EPA recommends action at or above 4.0 pCi/L.

That means the average tested home in this county is above the action level. Test before you finish the basement and before you sell.

The South Dakota DANR has offered free radon test kits to residents. Testing a finished basement or one you plan to finish is not optional in this region. For how to test, read results, and what mitigation looks like, go to [Radon Testing in a Sioux Falls Basement](/guides/radon-testing-sioux-falls).

## Before You Finish a Wet Basement

Finishing a basement that has had moisture problems is one of the most common and costly mistakes Sioux Falls homeowners make. Framing over a leaking wall, insulating before the moisture source is fixed, or skipping a permit can mean tearing everything out later.

The right order is:

1. Fix the water source completely
2. Wait through at least one wet season to confirm it's dry
3. Test for radon and mitigate if needed
4. Pull permits through City of Sioux Falls Building Services before framing or electrical work
5. Choose moisture-tolerant materials for the first few courses of wall

Permits matter. The City of Sioux Falls requires permits for finished basement work, and skipping them creates problems when you sell.

For a full checklist before you frame anything, read [Before You Finish a Basement That Has Been Wet](/guides/finishing-a-wet-basement).

## Choosing a Waterproofing Contractor in Sioux Falls

This is where many homeowners feel most nervous, and rightly so. The industry has no shortage of companies that lead with expensive solutions before confirming the cause.

The City of Sioux Falls requires a Residential Building Contractor's License for all contractors working on residential one and two family homes and townhomes within city limits, and according to the City, it does not accept licenses from other states. You can verify any contractor's license on the Currently Licensed Contractors list at siouxfalls.gov/licensed-contractors before signing anything.

Questions that separate careful contractors from oversellers:

- What specifically is causing the water entry in my home?
- Do I need an engineer's assessment before you can quote this accurately?
- Is the warranty transferable if I sell the house?
- What is the scope of work in writing, including materials and drainage termination points?

A contractor who explains the cause before proposing a fix, who welcomes a second opinion, and who puts everything in writing is a good sign. One who pressures you to sign the same day or dismisses the idea of an engineer is not.
