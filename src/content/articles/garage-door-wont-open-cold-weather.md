If you have ever walked into your garage on a January morning, pressed the opener button, heard the motor strain, and watched nothing happen, you are not alone. Sioux Falls winters are genuinely hard on garage doors, and cold weather failures follow a predictable pattern. The good news is that several of the most common causes are fixable in minutes without any tools. Others are warning signs of something that needs a professional. For everything from permits to choosing a company, see [Garage Door Repair in Sioux Falls, the Complete Guide](/guides/garage-door-repair-sioux-falls). This article focuses specifically on cold weather failures, working through the causes in the order you are most likely to encounter them.

## Why Sioux Falls Cold Is Harder on Garage Doors Than You Might Expect

Most of the country deals with cold snaps. Sioux Falls deals with something more sustained. A National Weather Service meteorologist, quoted by SiouxFalls.Business in February 2021, described the cold snap that month as the longest of its kind since about 1996. When temperatures stay below zero for days at a stretch, the physics of your garage door changes in several ways at once. Metal contracts. Lubricant thickens to something closer to molasses. Springs lose tension. And any moisture that collected along the bottom of the door freezes into a solid bond with the concrete floor.

What makes this particularly frustrating is that the symptoms often look the same from the outside. You press the button, the opener sounds like it is working, and the door simply does not move. That could mean five different things, and the right response depends on which one it is. Running the opener hard against a frozen door, for example, can strip gears or burn out the motor in minutes. Working through the causes in order protects both you and the equipment.

The freeze-thaw cycles that come with Sioux Falls winters also accelerate wear in ways that warmer climates do not see. A door that worked fine in October may have been slowly losing resilience in its springs and seals all fall, and the first serious cold snap is what finally pushes it past the point of functioning.

## The Most Likely Cause: Garage Door Frozen to the Ground

This is the single most common reason a garage door won't open in cold weather, and it is also the one most likely to damage your opener if you ignore it and just keep pressing the button. When snow or meltwater seeps under the bottom seal and then refreezes overnight, the door bonds to the concrete threshold. The motor runs, the spring engages, and nothing moves because the door is physically locked to the floor.

The fix is straightforward but requires patience. Do not keep running the opener. Instead, use a heat gun, a hair dryer, or even a jug of warm (not boiling) water poured carefully along the bottom edge. Work from the inside if you can, since that is usually warmer. Once the ice breaks free, manually lift the door a few inches to confirm it moves before engaging the opener again. A flat plastic scraper or a putty knife can help break the seal without gouging the door bottom or the threshold.

After you get it open, dry the threshold as well as you can. Applying a thin layer of silicon-based lubricant or even regular cooking spray to the bottom seal before the next hard freeze can prevent this from happening again. The bigger fix is replacing a worn or compressed bottom seal that is letting water in. Sioux Falls homes face blowing snow and strong prairie wind, which push moisture into gaps that would barely matter in a calmer climate. A well-fitted bottom seal is not a luxury here, it is maintenance. For more on what that repair involves, see [Garage Door Bottom Seal Replacement for Snow and Wind](/guides/garage-door-bottom-seal-replacement).

## Thickened Grease and What to Do About It

The second most common cold weather failure is lubricant that has thickened so much in the cold that it is essentially gluing the rollers, hinges, and tracks in place. Standard petroleum-based grease behaves very differently at minus ten degrees than it does at fifty. If the door moves a few inches and then stops, or moves much more slowly and noisily than usual, this is a likely culprit.

The solution is a silicone-based or lithium-based garage door lubricant, applied to the rollers, hinges, the torsion bar, and the spring. These formulas stay fluid at low temperatures where petroleum products gel up. Spray a small amount on each roller while manually cycling the door open and closed a few times. You should feel and hear the difference almost immediately.

What you want to avoid is WD-40, which is a solvent and water displacer, not a lubricant, and which can actually wash away the grease that is protecting your metal components. And never spray lubricant on the tracks themselves. The tracks are where the rollers ride, and a slippery track creates a different set of problems. The hinges, rollers, springs, and the torsion bar above the door are the right targets.

If you are not sure what lubricant is on your door right now, the safest move before winter hits is to clean off the old product with a rag and start fresh with a fresh cold-rated spray. ## Opener Force Settings and Why Cold Changes the Math

Garage door openers have an adjustable force setting that tells the motor how hard to push or pull before it assumes something is wrong and stops. This is a safety feature. The problem is that the same force that opened the door easily in September may be right at the edge of what is needed in January, when cold has stiffened every moving part.

Most openers have small adjustment screws on the back or side of the motor unit, labeled something like "up force" and "down force." Your owner's manual will tell you exactly where they are and how to adjust them. Turning the down-force adjustment up slightly gives the opener more authority to close a stiff, cold door. The same applies to the up-force setting if the door hesitates on the way up.

A word of caution: do not crank the force setting up dramatically. The auto-reverse safety feature depends on the opener stopping when it meets unexpected resistance. If you override that with excessive force, you create a safety hazard. Small adjustments, a quarter turn at a time, are the right approach. If you need to turn the force setting very high just to make the door move, the real problem is not the opener setting, it is something mechanical, and more force is not the answer.

For older openers that do not have clearly labeled adjustment controls, or openers that are already set near their maximum, this is also a good moment to evaluate whether the opener itself is the right size for the door. A single-car door and a heavy double door with a window row have very different requirements. See [Garage Door Opener Repair or Replace? How to Decide](/guides/garage-door-opener-repair-or-replace) for a fuller look at when adjustment makes sense versus when replacement is the smarter call.

## Weak Springs, Batteries, and When to Call a Pro

Springs do more of the actual lifting work than most people realize. The opener is really just a guide and a motor that handles a fraction of the load. Cold weather reduces spring tension noticeably, and a spring that was already near the end of its service life may simply not have enough left in it to lift the door when temperatures are well below zero. If the door rises a foot or two and then the opener strains to continue, or if the door falls quickly when you release it manually, a weakening spring is worth suspecting.

This is not a repair to attempt yourself. Garage door springs are under significant tension, and an amateur repair is genuinely dangerous. A trained technician has the tools to safely wind or replace torsion springs. If you hear a loud bang and the door suddenly will not open, the spring likely snapped. At that point, stop using the opener entirely. See [Broken Garage Door Spring in Sioux Falls, What to Do](/guides/broken-garage-door-spring-sioux-falls) for exactly what to do next and how to get a car out safely while you wait for service.

Remote and keypad batteries also lose effective voltage faster in cold weather. If the door works fine from the wall button but not from the remote, try fresh batteries before anything else. This is an easy fix that people often overlook. If the door fails from both the remote and the wall button, the issue is mechanical or with the opener unit itself, not the battery.

When you do call a company, it is worth asking upfront about after-hours fees. Several Sioux Falls garage door companies offer evening, weekend, or emergency service, while others keep standard weekday hours. The difference in cost between a standard call and an after-hours emergency call can be significant, so it is a fair question to ask before you book. On the licensing side, the City of Sioux Falls licenses residential contractors, and since February 2024 also offers a Residential Roofing and Repair Contractor license for certain smaller jobs. The city has not specifically listed garage doors in that announcement, so it is worth asking any company you hire which city license it holds, and you can verify whatever they tell you at siouxfalls.gov/licensed-contractors or by calling Building Services at 605-367-8672.
