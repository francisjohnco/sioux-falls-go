Deciding whether to repair or replace a furnace is one of the most stressful calls a homeowner makes, especially at 11 p.m. in January when Sioux Falls is sitting at zero degrees. The wrong choice in either direction costs real money. This article gives you a clear framework so you can walk into that conversation with a technician knowing what questions to ask. For the full picture of heating and cooling decisions in Sioux Falls, start with the [Heating and Cooling in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/hvac-guide-sioux-falls).

## How Long Does a Furnace Last in Sioux Falls?

Most gas furnaces last 15 to 20 years with consistent maintenance. Some push past 20, and some fail at 12. Sioux Falls conditions matter here.

Our winters run long, typically five to six months of serious heating demand. That is not the same load a furnace carries in, say, Raleigh. A furnace here runs more hours per year, which adds wear faster.

Hard water from the Big Sioux Aquifer runs between roughly 12.8 and 15 grains per gallon. That affects humidifiers and some system components over time, though the furnace heat exchanger itself is more affected by combustion cycles than water chemistry.

The more useful question is not just how old the furnace is, but how hard those years were. A 16-year-old furnace in a well-maintained home with annual tune-ups and regular filter changes is in a different position than a 14-year-old unit that never saw a technician until something broke.

Use age as one factor, not the whole answer.

## The 5,000 Rule: A Simple Way to Frame the Math

The 5,000 rule is a rough but useful tool. Multiply the furnace's age in years by the quoted repair cost in dollars. If the result is over 5,000, replacement is usually worth serious consideration.

Example: a 14-year-old furnace quoted at $450 for a repair gives you 14 x 450 = 6,300. That crosses the threshold.

A 7-year-old furnace quoted at $600 gives you 4,200. Repair probably makes more sense if the unit has been otherwise reliable.

This is not a hard rule, it is a conversation starter. It does not account for efficiency, comfort problems, or repair history. But it gives you a number to anchor the discussion when a technician is standing in your basement quoting a part.

## Repair History Matters as Much as Age

A furnace that has needed one repair in 15 years is different from one that has needed three in the last two winters. Repeated repairs on an aging unit are a signal worth taking seriously.

Ask yourself:

- How many paid service calls has this furnace needed in the last three years?
- Were those repairs on major components (heat exchanger, inducer motor, control board) or minor ones (igniter, flame sensor)?
- Is the same part failing more than once?

Heat exchanger cracks are a separate category. A cracked heat exchanger is a carbon monoxide risk, not just a performance problem. If a technician tells you the heat exchanger is cracked, ask for a written diagnosis and get a second opinion before spending anything. For more on that safety side, see [Carbon Monoxide and Your Furnace, What Every Homeowner Should Know](/guides/carbon-monoxide-safety-furnace).

Control board and inducer motor failures on a furnace over 15 years old often signal that other components are approaching the end of their service life too. Paying to fix one may just move the problem to the next part.

## Efficiency and Comfort: What the Numbers Actually Mean

Older furnaces often run at 80 percent AFUE or below. Modern high-efficiency furnaces run at 95 percent AFUE or higher. The difference shows up on your Xcel Energy bill every month from October through April.

Whether the efficiency upgrade pays back fast enough to justify early replacement depends on your usage, the size of the home, and how long you plan to stay. Do not let anyone give you a specific payback guarantee without showing you the math in writing.

Comfort problems are also worth factoring in:

- Cold rooms or uneven heat that persists even when the furnace is running
- The furnace running almost constantly on cold days but not keeping up
- Excessive dust or dry air that worsens every winter

These are not always solved by replacement, but they are worth noting when you are already weighing the decision. If cold rooms are part of the picture, [Cold Rooms and Uneven Heat, Causes and Fixes](/guides/uneven-heating-cold-rooms) covers what to look at before assuming a new furnace fixes it.

## When a Big Repair Quote Deserves a Second Opinion

Sioux Falls homeowners have reported being quoted very different prices for the same repair by different companies, sometimes saving hundreds of dollars by making one more call. That is not a reason to distrust every technician, but it is a reason to slow down on any quote over a few hundred dollars.

When you get a large repair quote, ask the technician to give you in writing:

- The specific part name and part number
- The labor time estimated
- Whether the diagnosis fee applies toward the repair
- The warranty on the part and the labor separately

Then call one other company and describe the same part and situation. You are not trying to catch anyone in a lie, you are getting enough information to make a confident decision. For a fuller guide on this process, see [When to Get a Second Opinion on an HVAC Repair](/guides/hvac-second-opinion).

If the repair quote is close to or above the cost of a new unit, ask what a replacement would include and get that in writing too. The City of Sioux Falls requires a mechanical permit for furnace replacement work. According to the City of Sioux Falls Building Permit FAQs, you can verify a contractor's license at 605-367-8672 or on the city's Currently Licensed Contractors list. A legitimate company will pull that permit without you having to ask.

## Making the Call: A Quick Decision Framework

When you are standing in front of a decision, run through these questions:

- Is the furnace over 15 years old? If yes, lean toward replacement when any major component fails.
- Has it needed multiple repairs in the last two to three years? Each additional repair on an aging unit reduces the case for continuing.
- Is the repair quote large enough that the 5,000 rule tips past 5,000? Consider replacement seriously.
- Is there a heat exchanger crack or a CO concern? Do not repair. Get a second opinion and plan for replacement.
- Is the furnace under 10 years old with a good repair history? A single repair is usually the right move.

One more thing worth noting: if you are replacing a furnace, you will face decisions about efficiency ratings, sizing, venting, and whether to match a new AC at the same time. Those choices all affect the final cost in ways that are worth understanding before you sign anything.
