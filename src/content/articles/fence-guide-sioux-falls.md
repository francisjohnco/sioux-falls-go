Building a fence in Sioux Falls involves more than picking a style and calling someone with a post driver. You have city permits, height limits that change by yard, property line questions your neighbor may see differently, soil that can push posts right out of the ground, and HOA covenants that can require you to tear the whole thing down if you skip them. This guide walks through every step in the order you actually need to do them, with the local details that matter most and links to deeper articles on each topic.

## Start with the Permit, Not the Quote

The first thing many homeowners skip is the permit, and it is the first thing that causes trouble later.

According to the City of Sioux Falls, a permit is required to build, move, replace, or repair a fence. That includes new installations and full replacements, not just brand-new construction on a bare lot.

The City of Sioux Falls lists the permit fee at $20. After construction, you must schedule a final inspection by calling the Zoning Office at 605-367-8254. Permits expire six months after issue, though extensions are available.

Skipping the permit does not just risk a fine. It can complicate a home sale, void a contractor's work, or mean pulling the fence out if an inspector flags it. Some fence companies will pull the permit for you as part of the job, which is worth asking about before you sign anything.

For the full permit walkthrough, what the application asks for, and what happens at inspection, see [Fence Permits in Sioux Falls, What to Know](/guides/fence-permit-sioux-falls).

## Know Your Height Limits Before You Design Anything

Height rules in Sioux Falls depend on where the fence sits on your lot, not just what you want.

According to the City of Sioux Falls residential fence guide, fences in the front yard can be up to four feet tall, on both corner lots and interior lots. The City guide applies that same limit to corner lots regardless of which yard faces the street.

Rear and side yards generally allow taller fences, but the specific limits depend on your lot type and your zoning district. The safest move is to confirm with the Zoning Office before you finalize your design, not after you have already ordered materials.

Corner lots have additional sight-line rules that can limit where you place a fence near an intersection. If your property sits on a corner, ask the Zoning Office about sight-triangle requirements before you draft any plan.

For a full breakdown of front, side, and rear yard rules, corner lot specifics, and what covenants can add on top of City rules, see [Fence Height Rules by Yard](/guides/fence-height-rules).

## Check Your HOA or Neighborhood Covenants

City rules are the floor, not the ceiling. Many Sioux Falls neighborhoods, including newer subdivisions and established areas with active homeowner associations, have covenants that restrict fence height, style, material, or color beyond what the City requires.

Covenants can require you to get written HOA approval before breaking ground. Building without that approval can result in a mandatory removal even if the fence fully complies with City code.

Your covenants are usually recorded with Minnehaha or Lincoln County. If you are not sure whether your neighborhood has them, check your closing documents, ask your HOA management company, or search the county recorder's records online.

Homeowners in neighborhoods like All Saints or the older blocks near McKennan Park often find covenants that require specific materials or styles that fit the neighborhood's character. Newer developments on the south or west edges of the city may have detailed HOA design standards as a condition of the original plat.

For the approval process and what to do if you bought a home and are not sure what covenants exist, see [Fences, Covenants and HOA Approval](/guides/fence-and-hoa).

## Confirm Your Property Lines Before the First Post Goes In

The single most common neighbor dispute around fencing comes down to one question: where exactly does your property end?

According to the City of Sioux Falls residential fence guide, locating property lines should be done by a licensed surveyor. Survey pins are the legal boundary markers, but they can be buried, moved, or missing after years of landscaping and grading.

Do not rely on a fence your neighbor installed, a sidewalk edge, or a rough estimate from Google Maps. If you build two inches over the line, you may have to move the fence at your own expense.

Talking to your neighbor before construction, not after, also matters. Sioux Falls homeowners have ended up in disputes where the fence was already in the ground before anyone compared notes. If you and your neighbor agree on the line and agree to share costs or placement, put it in writing.

For how to find your survey pins, what a survey costs, what setbacks mean, and how to document a neighbor agreement, see [Property Lines, Surveys and Your Neighbor](/guides/property-line-fence).

## Call 811 Before Anyone Digs

This step takes a few minutes and is not optional.

Calling 811 before any digging starts triggers a free utility locate service. Underground gas, electric, water, and telecom lines get marked so your installer knows where not to dig. Hitting a utility line is dangerous and can result in costs far beyond any fence job.

Call at least a few business days before your planned dig date, since locates take time to schedule. Note that 811 marks public utility lines, but private lines (a line you ran to a shed, for example) are your responsibility to know and mark yourself.

Your fence installer should ask about this before scheduling the dig. If they do not bring it up, ask them directly.

For timing, what happens during a locate, and how to handle private lines, see [Call 811 Before You Dig a Fence Line](/guides/call-811-before-digging).

## Choose Materials That Hold Up to Sioux Falls Winters

Fence material is not just about how something looks on day one. It is about how it performs after several winters of freeze-thaw cycles, heavy snow, and wind.

Sioux Falls homeowners have learned that some materials hold up better than others once the ground starts moving and snow sits against the boards for weeks. Wood is popular for privacy and curb appeal, but it needs regular maintenance, and gaps in staining or sealing can accelerate rot. Vinyl does not rot and is low maintenance, but cheaper vinyl can become brittle in deep cold. Aluminum holds up well and does not rust, but it does not give much privacy. Chain link is durable and low cost but purely functional.

The right material also depends on what you are fencing for. A containment fence for a large dog needs different specs than a decorative front yard fence or a privacy fence along a rear property line.

For a full comparison of how each material performs in a cold climate, what maintenance looks like, and which situations each one fits best, see [Wood, Vinyl, Aluminum or Chain Link?](/guides/fence-materials-cold-climate).

## Understand Why Posts Heave and What to Do About It

Sioux Falls homeowners have watched new fences lean or bow after just one or two winters. The most common cause is frost heave, where soil movement pushes posts upward or sideways as the ground freezes and thaws.

Clay soil makes this worse, and much of the soil under Sioux Falls-area lawns has significant clay content. Clay holds moisture, and moisture in clay expands when it freezes. A post set without an adequate footing or at insufficient depth is at real risk.

This is why post depth and footing design matter as much as the fence style itself. When you get quotes, ask specifically how deep each company sets posts, how they form the footing, and what they do differently in clay-heavy soil. Do not assume all installers handle this the same way.

For the full explanation of what causes heave, what a proper footing looks like, and what to do if posts are already leaning, see [Why Fence Posts Heave and Lean](/guides/fence-posts-frost-heave).

## Plan Your Gates Carefully

A gate that does not latch is not a safety feature for a dog or a child. Gates are also the part of a fence that takes the most abuse and fails first if they are not built right.

A sagging gate usually comes down to one of three things: an undersized or poorly set gate post, hinges that were not heavy enough for the gate's weight, or frost movement in the post. Wood gates are especially prone to sagging because wood moves with moisture and weight over time.

When designing your fence, think about where you need gate access, how wide the opening needs to be (can a lawn mower get through?), and whether the gate will be used heavily or mostly for emergencies. Double gates, which span wider openings, need center drop rods or they will swing and drift.

For what causes sagging, how to diagnose your specific gate problem, and when repair makes more sense than rebuilding, see [Fixing a Sagging Fence Gate](/guides/sagging-fence-gate).

## Get Quotes You Can Actually Compare

Once you have done the groundwork, permits confirmed, covenants checked, property lines verified, materials chosen, you are ready to get quotes.

The problem with fence quotes is that they are easy to miscompare. One quote may include permit fees; another may not. One may include old fence removal; another assumes the site is clear. Post depth, footing type, number of gates, gate hardware quality, and site cleanup can all vary between bids and dramatically affect what you end up paying.

Ask every company for an itemized quote that breaks out at minimum: linear feet of fence, number and type of posts, number of gates, any demolition or removal, the permit fee, and cleanup. Ask what warranty covers labor versus materials, and get it in writing.

Nationally, fence installation varies widely depending on material, length, and site conditions. Do not use any number you see online as a Sioux Falls average. The only number that matters for your project is a written quote for your specific lot, material, and scope.
