You notice a water stain on the ceiling, a drip in the garage, or a wet wall after a heavy rain. The first instinct is to look straight up and find the hole. But roof leaks almost never work that simply, and chasing the wrong spot wastes time while the damage keeps spreading.

This article walks you through the real sources of rain leaks, what you can do before a roofer arrives, and what an experienced contractor will actually inspect. For a broader look at repair versus replacement decisions, see [Roof Repair and Replacement in Sioux Falls, the Complete Guide](/guides/roof-repair-replacement-sioux-falls).

## Why the Leak Spot Inside Is Not the Problem Spot

Water does not fall straight down inside a house the way it does outside. It follows the path of least resistance: down a rafter, along a ceiling joist, pooling at a low point before it finally drips through.

That drip you see on your living room ceiling could be sourced from a failed piece of flashing six feet up the slope. A garage roof leak that shows up at the wall is often water that entered near the ridge or at a joint, then traveled the entire rafter span before showing itself.

This is why guessing at the source from inside is unreliable. A roofer traces water backward, from where it appears inside up through the decking and framing, to find where it actually entered.

## The Most Common Sources of a Roof Leak After Rain

Most rain leaks come from a small list of trouble spots. Here is what to suspect:

**Flashing failures**
Flashing is the metal trim that seals joints where the roof meets a vertical surface: chimneys, walls, dormer sides, and vent pipes. It is the single most common rain leak source. Flashing can lift, crack, or lose its sealant over time. A heavy rain pushes water into the gap.

**Valleys**
A valley is where two roof planes meet at a low angle. Water concentrates there in heavy rain. If the valley flashing or underlayment is compromised, it can let water through faster than any flat section of shingles would.

**Skylights and vents**
The curb seal around a skylight and the collar around a pipe boot are both small, easy-to-miss failure points. UV and freeze-thaw cycles work on these seals constantly. A roof leak after rain that tracks straight down from a skylight is a common pattern in older Sioux Falls homes.

**Gutters and fascia**
A clogged gutter overflows at the fascia line and can back up under the first course of shingles. The leak shows up at the eave or just inside the exterior wall. Gutters that have pulled away from the fascia create the same problem. This is an especially common source of a garage roof leak because garage eaves are often lower and collect debris faster.

**Missing or lifted shingles**
After wind or age, shingles can lift, crease, or blow off entirely. Without a shingle, the underlayment is exposed. It holds for a while, but a driving rain eventually wins. See [Wind Damage to Shingles After a Sioux Falls Storm](/guides/wind-damage-shingles-sioux-falls) for how to check and document this from the ground safely.

**Step flashing along additions**
Where a room addition or garage addition meets the main house, step flashing is installed up the wall. This is a high-failure zone, especially on older additions, and it is a source that is easy to miss on a quick visual inspection.

## What to Do Tonight Before a Roofer Arrives

You cannot safely walk a wet, steep roof. Do not try. What you can do:

- Put buckets or towels down to protect flooring and belongings.
- Take photos of every wet area, every stain, and any visible damage from the ground or attic.
- If you can safely enter the attic with a flashlight, look for daylight, wet insulation, or dark staining on the decking. Note where it is relative to the ridge and exterior walls.
- If the ceiling is bubbling or sagging with trapped water, carefully puncture it at the lowest point to let water drain into a bucket before it collapses.

If the weather forecast shows more rain coming in the next day or two, call a roofer and ask specifically about a temporary tarp. This is a reasonable request, not an unusual one. Keep receipts for any materials you buy for temporary protection, because the South Dakota Division of Insurance advises homeowners to document and save receipts for temporary repairs, while not making permanent repairs before contacting the insurer. You can reach the Division of Insurance at 605-773-3563.

Do not sign anything from a door-to-door contractor who shows up after a storm. The South Dakota Attorney General's consumer protection office specifically warns homeowners not to be pressured into a quick agreement after hailstorms, and door-to-door sales contracts typically carry a three-day right to cancel.

## What a Roofer Will Actually Check

A thorough roofer does not just walk to the spot above the stain and stop there. Here is the sequence a good inspection follows:

**Inside the attic first**
The roofer looks for moisture staining on decking and rafters, soft spots, daylight, and the direction water traveled. This often narrows the search before they even go up.

**All penetrations on the roof**
Every pipe, vent, skylight, and chimney gets checked. Sealant around pipe boots degrades over time. A roofer will flex and probe these to find the failure point.

**All flashing, in order**
Chimney flashing (step and counter), wall flashing along dormers or additions, and valley flashing all get a close look. Flashing is often the culprit even when shingles look fine from the ground.

**Gutters and the drip edge**
A roofer checks whether the drip edge is properly installed and whether gutters are pulling away or packed with debris at the fascia line.

**Shingle condition in the affected zones**
Lifted tabs, cracked shingles, or missing granules concentrated in one area can point to where water is entering.

If a roofer you contact is only willing to look at the spot above the stain and quote a patch without checking penetrations and flashing, that is worth noting. A leak that returns twice because the actual source was never found is a pattern Sioux Falls homeowners have described.

Before any work begins, confirm the contractor holds the City of Sioux Falls Residential Roofing and Repair Contractor license, which requires a $20,000 compliance bond and at least $300,000 in general liability insurance, according to the City of Sioux Falls. You can call Building Services licensing at 605-367-8670 to verify.

## Repair, Replace, or Wait?

A single leak from a failed pipe boot or a piece of lifted flashing on an otherwise sound roof is a repair situation. The fix can be targeted and relatively inexpensive, though costs depend on the specific work involved, and you should get a written quote before agreeing to anything.

A leak that is part of a pattern on an aging roof, or one where the roofer finds soft decking, widespread shingle failure, or multiple flashing problems, is a different conversation. At that point, repeated spot repairs may cost more over two years than a replacement would. [Repair or Replace Your Roof? A Sioux Falls Homeowner's Guide](/guides/repair-or-replace-roof-sioux-falls) covers how to think through that decision.

If the leak followed a hailstorm or a major wind event, document everything and contact your insurer before authorizing permanent repairs. The SD Division of Insurance advises homeowners to photograph damage, file promptly, and not make permanent repairs before contacting the insurer. Policies can set notice deadlines.

A leak after rain is stressful, but it is almost always fixable once the real source is found. The key is working with someone willing to do the diagnostic work, not just slap a patch and move on.
