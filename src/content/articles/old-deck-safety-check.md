Spring in Sioux Falls has a way of revealing what winter quietly destroyed. After months of freeze-thaw cycles and snow sitting on decking boards, an aging deck can look fine from across the yard and be genuinely dangerous up close.

If your deck is 15 to 20 years old, this is the right time to do a real inspection before anyone puts a chair on it. Many Sioux Falls homeowners reach this point and wonder the same thing: does this need repair, or is it time to rebuild? For the full picture on planning a new deck, start with [Building a Deck in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/deck-guide-sioux-falls).

This checklist walks through every part of the structure, what to look for, and what each problem usually means.

## Start With What You Can See: Boards and Surface Condition

Walk the entire deck surface slowly, pressing down with your foot on every board. What you feel tells you as much as what you see.

Watch for:

- Soft or spongy spots when you step (rot underneath, not just surface weathering)
- Boards that flex or bounce more than others
- Cracking along the grain that runs deeper than surface checks
- Cupped or warped boards that have lifted at the edges
- Gray, dry, or splintering wood with no visible moisture protection left

Surface rot on individual boards is often repairable. But if you find soft spots in several areas, or if the wood crushes when you press a screwdriver into it, the problem may have moved into the structure below.

If the deck has never been stained or sealed, or not in the last several years, the surface wood has taken a lot of abuse. [Staining and Sealing a Wood Deck](/guides/deck-stain-seal) explains what a treated deck should look like and how often it needs attention.

## The Frame Underneath: Joists, Beams, and Posts

This is where old deck repair gets serious. The frame carries the load, and rot here is a structural problem, not a cosmetic one.

You may need to crouch down or use a flashlight to get a good look. If your deck has a skirt board hiding the frame, remove a section or look through any gaps before you assume everything is fine.

Check for:

- Soft wood at the top of the joists, where water pools and sits
- Dark staining or fuzzy growth on any framing member
- Posts that feel loose, rock slightly, or show rot at the base where they meet concrete or soil
- Joist hangers and connectors that are rusty, bent, or pulling away from the wood
- Beams that show any sag or bow when viewed from the end

The hardware is easy to overlook. Galvanized fasteners and joist hangers have a lifespan too, and in Sioux Falls, the repeated freeze-thaw movement every spring accelerates metal fatigue and wood splitting around screws and nails.

If a post shows rot at grade level, the footing below it needs attention as well. Footings that were not set deep enough to clear the frost line can heave over years, tilting the whole structure. [Deck Footings and Frost Heave](/guides/deck-footings-frost) covers what to look for and when it becomes a rebuild decision.

## The Ledger Board: The One Place You Cannot Afford to Miss

The ledger is the board attached to your house that anchors the deck to the structure. It is the most common failure point on older decks, and it is the place most homeowners skip because it is hard to see.

Water gets behind the ledger when flashing is missing, damaged, or was never installed correctly. Once moisture is trapped between the ledger and the house rim joist, rot works fast and silently.

Look for:

- Any gap between the ledger and the house siding or rim joist
- Missing, lifted, or corroded flashing above the ledger
- Staining on the house siding directly below or beside where the ledger attaches
- Soft wood at the ledger itself or at the house framing behind it
- Lag bolts or screws that look corroded or that you can wiggle

If the ledger has rotted, it is not a patch job. It is a significant repair, and depending on how far the damage has spread into the house rim joist, it can affect the cost and scope of any rebuild considerably. [The Ledger Board, Where Decks Fail](/guides/deck-ledger-flashing) goes into more detail on how attachment and flashing work and what a proper inspection should include.

## Railings and Stairs: What Actually Injures People

A lot of deck injuries happen on railings and stairs, not on the decking surface. By the time a railing wiggles noticeably, it has often been loose for years.

Test every railing section by pushing and pulling it firmly from the top. It should not flex. Check each post where it connects to the frame or the decking.

Watch for:

- Rail posts that move in any direction when pushed
- Balusters (the vertical pieces) that are cracked, missing, or spaced irregularly
- The top rail cap that is split, lifting, or rotating
- Stair stringers (the diagonal supports) that show rot, cracks, or separation from the frame
- Stair treads that flex, feel soft, or are pulling away from the stringers
- Loose or missing fasteners at any connection point

Older decks were often built to codes that have since been updated. Even if the structure is solid, rail height and baluster spacing on a 20-year-old deck may not meet current standards. If you are having significant repair work done, a licensed contractor and a city inspector can tell you what current requirements apply to your project.

## When to Repair and When to Rebuild

After walking through this checklist, you will likely land in one of three places.

**Surface-level problems only.** Boards are worn or damaged, but the frame is solid, the ledger is dry and well-attached, and the posts and footings are stable. This is a good candidate for resurfacing or targeted old deck repair.

**Mixed damage.** Some framing issues alongside surface wear. This is where you need a licensed contractor to look before you decide anything. The repair cost versus rebuild cost question depends heavily on how far the structural damage has spread.

**Widespread structural rot or movement.** Multiple joists compromised, ledger damage, posts that have shifted, footings that have heaved. At this point, rebuilding usually makes more financial and safety sense than patching.

Before any significant work starts, check whether a permit is required. According to the City of Sioux Falls Building Permit FAQs, decks require a building permit. Structural repairs that go beyond cosmetic work typically require a permit as well. Confirm with City Building Services what your project scope triggers.

If you hire someone, verify they hold a Residential Building Contractor's License issued by the City of Sioux Falls. The City does not accept licenses from other states, and the City publishes a Currently Licensed Contractors list at siouxfalls.gov/licensed-contractors. Unlicensed work can create serious problems if you sell the home or file an insurance claim.
