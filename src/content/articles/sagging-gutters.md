Sagging gutters and gutters pulling away from the house are two of the most common calls local contractors get, and both problems look worse than they usually are. Most of the time the fix is straightforward. But left alone, either problem sends water exactly where you do not want it: behind your siding, down your foundation wall, or pooling against your home all winter. If you are new to this topic, start with [Gutters for a Sioux Falls Home, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/gutter-guide-sioux-falls) for a full picture before diving in here.

This article focuses on what causes sagging gutters and gutter pulling away from the fascia, how to tell whether repair or replacement makes sense, and what to check before you call anyone.

## Why Gutters Sag: The Real Causes

The gutter itself is rarely the first thing that fails. What fails is whatever is holding it up.

**Hangers spaced too far apart.** Gutters are held to the house with hangers, usually hidden spikes or screws driven through the gutter face into the fascia and rafter tails behind it. When hangers are spaced too far apart, the gutter flexes under load and sags between the attachment points. This shows up as a gentle dip or wave along the gutter run.

**Hangers that have worked loose.** Over time, the fasteners can back out of the wood, especially if the original installation used nails instead of screws. Every freeze-thaw cycle in a Sioux Falls winter puts mechanical stress on those connections. Each cycle the gutter expands slightly with ice and contracts as it melts, and nails back out a little more each time.

**Ice and snow weight.** When gutters fill with ice or hold heavy wet snow, the load is far greater than rainwater alone. Homeowners here often notice that gutters that were perfectly level in October look noticeably dipped by February. That is ice weight doing real work on the hangers. See [Gutters, Icicles and Ice Dams](/guides/gutters-and-ice-dams) for more on how winter buildup stresses your whole gutter system.

**Rotted or soft fascia board.** This one matters most. The fascia is the flat board that runs along your roofline and is what the gutter hangs from. When the fascia rots, there is nothing solid left for a hanger to grip. You can re-hang the gutter a dozen times and it will keep pulling away if the fascia is not repaired first. Rot usually starts where the gutter has been holding standing water or where the gutter seal has failed and water gets behind the back edge.

## How to Inspect Your Gutters From the Ground (and Safely Up Close)

You can learn a lot without leaving the ground.

Walk the perimeter of your house and look for:

- Any section of gutter that dips visibly between hangers
- Gaps between the top back edge of the gutter and the fascia board
- Rust stains, paint peeling, or dark streaking on the fascia below the gutter
- Sections that have pulled away entirely and are only held by one or two points
- Joints or end caps that have separated because the gutter shifted

If you go up a ladder, have someone hold it and do not lean out past your hips. With the gutter at eye level, press on the front face and feel whether it flexes. Try to wiggle it. A solid gutter barely moves. A loose one shifts noticeably at the hangers. Look at the fascia behind the top edge of the gutter. If it looks dark, soft, or has visible gaps, that board likely needs attention before anything else.

Also look at the top inside surface of the gutter. Standing water that never drains indicates a slope problem. Gutters should drop about a quarter inch for every ten feet of run toward the downspout. A sagging section creates a low point that holds water, which speeds up rust on steel gutters and contributes to more ice buildup in winter.

## Repair or Replace: How to Think Through It

Not every sagging gutter needs to be replaced. Here is a simple way to frame the decision.

**Repair usually makes sense when:**

- The gutter itself is in good shape (no cracks, splits, or significant corrosion)
- The fascia is solid and the hangers simply need to be repositioned or replaced with screws
- The sag is limited to one or two sections
- The gutter is relatively new

**Replacement usually makes more sense when:**

- The fascia is rotted and needs to be replaced anyway, making re-hanging costly relative to new gutters
- The gutter is old, corroded, or has multiple failing seams
- The sag is across most of the run, suggesting original installation problems
- You are already doing roofing or siding work (combining projects saves money on labor and staging)

Sioux Falls homeowners who have been through hail seasons sometimes discover sagging gutters at the same time as storm damage to the roof or siding. When the scope grows to include multiple systems, it is worth reading [Hail Damaged Gutters and What Insurance Looks At](/guides/hail-damaged-gutters) before you commit to any scope of work with a contractor.

One important point: if the fascia is rotted, do not let anyone hang new gutters over it without replacing the board first. That repair will fail again, possibly faster.

## What the Repair Actually Involves

For a simple hanger problem on solid fascia, the repair is not complicated.

A contractor will remove the gutter at the problem section, re-align it to the correct slope, and re-hang it with new screws (not nails) driven into the rafter tails behind the fascia. Hangers should be spaced no more than roughly two feet apart, and closer in areas that see heavy ice loads, which describes most of Sioux Falls in a typical winter.

If the fascia needs to be replaced, that work happens first. The gutter comes down, the rotted board is removed and replaced with new primed lumber or a composite board, and then the gutter is re-hung. This is a bigger job but it is the right sequence.

For a gutter that has pulled away from the house along a longer run, a contractor may also need to check the rafter tails behind the fascia for rot, since water that damages the fascia often gets into the structure behind it too.

According to the City of Sioux Falls, gutters and fascia do not require a building permit, so this work can move quickly once you have scheduled a contractor. That said, if the fascia repair is part of a larger project involving re-siding or significant structural work, different licensing may apply.

## Hiring Someone to Fix It: What to Ask

The City of Sioux Falls requires a Residential Building Contractor's License for all contractors working on residential homes within city limits, and the City does not accept licenses from other states. You can verify any contractor at siouxfalls.gov/licensed-contractors before signing anything.

When you call for quotes, ask:

- Will you replace the fascia if it is rotted, or just re-hang the gutter? (The answer should always be to fix the fascia first)
- Are you using screws or spikes? (Screws hold through freeze-thaw cycles, spikes do not)
- How far apart will hangers be spaced?
- What is included in the quote: labor, materials, fascia if needed, and cleanup?
- Do you carry liability insurance and workers' compensation?

Get at least two written quotes before agreeing to anything. Because fascia rot is not always visible until the gutter comes down, ask each contractor how they handle the possibility that the rot is more extensive than it appears from the outside. A contractor who gives you a clear answer to that question before work starts is easier to work with than one who surprises you with add-ons mid-job.
