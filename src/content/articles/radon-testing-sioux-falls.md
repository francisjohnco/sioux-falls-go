Radon does not smell, it does not show up as a stain on the wall, and it does not trip your sump pump alarm. It seeps up from the soil beneath a home and collects quietly in the lowest livable space. For Sioux Falls homeowners, it is worth taking seriously: according to Dakota News Now reporting in April 2023 on state data from 2008 to 2017, Minnehaha County had an estimated mean radon level of 6.5 pCi/L across 1,920 tests, well above the 4.0 pCi/L level at which the EPA recommends taking action. If you are thinking about the full picture of what is happening in your basement, [A Dry Basement in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/basement-waterproofing-guide-sioux-falls) is a good place to start before or after you read this.

## Why Radon Is a Real Concern in Sioux Falls

The EPA divides the country into radon zones based on predicted indoor levels. According to the South Dakota Department of Agriculture and Natural Resources (DANR), the eastern portion of South Dakota, which includes Sioux Falls and Minnehaha County, falls into EPA Radon Zone 1, the highest-risk category.

That does not mean every home in Sioux Falls has a problem. It means the geology here is more likely to produce elevated levels than in many other parts of the country.

Radon comes from uranium decaying naturally in rock and soil. It enters a basement through cracks in the floor, the cove joint where the floor meets the wall, gaps around pipes, and even through poured concrete. Finished or unfinished, dry or damp, any basement can have elevated radon.

The only way to know your home's level is to test. DANR has stated plainly that there is no other way to determine a building's radon level, and the department has offered free test kits to South Dakota residents in past programs. Check DANR's current website to see if free kits are available when you are ready to test.

## How a Radon Test Actually Works

There are two main types of tests a homeowner can run without hiring anyone: short-term charcoal canisters and long-term alpha track detectors.

Short-term tests run for 2 to 7 days. They give you a quick snapshot, which is useful if you are on a deadline, such as a home sale.

Long-term tests run for 90 days or more. They measure the level across seasons and changing weather, which gives a more accurate picture of what your family is actually exposed to over time.

Both types are placed in the lowest livable area of the home, usually the basement. Placement matters:

- Keep the test at least 20 inches above the floor
- Keep it away from exterior walls, windows, and drafts
- Do not run fans or leave windows open during a short-term test
- Follow the kit instructions exactly, because small errors affect results

After the test period, you mail the device to a lab. Results come back with a number in picocuries per liter (pCi/L). The EPA recommends action at 4.0 pCi/L or higher, and considers levels between 2.0 and 4.0 pCi/L worth addressing if you can.

## Reading Your Results and What They Mean

If your result comes back below 2.0 pCi/L, most guidance says no action is needed, though retesting every few years is a reasonable habit.

If your result is between 2.0 and 3.9 pCi/L, the EPA considers mitigation optional but worthwhile. Some homeowners in this range choose to act, especially if they spend a lot of time in the basement.

If your result is 4.0 pCi/L or higher, the EPA recommends mitigation. The mean level in Minnehaha County is 6.5 pCi/L according to that 2023 Dakota News Now report, so a result in that range is not unusual here, but it is a reason to call a certified mitigator.

A single short-term test at an unusually high or low level is worth confirming with a second test before making decisions. If the first result is very high, above 10 pCi/L, run a second short-term test rather than waiting for a long-term one.

## Radon Mitigation: What the Fix Looks Like

The most common fix for elevated radon in a basement is a sub-slab depressurization system. A contractor drills one or more holes through the basement floor, inserts a pipe, and runs it up through or around the house to vent outside above the roofline. A small fan draws radon-laden air out from under the slab before it can enter the living space.

The system runs continuously and quietly. Most homeowners never notice it after installation.

What to ask a mitigation contractor before they start:

- Are you certified through the National Radon Proficiency Program (NRPP) or National Radon Safety Board (NRSB)?
- Will you run a post-mitigation test to confirm the level dropped below 4.0 pCi/L?
- What does the fan warranty cover and who services it if it fails?
- Does your license cover residential work in Sioux Falls?

On that last point: the City of Sioux Falls requires a Residential Building Contractor's License for all contractors working on residential one and two family homes within city limits, and the City does not accept licenses from other states. You can confirm a contractor is on the City's current list at siouxfalls.gov/licensed-contractors before anyone starts drilling through your floor.

Do not guess at costs based on national averages you read online. Ask for a written quote that includes post-mitigation testing.

## Radon, Home Sales, and Finished Basements

If you are selling a home in Sioux Falls, expect buyers to request a radon test during inspection. A result at or above 4.0 pCi/L will almost certainly come up in negotiations.

Getting ahead of it, testing before you list and mitigating if needed, removes the uncertainty and gives you documentation to share with buyers.

If you are buying a home with a finished basement, ask whether radon has ever been tested and whether a mitigation system is present. A passive pipe without a fan is not a fully active system. Confirm any existing system has been tested after installation.

If you are thinking about finishing an unfinished basement, radon testing belongs near the top of your to-do list before you start. Finishing a space and then discovering elevated radon means disrupting new flooring and drywall to install a mitigation pipe. For more on what to check before you finish a basement that has had any moisture history, [Before You Finish a Basement That Has Been Wet](/guides/finishing-a-wet-basement) covers the sequencing well.

One more thing worth knowing: radon levels can shift if you significantly change a basement's airflow, such as adding HVAC, sealing cracks, or installing a full interior drainage system. If you have recent waterproofing work done, a follow-up radon test is a good idea even if you tested before.
