Every winter, Sioux Falls homeowners look up and see icicles hanging from their gutters and wonder whether something is broken. Sometimes it is, and sometimes the gutters are just doing what physics demands. Knowing the difference saves you money and keeps you from "fixing" the wrong thing. For a full picture of how gutters work on a Sioux Falls home year-round, start with the [Gutters for a Sioux Falls Home, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/gutter-guide-sioux-falls).

## Why Ice Forms in Gutters in the First Place

Snow sits on your roof. The underside of that snow layer is in contact with your roof deck, which is warmed by heat escaping from your living space. That heat melts the bottom of the snowpack, and water runs down the slope.

When that water reaches the cold overhang above your gutters, the heat from inside the house is gone. The water refreezes.

Your gutters catch that refreezing water and hold it. Ice builds up, more meltwater backs up behind it, and you get a ridge of ice at the eave. That ridge is an ice dam, and the water pooling behind it has nowhere to go except under your shingles.

The gutters did not cause this. The source is heat loss through your attic floor or roof deck. Gutters are just where you can see the result.

## What Gutters Do and Do Not Cause

Gutters get blamed for ice dams, but they are rarely the reason a dam forms. A dam can build on a house with no gutters at all.

What gutters do affect:

- Where the ice concentrates (at the eave, inside the gutter channel)
- How heavy the ice load on the gutter becomes
- Whether the gutter pulls away from the fascia under that weight (see [Sagging or Pulling Away Gutters](/guides/sagging-gutters) for what to watch for)
- Whether meltwater that escapes the dam has a path away from your foundation

A clogged gutter going into winter does make ice dams worse. Standing water in a gutter freezes solid, creates a thicker ice base, and gives future meltwater even less room to drain. Clearing your gutters before the first freeze is one of the simplest things you can do. [Getting Gutters Ready for Winter](/guides/winter-gutter-prep) covers that checklist in detail.

## Where Ice Dams Really Start

The honest answer is your attic, not your roof line. When the attic floor is under-insulated or has air leaks around fixtures, wiring, and plumbing penetrations, heat from your living space rises directly into the attic cavity.

That warm attic heats the roof deck from underneath. The deck stays warm enough to melt snow even when it is well below freezing outside.

The fix is sealing those air leaks and adding insulation to the attic floor, not to the rafters. A properly cold attic means the roof deck stays close to outside air temperature, the snow does not melt unevenly, and you stop feeding the cycle that creates ice dams in the first place.

For a thorough explanation of how ice dams damage roofs and what an attic inspection should cover, the [Ice Dams on Sioux Falls Roofs](/guides/ice-dams-sioux-falls-roof) guide goes deep on the building science and repair side.

## Heat Cables: What They Do and When They Make Sense

Heat cables (also called heat tape or roof de-icing cables) run along the eave and sometimes down into the gutter channel. They draw enough electricity to keep a narrow path of water liquid so meltwater can drain instead of freezing solid.

They are a management tool, not a cure. The attic problem that causes the dam is still there.

When heat cables make sense:

- On a specific problem section of roof where re-insulating is not practical
- As a temporary measure while you plan a larger attic project
- On low-slope sections or dormers that consistently trap ice

When they may not be worth it:

- Whole-roof installation on a large home adds up in electricity costs
- Cables that are not installed in a zigzag pattern at the eave can create their own ice ridges
- Cheap cables can fail after a season or two

If you have heat cables installed, have an electrician inspect the connection points before each winter. Damaged insulation on a heat cable sitting in wet, freezing conditions is a real hazard.

## Removing Ice Safely

The wrong removal method causes more damage than the ice itself. Chipping at gutters with a shovel or ice pick can dent aluminum gutters, crack the gutter seam, and bend hangers away from the fascia.

Safe options:

- **Calcium chloride ice melt** in a nylon stocking or mesh tube, laid perpendicular across the ice dam, creates a channel for water to drain. Do not use rock salt; it corrodes aluminum and damages shingles.
- **A roof rake** with a long handle lets you pull snow off the lower few feet of the roof from the ground. Removing snow before it melts removes the source of water.
- **Warm water from a hose** can clear a gutter channel in mild above-freezing weather, but the water needs somewhere to drain before it refreezes.

Do not climb onto an icy roof. The slope that looked manageable in September is a different thing with ice on it.

The City of Sioux Falls reminds residents to place any cleared snow in your yard, not on sidewalks, streets, or the right of way, since depositing snow in the street violates City ordinance.

## The Attic Fix That Actually Solves It

If you have ice dams every winter, budget for an energy audit or a conversation with an insulation contractor. The goal is a cold roof deck.

The two-part solution most building science professionals recommend:

1. **Air sealing first.** Close every penetration in the attic floor: recessed light cans, plumbing stacks, wiring holes, the attic hatch. Blown-in insulation alone does not stop air movement.
2. **Then add insulation** to the attic floor to slow heat transfer.

This work does not require a permit for most projects, but if the work involves electrical changes (such as replacing old recessed cans that leak air), that work does require a permit and a licensed electrician.

For any contractor working on the structure of your home, the City of Sioux Falls requires a Residential Building Contractor's License for work on one and two family homes within city limits, and the City does not accept licenses from other states. You can verify any contractor at siouxfalls.gov/licensed-contractors.

Solving the attic problem is the one change that makes every Sioux Falls winter easier: fewer ice dams, lower heating bills, and gutters that actually drain instead of turning into an ice trough by February.
