When your furnace won't turn on in the middle of a Sioux Falls January, the instinct is to call for help immediately. That is sometimes the right move. But a surprising number of service calls turn out to be something the homeowner could have resolved in five minutes without paying a diagnostic fee.

This checklist walks you through every safe check you can do yourself, what the results mean, and exactly when to stop and call a professional. For the full picture of heating your home here, see [Heating and Cooling in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/hvac-guide-sioux-falls).

## Start Here: The Thermostat

The thermostat is the most common reason a furnace won't turn on, and it takes about two minutes to check.

First, confirm the mode is set to HEAT, not COOL or OFF. Then set the temperature at least five degrees above whatever the room actually reads right now. If nothing happens within a minute or two, the thermostat may not be sending a call for heat.

Check the batteries. Many digital thermostats run on AA or AAA batteries, and low batteries can cause erratic behavior or a blank screen. Replace them even if the display looks fine.

If you have a smart thermostat, open the app and confirm it has a live connection to your home Wi-Fi. A lost connection can prevent it from responding to any setting you change manually. Some models have a "hold" or "vacation" mode that overrides the schedule without being obvious about it.

- Mode set to HEAT
- Set point at least 5 degrees above room temp
- Batteries replaced
- Smart thermostat connected and not in hold or vacation mode

For more detail on thermostat settings specific to Sioux Falls winters, [Thermostat Settings for a Sioux Falls Winter](/guides/thermostat-settings-winter-sioux-falls) is worth reading next.

## Check the Filter, the Breaker, and the Power Switch

A clogged filter is one of the most common causes of a furnace shutting itself off. Modern furnaces have a high-limit safety switch that cuts the system when airflow gets dangerously restricted. If the filter is gray and matted, replace it before you do anything else.

Sioux Falls winters are hard on filters. The furnace runs long hours in sustained cold, and if you have pets or recently had any remodeling work done, you may need to change the filter more often than the package suggests. See [Furnace Filters, Which One and How Often](/guides/furnace-filter-guide) for guidance on ratings and timing.

After the filter, go to your electrical panel and find the breaker labeled for the furnace or air handler. It should be fully in the ON position. If it is tripped (usually sitting in the middle, not snapped to either side), flip it fully to OFF and then back to ON. If it trips again right away, stop and call an electrician. That is not a furnace problem.

There is also a physical power switch on or near the furnace itself. It looks exactly like a regular light switch and is sometimes on the wall nearby or on the unit's side. It gets bumped off more often than you would expect. Make sure it is in the ON position.

- Replace a dirty filter before anything else
- Check the breaker panel for a tripped breaker
- Reset the breaker once only (if it trips again, call)
- Confirm the furnace power switch is ON

## Check the Furnace Door, the Gas, and the Pilot or Igniter

Most furnaces have a safety switch built into the access panel door. If the door is not fully latched, the furnace will not run. Remove the panel, then push it back until you feel it click into place.

If your furnace runs on natural gas, confirm that the gas supply valve is open. The valve is usually on the gas line running into the furnace and is a lever or knob. When the lever is parallel to the pipe, gas is flowing. When it is perpendicular, it is closed. Only check this if you can reach it safely and you are certain which valve it is.

Older furnaces have a standing pilot light. If it is out, the furnace manual will have relighting instructions, usually printed on the inside of the access panel. Newer furnaces use an electronic igniter that you cannot relight yourself. If you hear clicking but no ignition, the igniter may need professional attention.

If you smell gas at any point, do not try to relight anything. Leave the house, leave the door open, and call your gas utility from outside or from a neighbor's home.

## Read the Blinking Light Codes

If the furnace still won't turn on after the checks above, look for a small LED on the control board inside the access panel. Most modern furnaces display a blinking fault code when something has gone wrong.

Count the blinks. A pattern of two blinks, a pause, then two more blinks is different from three-and-one, and each pattern means something specific. The legend for those codes is usually printed on a sticker inside the access panel door or in the owner's manual.

Common codes point to things like a pressure switch fault, a flame sensor that needs cleaning, or a blocked condensate drain. Some of these have easy fixes; others require a technician.

Write the code down before you close the panel. When you call for service, giving the technician the exact fault code saves time and helps them arrive with the right parts.

- Look for a blinking LED on the control board
- Count the blink pattern precisely
- Find the code legend on the inside of the access panel
- Write it down to give to the technician

## The Condensate Drain Line

High-efficiency furnaces (the kind with two PVC vent pipes going through the wall instead of a metal flue going up through the roof) produce condensation as they run. That water drains through a small plastic line, and if the line clogs or the drain pan fills up, the furnace shuts itself off as a safety measure.

Find the condensate drain line, usually a white or gray PVC tube running to a floor drain, utility sink, or condensate pump. If the line is visibly clogged with slime or debris, or if the pump reservoir is full and not draining, that may be your problem. Clearing the line is sometimes as simple as flushing it with water, but if the pump itself has failed, it needs to be replaced.

This is more common in spring and fall when the furnace cycles on and off frequently, but it can happen in winter too. If you have ever noticed water pooling near your furnace, a clogged condensate line is worth checking every year.

## Carbon Monoxide Warning Signs: Stop and Get Out

A furnace that is malfunctioning can produce carbon monoxide (CO). CO is colorless and odorless, and it is dangerous fast.

Do not continue troubleshooting if you or anyone in the house has unexplained headaches, dizziness, nausea, or flu-like symptoms that seem to go away when you leave the building. Those are CO warning signs.

If a CO detector is alarming, leave the house immediately, leave the door open behind you, and call 911 from outside. Do not go back in to grab anything.

Sioux Falls homes should have CO detectors on every level, including near sleeping areas. If yours are more than five to seven years old or have never been tested, replace them. For a full explanation of how CO happens and what to do, [Carbon Monoxide and Your Furnace, What Every Homeowner Should Know](/guides/carbon-monoxide-safety-furnace) covers it in detail.

## When to Stop and Call, and What to Tell the Technician

Stop the DIY checks and call a professional if any of these are true:

- You smell gas
- A CO detector is alarming
- The breaker trips a second time after you reset it
- The furnace runs briefly then shuts off repeatedly (short cycling)
- You hear banging, grinding, or screeching
- You see visible damage, scorching, or melted components
- The fault code points to a heat exchanger issue (cracks in the heat exchanger are a CO risk)

When you call, tell the technician exactly what you checked and what you found. Mention the fault code if you have one. Tell them when the problem started and whether anything unusual happened before it stopped working (a power outage, a very cold night, recent filter change). That information helps them diagnose faster and may mean they arrive with the right part on the first visit.

If the technician quotes a large repair, it is completely reasonable to ask for the specific part number, the labor breakdown, and time to get a second opinion. Sioux Falls homeowners have saved significant money by doing exactly that.
