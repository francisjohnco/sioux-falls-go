Buying a home in Sioux Falls means buying whatever is on top of it, and roofs here take a beating. The humid continental climate brings hard freezes, freeze-thaw cycles through spring, and hail seasons that insurance companies watch closely. A general home inspection gives you a starting point, but it rarely tells you everything you need to know before signing closing documents.

For a full picture of how roofing works in this market, start with [Roof Repair and Replacement in Sioux Falls, the Complete Guide](/guides/roof-repair-replacement-sioux-falls). This article focuses specifically on what to do before you buy.

## Why a General Home Inspection Is Not Enough

A licensed home inspector covers a lot of ground in two to three hours. The roof is one line item among dozens, and most inspectors view it from the ground or from the edge of a ladder.

They are looking for obvious failures, not for the kind of detail a roofer brings. They may note missing shingles or visible sagging, but they typically will not get on the roof, check flashing closely, or assess whether the remaining life is two years or twelve.

A roofer doing a dedicated roof inspection before buying a house will walk the surface, test flashing at the chimney and skylights, probe for soft decking, check the valleys, and look at the ventilation. That is a meaningfully different scope than what a general inspection delivers.

The two inspections complement each other. Get both, not one or the other.

## What a Roofer Should Examine on a Pre-Purchase Inspection

Ask the roofer to give you a written report, not just a verbal summary. Here is what a thorough inspection should cover:

**On the roof surface:**
- Shingle condition: curling, cracking, missing tabs, blistering
- Granule loss (significant loss means the shingles are near the end)
- Hail bruising (soft spots in the shingle surface, often easier to feel than see)
- Number of layers (Sioux Falls allows up to two layers before a full tear-off is required at next replacement)
- Ridge caps and hip shingles, which wear faster than field shingles

**At transitions and penetrations:**
- Flashing at the chimney, vents, skylights, and any walls the roof meets
- Valley condition (open or closed metal valleys versus woven shingles)
- Pipe boot collars around plumbing vents

**In the attic:**
- Soft or stained decking boards, which signal past or ongoing leaks
- Daylight visible through the deck (a real problem)
- Attic ventilation: intake at soffits, exhaust at the ridge or vents
- Insulation condition, which affects ice dam risk in winter

Poor attic ventilation is one of the most overlooked factors in shingle lifespan. If you want to understand how much it matters, [Attic Ventilation and Your Roof's Lifespan](/guides/attic-ventilation-roof-lifespan) explains the relationship in plain terms.

## Questions to Ask the Seller Before Closing

The seller's disclosure form is a legal document, but it asks broad questions. You should ask more specific ones, in writing through your agent.

**Age and history:**
- How old is the roof, and what is the shingle brand and style?
- Has any portion of the roof been replaced separately? If so, when and why?
- Are there records of any roof permits pulled through the City of Sioux Falls?

Permit records matter more than most buyers realize. According to the City of Sioux Falls, shingle work on a home requires a building permit, and the contractor performing it must hold a Residential Roofing and Repair Contractor license or a broader Residential Building Contractor license. If a previous owner had work done without a permit, that work was not inspected, and you have no independent verification it was done correctly. You can call Building Services licensing at 605-367-8670 to check whether permits were pulled for a specific address.

**Insurance claims:**
- Has the roof been subject to any insurance claims? If so, for what and when?
- Was the full scope of work completed, or was only part of the claim addressed?
- Did insurance pay actual cash value or replacement cost?

A home with an older roof and a recent hail claim on record may already have an insurer flagging it. Some insurance companies decline to cover roofs beyond a certain age or condition, or they limit coverage to actual cash value instead of full replacement. That changes your cost exposure as the new owner.

**Repairs and contractors:**
- Who did the work, and do you have any receipts or warranties?
- Were the contractors licensed with the city?

A roofer's workmanship warranty often does not transfer automatically to a new owner, though some manufacturers' shingle warranties do transfer if you register within a set window. Ask what warranties exist and read the actual terms. [Roof Warranties Explained, Manufacturer vs Workmanship](/guides/roof-warranty-explained) walks through how both types work and what to ask for in writing.

## How to Use the Inspection in Negotiating

A written roofer's report gives you something concrete to bring to the negotiation table. A verbal "looks okay" does not.

If the inspection finds problems, you have a few options:

- **Ask the seller to repair before closing.** This works best when the issue is clear and specific, like failed flashing or a handful of missing shingles. Be cautious here: sellers may use the cheapest fix available, and you will not always know what quality of work was done before you close.
- **Ask for a price reduction.** This is often cleaner. You get the credit, you choose the contractor, and you control the work. Use the roofer's written estimate as your basis, not a rough guess.
- **Ask the seller to provide a roofing allowance at closing.** Similar to a price reduction but sometimes structured differently depending on your financing.
- **Walk away if the numbers do not work.** A roof replacement is a significant expense. If the seller will not negotiate and the roof is near end of life, that cost is yours the moment you close.

One thing worth knowing: if you are buying in a neighborhood with older housing stock, like many homes in the All Saints or McKennan Park areas, you are more likely to encounter roofs with multiple layers, older flashing systems, or low-slope sections over additions. Those are not automatically deal-breakers, but they affect the cost conversation.

## What to Watch for If the Roof Is Older or Previously Repaired

Sioux Falls gets plenty of snow every winter, plus spring thaw cycles and summer hail. A roof that has been through ten or fifteen years of that is a different animal than a newer one.

Signs that should prompt extra scrutiny:

- Mismatched shingles (indicates patchwork repairs over time)
- Granule buildup in gutters (normal some, heavy buildup means shingles are shedding)
- Fascia or soffit damage near the gutters (can mean chronic ice dam or ventilation problems)
- Staining on interior ceilings, even if painted over

Do not let a fresh coat of paint inside the house reassure you if the roof is old. Ask the roofer to check the attic decking directly.

Also ask whether the home has a low-slope section, which is common on additions, garages, and ranch-style homes. Low-slope systems like tar and gravel or EPDM membrane require different contractors and have different lifespans than sloped shingle roofs. Fewer roofers in the area will quote that work, which affects both repair timelines and costs.

If you want honest answers rather than reassurance, tell the roofer that upfront. A good inspector will tell you what the roof has left in it, not just whether it passed a visual scan.
