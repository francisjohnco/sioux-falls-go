Choosing new siding in Sioux Falls is not the same decision it would be in Phoenix or Atlanta. Our climate throws deep cold, brutal freeze-thaw cycles, hail seasons, and humid summers at every exterior surface. The three materials most homeowners are comparing right now are vinyl, fiber cement, and engineered wood, and each has a genuinely different personality in this climate. Before you read this comparison, the [New Siding in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/siding-guide-sioux-falls) covers the full process from permits to picking a contractor, so this article focuses narrowly on how the materials themselves perform here.

## How Each Material Is Built

Understanding what these materials are made of explains most of their differences in performance, upkeep, and appearance.

**Vinyl** is extruded PVC plastic, hollow or foam-filled. It is light, flexible at room temperature, and color runs all the way through, so small scratches are less visible than on painted products.

**Fiber cement** is a mixture of Portland cement, sand, and cellulose fiber, pressed into panels or planks. It is dense, heavy, and typically arrives pre-primed or primed and pre-painted, though it must be repainted on a cycle.

**Engineered wood** (sometimes called composite wood siding) uses wood strands or fibers bonded with resin and coated with a factory finish. It is lighter than fiber cement but heavier than vinyl, and it is designed to look more like natural wood grain than either of the other two.

## Performance in a Sioux Falls Winter and Spring

This is where the comparison gets local fast. Sioux Falls averages around 38 inches of snow per year, and the freeze-thaw cycle between January and April puts real stress on exterior materials.

Vinyl becomes brittle in deep cold. A hard impact, like a piece of ice falling from a roofline, can crack a panel that would have dented or dinged in warmer weather. Panels also expand and contract significantly between seasons, which is why proper installation with the right nail slot clearance matters enormously here. Ask any contractor you interview whether they adjust fastening technique for cold-climate installs.

Fiber cement handles temperature swings better than vinyl does. Cement does not expand and contract at the same rate as plastic, so panels stay tight longer. The tradeoff is that fiber cement is a painted product, and paint can crack at seams or joints if caulking fails. In a wet spring thaw, moisture can wick into unpainted cut ends. A properly installed, fully painted and caulked fiber cement job holds up very well here.

Engineered wood sits between the two. The factory finish is designed to flex slightly, and the material does not have vinyl's cold-brittleness problem. However, engineered wood is more sensitive to sustained moisture than either vinyl or fiber cement, so installation details around windows, trim, and low-clearance areas matter more.

## Hail and Wind Resistance

Sioux Falls homeowners know hail season well. Many local reviews from homeowners who have dealt with siding contractors mention needing to replace siding, roofing, gutters, and window wells all from the same storm.

Vinyl is the most vulnerable of the three to hail. Hail damage on vinyl typically shows as cracks, holes, or spiderweb fracturing, and it is usually not repairable. Individual panels can be replaced, but matching color and profile on older siding is a known challenge. The [Matching New Siding to Old After a Repair](/guides/matching-siding-colors) guide walks through why that gets complicated.

Fiber cement handles hail better. The dense cement composition resists cracking from most hail impacts that would destroy a vinyl panel. After a significant storm you may see surface paint damage or small chips at impact points, but the structural integrity of the panel often survives.

Engineered wood falls somewhere in the middle. It is more impact resistant than vinyl in cold weather, but a severe hailstorm can still damage the factory finish or the substrate, which allows moisture in if not addressed quickly. If you are in a neighborhood that has had repeat hail events, fiber cement is the more durable long-term choice for that specific threat.

Wind resistance is similar across all three when properly installed with the right fasteners and flashing. The [What Goes Under Your Siding and Why It Matters](/guides/what-goes-under-siding) guide explains how the layer beneath the siding affects how well any material holds up in high wind.

## Maintenance, Painting, and Long-Term Upkeep

This is one of the biggest practical differences between the three materials.

**Vinyl** requires almost no maintenance beyond cleaning. It never needs painting. Mildew can build up in shaded areas, but a gentle wash handles it. The color does fade over decades, and faded panels on only part of a house are hard to match, but the upkeep burden year to year is genuinely low.

**Fiber cement** must be repainted on a schedule. The factory paint (or the paint applied after a primed install) will eventually chalk, fade, or peel. How often depends on sun exposure, the paint quality used, and how well the original job was done. When you get quotes, ask what kind of paint finish is included and what the expected repaint cycle is.

**Engineered wood** comes with a factory-applied finish that is typically a solid color coat. It resists moisture better than raw wood, but the finish does need to be monitored at seams, joints, and cut edges. Repainting is possible but should be done with products compatible with the original finish. Ask your contractor about maintenance expectations in writing before you sign.

For a full seasonal maintenance routine, the [Siding Care Through a Sioux Falls Year](/guides/siding-cleaning-maintenance) guide breaks down what to check and when.

## Appearance, Curb Appeal, and Resale

This is partly subjective, but there are real differences worth knowing.

Vinyl has improved significantly over the past two decades. Thicker panels with deeper profiles and realistic wood-grain embossing look better than the flat vinyl of earlier generations. That said, most design-conscious buyers and appraisers still recognize vinyl when they see it. In established neighborhoods like McKennan Park or All Saints, where many homes have distinct architectural character, vinyl may feel out of place depending on the profile and color chosen.

Fiber cement takes paint well and can be installed as wide planks, narrow clapboard, shakes, or vertical panels. It offers more design flexibility than vinyl and is widely accepted as a premium material by buyers and appraisers in most markets.

Engineered wood has the most convincing natural wood appearance of the three. The texture and depth are closer to real wood than either vinyl or fiber cement, which matters in historic or design-forward neighborhoods. It is also lighter than fiber cement, which can simplify installation on certain wall assemblies.

On resale, fiber cement and engineered wood generally signal a quality exterior in ways that standard vinyl does not, though any well-maintained siding is better than any neglected siding. If resale is a primary driver for you, ask your real estate agent what buyers in your specific neighborhood have responded to in recent sales.

## The Permit Reality for Any of These Materials

Regardless of which material you choose, re-siding a Sioux Falls home requires a building permit. The City of Sioux Falls confirmed in its Building Permit FAQs that re-siding is a permitted project. The contractor you hire must hold a City of Sioux Falls Residential Roofing and Repair Contractor license (the license category created in 2024 that covers re-siding), and the City does not accept out-of-state licenses. You can verify any contractor on the currently licensed list at siouxfalls.gov/licensed-contractors before you sign anything. The [Siding Permits and Licensed Contractors in Sioux Falls](/guides/siding-permits-licensed-contractors) guide has the full detail on what to check.
