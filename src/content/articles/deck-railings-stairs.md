Railings and stairs are where most homeowners spend the least time thinking and end up with the most regrets. They affect how the deck looks from the yard, how safe it feels for kids and older guests, and how much maintenance you'll be doing in five years. If you're starting from scratch or replacing an aging deck, [Building a Deck in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/deck-guide-sioux-falls) is the right place to start before diving into railing and stair specifics here.

This article covers the decisions that actually trip people up: material choices, what holds up through freeze-thaw cycles, what to ask your builder, and what corners look like when they're cut.

## Why Railings and Stairs Deserve Their Own Conversation

A lot of homeowners treat railings as an afterthought, something picked from a catalog after the decking is already chosen.

That's a mistake. Railings and stairs are structural, they're the first thing to show wear, and they interact directly with snow, ice, and the temperature swings Sioux Falls sees between December and April.

They also carry more of the visual weight than people expect. Walk up to your house from the street and the railing profile is what you see, not the decking.

If you have a deck that's been around for 20 years, the railings and stairs are likely the first thing showing fatigue, even if the boards still look okay. [Is Your Old Deck Safe? A Spring Checklist](/guides/old-deck-safety-check) walks through what to look for before you decide what to fix or replace.

## Railing Materials and How They Handle Sioux Falls Winters

You have four main options, and each one behaves differently once temperatures drop.

**Pressure-treated wood**

This is the most common choice and the most affordable to install. It holds up reasonably well if it's sealed and maintained, but it moves with moisture and temperature. Posts can crack, rails can cup, and horizontal surfaces collect snow and hold water longer than you want.

**Composite railings**

Composite railing systems (posts, balusters, and top cap all in composite) are low maintenance and don't rot. The trade-off is cost and the fact that composite expands and contracts more than wood in temperature swings. A good installer accounts for this in how they fasten components. Ask your builder how they handle expansion gaps.

**Aluminum railings**

Aluminum is the most freeze-thaw stable of the common options. It doesn't rot, doesn't need sealing, and holds its shape year after year. It tends to look sleek and modern, which works well on some homes and feels out of place on others. It's also worth asking about powder-coat quality since cheap finishes can chip over time.

**Glass panel railings**

Glass infill panels (usually tempered) are popular for decks with a view or on upper levels where you don't want the railing to block sightlines. They require more cleaning and can show condensation and water spots more than other options. Snow and ice can put stress on the hardware that holds the panels, so ask your installer specifically about how the hardware is rated for cold weather.

A quick comparison to scan:

- Wood: lower upfront cost, needs maintenance, moves with moisture
- Composite: low maintenance, higher cost, needs expansion gaps accounted for
- Aluminum: very stable in freeze-thaw, modern look, check powder-coat quality
- Glass: great views, more cleaning, ask about cold-weather hardware ratings

## Deck Stairs That Actually Work in Winter

Stairs are the most used and most dangerous part of a deck in winter, and they're often the least carefully designed.

**Width and rise**

Wider stairs feel safer and are easier to navigate when carrying groceries or helping someone with limited mobility. The specific dimensions are set by code, so your builder will handle that, but ask to see the stair design before it's built if you have strong preferences about width or the number of steps.

**Stringer material**

The stringers are the angled side supports that carry the load of the treads. Pressure-treated wood is standard. Rot at the base of stringers, where they meet the ground or a landing pad, is one of the more common failure points on older decks. Ask your builder how the stringers will be terminated at the bottom.

**Tread material**

Composite treads are popular because they resist rot and don't splinter, but surface texture matters a lot on stairs. Smooth composite can be genuinely slippery when wet or icy. Ask specifically about the slip resistance rating of whatever tread product your builder plans to use.

Wood treads are rougher by nature and easier to add grip tape or strips to. If you go with wood, ask about the gap spacing between boards since debris and ice can pack in and become a hazard.

**Stringers and footings**

If your stairs are long, the bottom of the stringer structure needs a footing. According to City of Sioux Falls Building Services, footings must reach below the local frost line to avoid frost heave. City Building Services is the right place to confirm the required depth for your specific project, so ask your builder what depth they plan to use and have them confirm it with the permit process. The guide on [Deck Footings and Frost Heave](/guides/deck-footings-frost) goes deeper on why this matters.

**Stair railings**

Stair railings have to work at an angle, which means the attachment points carry different loads than a flat deck railing. Ask your builder how the posts are attached at the stringer and at the deck frame. Posts lag-bolted through the stringer are stronger than posts surface-mounted to the outside face of the stringer.

## What to Ask Your Builder About Railings and Stairs

A lot of problems come from vague quotes. If a quote just says "railings included," you don't know what you're getting.

Ask these before you sign anything:

- What is the railing system (brand or product line, material, post size)?
- How are posts attached (through the deck frame, to the rim joist, surface-mounted)?
- How are the stair stringers terminated at the bottom, and will there be a footing?
- What is the slip resistance rating of the stair treads?
- What maintenance will the railing system require in years one through five?
- Is the railing and stair work included in the permit being pulled for the deck?

That last question matters. According to the City of Sioux Falls, decks require a building permit, and railings and stairs are part of that permitted scope. The work should be inspected as part of the permit process, not treated as a separate add-on that skips inspection. The contractor pulling the permit must hold a valid Residential Building Contractor's License from the City of Sioux Falls. The City does not accept out-of-state licenses, and you can verify any contractor's license at siouxfalls.gov/licensed-contractors before you sign.

## Common Railing and Stair Mistakes to Avoid

These are the patterns that come up in projects that go wrong.

- Posts surface-mounted to the outside of the frame with lag bolts look fine but flex more than posts that run down inside the frame or are through-bolted with blocking. Ask how yours will be done.
- Skipping the stair footing on longer runs because it adds cost. Frost heave will push the stringer up and the stair will pull away from the deck over a few winters.
- Choosing a railing system based on the catalog photo and not asking about how the parts connect. Some systems have poor-quality connectors that corrode or loosen quickly.
- Not accounting for how railings drain. Horizontal surfaces on posts or cap rails hold standing water, which accelerates rot in wood and can loosen hardware over time. Ask if the cap rail design sheds water.
- Matching your decking material but not checking if the railing system is from the same manufacturer. Mixing brands can create warranty gaps.
