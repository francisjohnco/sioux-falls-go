A furnace that quits on a calm October afternoon is an inconvenience. A furnace out in winter during a Sioux Falls cold snap, when overnight lows can drop below zero and wind chills push further, is a genuine no heat emergency. The good news is that if you move through a short checklist in the right order, you can protect your home and your family while you wait for a technician. For a broader look at how heating and cooling works here year-round, see [Heating and Cooling in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/hvac-guide-sioux-falls).

## First, Rule Out the Simple Stuff Before You Call

Before you assume the worst, go through the basics. A surprising number of "furnace out" calls turn out to be a tripped breaker, a flipped power switch, a clogged filter, or a thermostat set to "on" instead of "auto."

Check these in order:

- Thermostat: Is it set to heat, and is the temperature set above what the room actually reads?
- Power switch: The furnace has a wall switch nearby that looks like a light switch. It gets bumped off more often than you'd think.
- Breaker: Look for a tripped breaker in your panel labeled "furnace" or "air handler."
- Filter: A filter so clogged it shuts the furnace down on high-limit is a real thing. Pull it and check.
- Condensate line: High-efficiency furnaces will lock out if the condensate drain is blocked.

For a step-by-step walkthrough of each of these checks, including what the blinking error codes on your furnace mean, see [Furnace Not Working? What to Check Before You Call](/guides/furnace-not-working-sioux-falls). That article also covers exactly when to stop checking and pick up the phone.

## Protect Your Pipes While You Wait

Sioux Falls sits on expansive clay soil and the city's frost line runs 42 inches deep. That means water supply and drain lines inside exterior walls and in crawl spaces are genuinely vulnerable when a house gets cold.

If the furnace is not running and outdoor temps are at or near freezing, protecting your pipes is the first practical job while you wait for a technician.

- Open cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls so warmer interior air can reach the pipes.
- Let cold-water faucets drip slowly, especially on the side of the house facing the wind.
- Know where your main water shutoff is. If a pipe bursts, you want to find that valve in five seconds, not five minutes.
- If the house will be unoccupied for more than a few hours in genuinely cold conditions, consider shutting off the main and draining the lines.

The indoor temperature that triggers real pipe risk depends on how well your walls are insulated and where the pipes run, but a generally cited threshold is around 55 degrees Fahrenheit inside. If the house is approaching that, treating pipe protection as urgent is not an overreaction.

## Safe Space Heater Use in a Cold House

A portable space heater can keep one room livable while you wait for the furnace to come back. It can also start a fire or cause a carbon monoxide problem if used wrong. Here is what actually matters.

Use only electric space heaters, never propane or kerosene indoors. Combustion heaters produce CO and require ventilation that most rooms cannot safely provide.

For electric heaters:

- Plug directly into a wall outlet, never an extension cord or a power strip.
- Keep at least three feet clear on all sides.
- Never leave one running in a room where no one is awake and present.
- Do not use an oven or gas range to heat the house. Beyond the fire risk, it produces carbon monoxide.

One room strategy: pick the room with the best insulation, close the door, and heat only that space. Trying to warm a whole house with space heaters overloads circuits and creates a false sense of safety that leads to risky shortcuts.

## Carbon Monoxide: The Risk You Cannot See

A cracked heat exchanger is one of the more common reasons a furnace suddenly quits, and it is also a carbon monoxide risk. CO is colorless and odorless. The symptoms, headache, dizziness, nausea, and confusion, feel like the flu.

If your CO detector goes off, or if people in the house are feeling symptoms, get everyone outside immediately and call 911. Do not go back inside to grab things. Do not try to find the source yourself.

If you do not have a CO detector on every level of your home near sleeping areas, that is worth fixing before the next heating season. For a full explanation of how CO happens, where to put detectors, and what a technician checks for, see [Carbon Monoxide and Your Furnace, What Every Homeowner Should Know](/guides/carbon-monoxide-safety-furnace).

A furnace that is short-cycling, producing a strange smell, or showing a yellow or flickering burner flame (it should be blue) deserves a technician's eyes before you run it again, even after a reset seems to bring it back.

## Calling for Emergency HVAC Service in Sioux Falls

When you decide it is time to call, a few things will make that call go better.

**What to have ready when you call:**

- Brand and model of your furnace (usually on a sticker inside the front panel door)
- How old the furnace is, if you know
- What it is doing, or not doing (no ignition, blower runs but no heat, error code blinking)
- Whether you have a CO detector and whether it has alarmed

**Questions to ask on the phone:**

- Do you have a technician available today, or is this a next-available dispatch?
- Is there an after-hours or emergency service fee, and how much is it?
- Is the diagnostic fee applied toward the repair if I proceed?
- Are parts typically on the truck, or could this be a two-visit repair?

After-hours fees are real and vary. Many companies charge a separate trip fee or premium rate for evenings, weekends, and holidays. Ask for the total estimated cost of the visit before anyone gets dispatched, not just the hourly rate.

If you receive a repair quote on that first visit that feels high, it is completely reasonable to ask for a written breakdown of the part and labor cost separately. Getting a second opinion on a large repair is normal, and homeowners in Sioux Falls have reported saving significantly by doing exactly that. See [When to Get a Second Opinion on an HVAC Repair](/guides/hvac-second-opinion) for how to handle that conversation without slowing things down in a crisis.

**Verifying a contractor:** You can confirm that an HVAC company holds a current mechanical contractor license through the City of Sioux Falls at 605-367-8672 or on the city's Currently Licensed Contractors list online, according to the City of Sioux Falls Building Permit FAQs. Any mechanical work on your furnace that involves a permit should be done by a licensed mechanical contractor, not a handyman.

## After the Furnace Is Running Again

Once heat is restored, there are a few things worth doing before you close the door on the whole experience.

If the technician found a cracked heat exchanger, a failed igniter, or a failing inducer motor, ask whether the age and repair history of the furnace make repair the right call or whether replacement makes more sense. A furnace over 15 to 18 years old that just needed a significant repair is worth evaluating seriously before the next cold snap puts you back in the same spot.

If the furnace is staying, ask the technician to show you the service record and explain what was found. Ask whether a tune-up was performed as part of the visit or whether one is recommended before next season.

And if this was a close call with CO or a cracked heat exchanger, make sure you have working detectors on every level before the next cold night.
