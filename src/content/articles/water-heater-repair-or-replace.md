A water heater that stops working is one of those problems that feels urgent at every hour, including two in the morning. Sioux Falls homeowners have called plumbers in the middle of the night for exactly that reason, and the stress of the moment can push you toward a fast decision you later regret. Before you agree to a full replacement, it helps to know what actually signals "done" versus "fixable." This article is part of a broader series on home plumbing. For the full picture, start with [Plumbing in a Sioux Falls Home, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/plumbing-guide-sioux-falls).

## What Sioux Falls Hard Water Does to Your Water Heater

Water hardness matters more here than in most parts of the country.

According to the City of Sioux Falls 2020 finished water quality report, total hardness runs about 15 grains per gallon for water treated at the City's own plant and about 10 grains per gallon for Lewis and Clark water. Which source your home gets can vary by address.

That hardness means minerals accumulate inside your tank faster than they would in a soft-water city.

The result is a layer of sediment on the bottom of the tank. You may hear it as popping or rumbling during heating cycles, which many homeowners mistake for a sign the heater is dying.

Sediment insulates the burner from the water, forcing the unit to work harder, which shortens its life and raises your energy bill. In a tank that is otherwise sound, flushing that sediment can restore performance. For a deeper look at what hard water does to your whole plumbing system, see [Hard Water and Your Sioux Falls Plumbing](/guides/hard-water-plumbing-sioux-falls).

## Signs That Usually Mean Repair, Not Replacement

Not every water heater problem is terminal. Some of the most common failures are component-level and are worth diagnosing before you commit to a new unit.

Signs that often point toward a repair:

- No hot water but the pilot light is out or the igniter has failed
- Lukewarm water from a failed thermostat or a burned-out heating element (on electric units)
- Popping and rumbling sounds with no other symptoms (sediment buildup, often flushable)
- Pressure relief valve dripping once after a pressure spike (the valve itself may need replacement, not the tank)
- A tripped circuit breaker on an electric unit with no other damage

The pattern that comes up repeatedly from local homeowners is being told a water heater is completely dead, only to have a second opinion find a single failed part. That outcome is not unusual. Component failures happen on units of all ages, and a thorough diagnosis is not the same as a visual look and a replacement quote.

If the first technician cannot tell you exactly which component failed and why, that is a reasonable moment to ask for more detail, or to call someone else.

## Signs That Usually Mean Replacement

Some situations genuinely do mean the unit has reached the end of its useful life. Knowing them saves you from paying for a repair that buys only a few more months.

Signs that point toward replacement:

- The tank is leaking from the bottom or from the body of the tank (not the fittings)
- The unit is more than 10 to 12 years old and having its second or third problem
- Rust-colored water that continues after flushing (indicates internal corrosion)
- The anode rod is completely depleted and the tank wall is already pitting
- Repair cost approaches or exceeds roughly half the cost of a new unit of the same type

A tank leak is the clearest signal. Once the steel tank itself has corroded through, no repair stops it. Rust in the water is the same message from the inside out.

Age matters alongside symptoms. A 6-year-old unit with a failed thermostat is a repair candidate. A 13-year-old unit with the same problem may not be worth fixing, because other components are likely to follow.

## Getting a Second Opinion and Knowing Your Permit Rights

Sioux Falls homeowners surprised by a replacement recommendation have every right to call another licensed plumber before they decide.

According to the City of Sioux Falls, all contractors doing plumbing work within city limits are required to hold a City license, and the City does not accept licenses from other states. You can verify any plumber by checking the Currently Licensed Contractors list at siouxfalls.gov/licensed-contractors before anyone starts work.

As noted by KELO News quoting the City of Sioux Falls permitting support manager, water heater change-outs are among the projects that require a city permit, and homeowners sometimes skip that step. A permit for a replacement is not optional. It triggers an inspection that protects you, confirms the work was done correctly, and matters when you sell the house.

If you own and live in a single-family home, the City does allow you to pull a homeowner plumbing permit and do the work yourself, but you are responsible for code compliance and passing inspection. For most people, hiring a licensed plumber who pulls the permit as part of the job is the more straightforward path. See [How to Choose a Plumber in Sioux Falls](/guides/how-to-choose-a-plumber-in-sioux-falls) for what to ask before you hire anyone.

Do not let any contractor start a replacement without confirming who is pulling the permit. If they tell you a permit is not needed for a swap, that is a red flag.

## Questions to Ask Before You Decide

Whether you are leaning toward repair or replacement, the conversation with your plumber should produce clear answers, not just a recommendation.

Ask these questions:

- What specific component failed, and how did you diagnose it?
- If we repair it, what is the realistic remaining life of the tank?
- Is sediment buildup a factor, and would flushing help?
- What type of replacement would you recommend and why (tank, tankless, heat pump)?
- Who pulls the permit for a replacement, and is that included in your quote?
- Do you carry a current City of Sioux Falls plumbing contractor license?

A plumber who explains the problem and lays out your options before asking you to sign anything is doing the job right. Homeowners who have been through this process more than once consistently say that explanation, not just speed, is what they valued most.
