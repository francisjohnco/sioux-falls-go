A deck that looked perfectly level last fall can show up tilted, cracked, or pulling away from the house come spring. In Sioux Falls, that is usually frost heave at work. Understanding why footings fail here, and what warning signs to watch for, can save you from a much bigger repair bill later.

For a full picture of how a deck project comes together, start with [Building a Deck in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/deck-guide-sioux-falls).

## Why Deck Footings Matter So Much in Sioux Falls

Footings are the concrete columns that hold your deck posts off the ground and transfer the weight of the structure down into stable soil.

If a footing sits too shallow, frozen ground can grab it and push it upward. That is frost heave, and it does not need to push far to do real damage.

Sioux Falls sits on expansive clay soil in many neighborhoods, including older areas like McKennan Park and All Saints. Clay holds moisture and shifts more dramatically through freeze-thaw cycles than sandy or loamy soils do.

That combination of cold winters and clay soil is exactly why the City of Sioux Falls Building Services sets a required footing depth for residential decks. According to City guidance, footings must reach below the local frost line to prevent heave. For the exact required depth, confirm directly with City Building Services before any work begins, because the number matters and you do not want to guess.

A contractor who pours footings at the wrong depth is not saving you money. They are setting up a problem you will deal with years from now.

## What Frost Heave Actually Does to a Deck

Heave does not always look dramatic. Sometimes it is subtle enough that homeowners do not connect the symptom to the cause.

Watch for these signs each spring:

- Posts that look bowed, tilted, or no longer plumb
- Gaps opening between the deck boards and the house
- Railings that wobble or lean when they did not before
- Stair stringers that have lifted off their pads
- Deck boards that visibly slope toward or away from the house

A single lifted footing changes the load path through the whole frame. Joists that were level now bear weight at an angle, hardware that was tight now has gaps, and the ledger connection to the house can be stressed in ways you cannot see from the surface.

If you have a deck that is 15 to 20 years old and has never had its footings inspected, a tilted post is not just cosmetic. Run through [Is Your Old Deck Safe? A Spring Checklist](/guides/old-deck-safety-check) to understand what else to look for before you decide whether to repair or replace.

## What Causes a Footing to Fail

The most common reasons footings heave or settle in Sioux Falls yards:

- **Poured too shallow.** The footing never reached below the frost line and the frozen ground found it every winter.
- **Too narrow a base.** A footing that does not spread the load wide enough can sink in soft or wet clay.
- **No bell at the bottom.** Some footing designs flare at the base so the soil cannot push the whole column up. Without that, frozen soil can grip the sides and lift.
- **Water pooling around the post.** Poor drainage soaks the soil near the footing, giving frost more water to work with.
- **Original construction before code required deeper footings.** Older decks were sometimes built without a permit or to standards that have since changed.

A deck built without a permit is one of the most common situations Sioux Falls homeowners discover when they go to sell a house or pull a permit for an addition. According to City of Sioux Falls Building Permit FAQs, decks require a building permit. Work done without one, especially footings poured at the wrong depth, may need to be opened up or even removed to pass inspection later.

## How the Fix Usually Works

If you have confirmed heave, the repair approach depends on how bad the damage is and what caused it.

For a footing that has lifted but left the frame intact, a contractor may be able to excavate around the post, deepen or replace the footing, and re-level the structure. This is not a weekend project. It involves temporary bracing, concrete work, and usually a permit and inspection.

If the frame has been racked or the ledger connection has been stressed, the scope grows quickly. That is worth getting assessed honestly before you agree to a price. Ask your contractor in writing what they plan to do with the footing, what depth they will pour to, and whether a permit will be pulled.

According to City of Sioux Falls licensing requirements, all contractors working on residential homes within city limits must hold a Residential Building Contractor's License, and the City does not accept licenses from other states. You can verify any contractor on the Currently Licensed Contractors list at siouxfalls.gov/licensed-contractors before signing anything.

## What to Do Before You Build or Rebuild

If you are planning a new deck or replacing an old one, footings are the right place to start the conversation with your contractor, not an afterthought.

Ask these questions before any concrete is poured:

- What depth are you planning to pour the footings, and how does that meet the City's requirement?
- Are you pulling a building permit? (If not, walk away.)
- What footing design are you using, and does it account for the soil type in my yard?
- How will you handle drainage around the post bases?
- Will there be an inspection of the footings before the frame goes up?

That last point matters more than most homeowners realize. A footing inspection by City Building Services happens before the concrete is covered. Once the frame is built, there is no way to verify what is underneath without tearing things apart.

If you are also thinking about adding a hot tub or significant load to the deck, footings become even more critical. The loads change the whole calculation. [Can Your Deck Hold a Hot Tub?](/guides/hot-tub-on-deck) explains what that conversation with a contractor and possibly a structural engineer should look like.

Footings are invisible once the deck is done. Getting them right is the one part of the job you cannot go back and fix without starting over.
