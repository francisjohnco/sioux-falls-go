Deciding whether to repair or replace a roof is one of the more stressful calls a homeowner makes, especially after a hail storm or a stubborn leak shows up mid-winter. The answer depends on a handful of concrete things you can actually check, not just on what a contractor tells you at the door. This guide walks through each one so you can go into any conversation with a roofer already knowing what questions matter. For the full picture of how repairs, replacements, insurance, and contractors all connect, start with [Roof Repair and Replacement in Sioux Falls, the Complete Guide](/guides/roof-repair-replacement-sioux-falls).

## Age and How Long a Roof Really Lasts in South Dakota

The question of how long a roof lasts in South Dakota is not as simple as the number on the shingle package.

Manufacturers often rate standard three-tab shingles at 20 to 25 years and architectural shingles at 25 to 30 years. But those numbers assume moderate climates.

South Dakota roofs take a harder beating than the warranty assumes:

- Roughly 38 inches of snow per year on average in Sioux Falls
- Freeze-thaw cycles that can run deep into spring, stressing shingles and flashing repeatedly
- Hail seasons that can hit the same roof multiple times over a decade
- Summer heat that accelerates granule loss

A realistic lifespan for a Sioux Falls roof is often toward the lower end of the rated range, especially if the roof has seen multiple hail events without a full inspection in between.

If your roof is 15 years old and you are looking at a repair, the math changes fast. A repair today might cost a fraction of a replacement, but if the rest of the roof has only a few years left, you are spending money to delay the inevitable.

A roof under 10 years old in decent shape is almost always a repair candidate. A roof past 20 years, with other warning signs, is usually a replacement conversation.

## Signs a Repair Is Probably Enough

Not every roofing problem is a replacement. These are the situations where a repair often makes sense:

- **Isolated missing shingles** after a wind event, with the surrounding field in good shape
- **One flashing failure** at a chimney, vent, or valley that is causing a single leak point
- **A few lifted shingles** that have not been creased or torn yet
- **A recent installation** (under 10 years) where the damage is clearly limited to one section

The honest complication is shingle matching. Manufacturers change colors and profiles over time, so a patch repair on a 12-year-old roof may not match the existing shingles closely. That matters mostly for resale. If you plan to stay in the home, a slight color difference is not a reason to replace the whole roof.

One thing homeowners describe repeatedly: getting the run-around on small repairs, because some contractors simply do not want to come out for a minor job. If that happens, ask specifically whether the contractor does repair-only work before scheduling. It is a fair question.

## Signs Replacement Is the Right Call

Some conditions mean a repair will not hold. Watch for:

- **Curling or cupping shingles across a large area**, which signals the shingles are at or near the end of their life
- **Heavy granule loss** showing up in gutters or at downspout outlets, exposing the asphalt mat underneath
- **Soft or spongy spots** when walking the roof, which can indicate rotted decking beneath the shingles
- **Hail bruising on most or all of the roof field**, which looks like dark dents or missing granules scattered evenly across the shingles
- **Repeated leaks in different spots**, especially if previous repairs did not hold
- **Multiple shingle layers already on the roof** (most codes allow two layers maximum before a full tear-off is required)

Hail bruising is worth explaining more. A single bruised shingle here and there may qualify as a repair or a supplement. When bruising covers most of the roof evenly, it usually means the protective granule layer is compromised across the whole surface, and repair is not a realistic solution.

## The Spot-Repair Problem and When to Get a Second Opinion

Homeowners sometimes hire a roofer for a small repair, pay for it, and then see a new leak a season or two later. This is not always a sign of bad work. It can mean the original problem was a symptom of a roof that is failing more broadly.

The pattern tends to go like this:

1. Leak appears, often after a heavy rain or snow melt
2. A roofer patches the visible problem
3. A year or two later, a new leak appears nearby
4. Eventually the homeowner has paid for two or three repairs and still needs a full replacement

If you are already on your second or third repair in the same general area of the roof, that is a good reason to get a full inspection from a different contractor before paying for another patch.

A second opinion costs a roofer's time, and most will charge a small inspection fee or nothing at all for it. Getting one is completely reasonable, especially before a larger repair or when an insurance adjuster and a contractor are giving you different answers about scope.

If a roofer dismisses the idea of a second opinion or pressures you to sign quickly, that is a reason to slow down. The South Dakota Attorney General's consumer protection office specifically warns homeowners not to be pressured into a quick agreement after hailstorms. You can reach their office at 1-800-300-1986.

## What to Check Before Any Repair or Replacement Decision

Before you call a roofer, do a few things from the ground:

- **Check your gutters** for heavy granule buildup, which signals aging shingles
- **Look at the roofline** from the street for visible sagging, which can mean decking or structural problems underneath
- **Check inside the attic** after rain for active moisture, staining, or daylight through the decking
- **Note the age of the roof** as best you can, from permits, the seller's disclosure, or old receipts

Then, when you talk to a roofer, ask for the inspection findings in writing. A contractor who can tell you specifically which sections have problems, and which are still in good shape, is giving you information you can actually use.

Make sure any contractor you hire holds a City of Sioux Falls Residential Roofing and Repair Contractor license. That license requires a $20,000 compliance bond and at least $300,000 in general liability insurance, according to the City of Sioux Falls. You can verify a contractor's license by calling Building Services at 605-367-8670.
