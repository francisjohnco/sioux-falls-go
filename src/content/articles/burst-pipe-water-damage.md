A pipe bursting inside a wall is one of the more disorienting things that can happen in a home. You might hear a pop, notice a wet spot on drywall, or find your floor soaking wet before you even know where the water is coming from.

Sioux Falls winters make this more common than many homeowners expect. The city gets plenty of snow every winter, and freeze-thaw swings can push water in exterior walls or unheated spaces past the point pipes can handle. If this is your situation right now, the [Water Damage in Your Sioux Falls Home, What to Do](/guides/water-damage-guide-sioux-falls) guide has the full picture. This article focuses specifically on burst pipe water damage inside walls, from the first minutes through drying, opening walls, and preventing mold.

## Shut Off the Water First, Then Breathe

The single most important thing you can do in the first two minutes is stop the water.

Go to your main shutoff valve and close it. In most Sioux Falls homes, it is in the basement, near where the water line comes in from the street, often on the north or east side of the foundation.

If you have never located it before, look for a ball valve (lever handle) or gate valve (round wheel) on a copper or steel pipe. Turn a ball valve 90 degrees so the handle is perpendicular to the pipe. Turn a gate valve clockwise until it stops.

If you cannot find the main valve, or if it will not close, call your utility or a plumber immediately. Do not wait. Every minute the water runs, it spreads further inside the wall cavity, into insulation, and down through the floor assembly.

Once the water is off:

- Turn off the electricity to any rooms or circuits near the wet area, at the breaker box, before you touch anything wet.
- Take photos and video of everything visible before you move a single item. This documentation matters for your insurance claim.
- Call your insurance company to report the loss. The South Dakota Division of Insurance advises homeowners to file promptly because policies can set notice deadlines, make temporary repairs to prevent more damage and keep the receipts, and not make permanent repairs before contacting the insurer.

## What Is Happening Inside That Wall Right Now

This is the part most homeowners do not see, and it is why a pipe burst in a wall is more serious than a burst pipe in an open basement.

Water does not stay where the break is. It follows the path of least resistance, which usually means down. It soaks into the wall cavity, saturates insulation (fiberglass or spray foam holds water differently, but both create problems), and runs down the wall framing into the bottom plate, which sits on your subfloor or concrete slab.

If the break is on an upper floor, water will travel into the ceiling of the floor below. If it reaches the basement, you may see it pooling well away from the actual pipe location.

Sioux Falls homes built on the clay-heavy soils common throughout the city often have concrete basements with wood framing built against the foundation walls. When water reaches that framing, it has very little airflow to dry on its own. That is where mold problems tend to start, often inside the wall where you cannot see it.

The window for drying before mold becomes a serious risk is roughly 24 to 48 hours. That timeline is why speed matters more than almost anything else with burst pipe water damage.

## Opening the Wall, When and How

A lot of homeowners want to avoid opening walls if they can. That instinct is understandable, but it usually makes things worse.

The wet drywall and insulation inside the wall cavity have to come out to dry properly. Leaving saturated material inside a closed wall creates the exact conditions mold needs: moisture, organic material, and no airflow. No fan or dehumidifier can dry what it cannot reach.

A restoration crew will use a moisture meter to map how far the water has traveled, both up and down the wall and horizontally. They cut out drywall to those boundaries, not just to where it looks wet on the surface. Wet drywall often looks dry on the outside while the insulation and framing behind it are still saturated.

What you can expect when walls are opened:

- Insulation is removed and discarded if it is wet. It cannot be dried effectively.
- Framing is inspected for signs of damage or early mold growth.
- Drying equipment, air movers and dehumidifiers, is placed inside the open wall cavity to bring moisture levels down to safe readings.
- Moisture readings are taken daily until the framing and subfloor hit the target numbers.

For a detailed look at how the drying process actually works, the article [How Professional Drying Works After Water Damage](/guides/drying-process-explained) explains the equipment, the readings, and why the process takes as many days as it does.

## Mold Prevention After a Pipe Burst in the Wall

If drying is done correctly and fast enough, mold may never become an issue. But if the wall stayed wet for more than a day or two before work started, or if the first attempt at drying was not thorough, mold is a real possibility.

The signs to watch for after a burst pipe and wall repair:

- A musty smell that returns after the work is done.
- Discoloration on new drywall or paint, especially near the original repair area.
- Allergy-like symptoms in family members that seem tied to being in a specific room.

Mold inside a wall cavity after a burst pipe is not always visible until the wall is opened again. This is one reason it is worth asking any restoration contractor how they verify that framing is dry before they close the wall. Ask to see the moisture readings before drywall goes back up.

If you have any concern that mold may already be present, [Mold After Water Damage, Signs and What to Do](/guides/mold-after-water-damage) covers what professional remediation looks like and what containment means in practice.

## Insurance, Repairs, and What Comes Next

A sudden pipe burst is generally treated differently by insurers than a slow, gradual leak, but every policy is different. The South Dakota Division of Insurance advises homeowners to read their policy carefully, photograph all damage, keep a log of every contact with the insurer and contractor, and ask for an itemized explanation of any settlement offer. If you have questions about your rights, the Division of Insurance can be reached at 605-773-3563.

Do not make permanent repairs, meaning closing the wall with new drywall, until the insurer has had a chance to inspect. Temporary repairs, like covering an opening with plastic sheeting to contain dust or block drafts, are appropriate and receipts for those should be kept.

Once drying is complete and the insurer has signed off, the repair work involves putting the wall back: new insulation, new drywall, tape, mud, and paint. If flooring was affected, that gets addressed in the same phase. The article [Rebuilding After Water Damage, What to Expect](/guides/rebuilding-after-water-damage) walks through what that reconstruction phase typically looks like and what questions to ask a contractor before work begins.

A few things to confirm before the wall closes:

- Ask to see the final moisture readings on the framing and subfloor, in writing.
- Confirm that the plumber has pressure-tested the repaired pipe before anything is enclosed.
- Make sure any permit required by City of Sioux Falls Building Services has been pulled for the plumbing repair. South Dakota licenses plumbers at the state level, and permitted work gives you a record and an inspection.
