Winters in Sioux Falls are not gentle. Between the heavy lake-effect-style dumps that roll in fast and the freeze-thaw cycles that turn a cleared driveway into a skating rink by morning, snow removal is not optional for homeowners here. It is also not simple. There are city ordinances, parking alerts, real penalties, and plenty of ways to damage your concrete or lawn if you get the details wrong. This guide pulls everything together so you know what the rules are, what to watch for when you hire help, and how to protect your property from the first flake to the spring thaw.

## The 48-Hour Sidewalk Rule in Sioux Falls

The City of Sioux Falls requires homeowners to clear sidewalks edge to edge, down to the concrete, within 48 hours after snow stops falling. That requirement comes from City Ordinance 96.100 and it applies to corner lots too, including the curb ramps.

A few things homeowners get wrong:

- The clock starts when snow **stops**, not when it starts.
- Edge to edge means the full width of the sidewalk, not a narrow path.
- Corner lot? Both sidewalks and both curb ramps are your responsibility.

The City does not investigate reports filed before the 48-hour mark, so a complaint filed an hour after a storm ends will not go anywhere. But once that window closes, the exposure is real.

According to Dakota News Now as reported by KSOO in 2025, an uncleared sidewalk can bring a $100 penalty. Under City Ordinances 96.103 and 96.104, the City can also hire a snow removal company to do the work and bill the cost directly to the property owner as a special assessment.

Older homeowners or anyone with physical limitations worry about this rule more than anyone. If you cannot safely clear the sidewalk yourself, the solution is to arrange help before a storm, not after. See the full breakdown of timing, corner lot rules, and what happens if the City steps in at [The 48-Hour Sidewalk Rule, Explained](/guides/sidewalk-snow-48-hours).

## Snow Alerts and Where to Park

When the City plans to plow all streets, it declares a snow alert. During a snow alert, vehicles parked on City streets may be ticketed and towed. That is not a maybe. It happens, and the fine plus towing cost adds up fast.

The City runs a Snow Alert Tracker with zone maps and real-time plow progress. Checking it before a storm means you are not scrambling at 10 p.m. to move a car.

A few things to do before snow season:

- Identify where your household vehicles will park during a snow alert (a driveway, a garage, a friend's driveway).
- Sign up for City alerts so you are not caught off guard.
- Check the tracker even on days with light snow, because alerts can be declared for smaller events.

For the full breakdown of how alerts work, what the tracker shows, and how to plan ahead, read [Snow Alerts and Where to Park](/guides/snow-alert-parking).

## Where Snow Can and Cannot Go

This one surprises a lot of homeowners. When you shovel or blow snow, you cannot put it in the street or on the sidewalk. The City ordinance is clear: snow belongs in your yard, not in the right of way.

Beyond the legal side, there are practical reasons to care about snow placement:

- Piles at the end of a driveway can block sight lines when you back out, which is a safety issue.
- Snow piled over a mailbox creates problems for delivery and may bring a complaint from the postal carrier.
- Piles near a fire hydrant can bury it, which is a serious problem for emergency responders.
- Heavy piles along lawn edges can damage grass and landscaping when they melt and refreeze repeatedly.

Planning where snow goes before you start moving it saves a lot of frustration. It also matters in spring, when large piles melt and drain. If the melt runs toward a foundation or a neighbor's property, you may have a bigger problem than a temporary pile. The full guide on placement, sight lines, drainage, and hydrants is at [Where to Put All That Snow](/guides/where-to-put-snow).

## Hiring a Snow Removal Service

Hiring a pro is the right call for a lot of Sioux Falls homeowners, especially those who leave early for work and need the driveway open before 7 a.m., or anyone who physically cannot do the work safely.

The most common frustrations homeowners run into:

- The service comes, but skips the sidewalk.
- After a second storm hits the same day, no one comes back.
- Plow stakes are missing and the plow tears up lawn edges or landscaping.
- Snow piles end up blocking the mailbox or the view at the end of the driveway.

Before you sign anything, ask these questions in writing:

- What is the trigger depth? (Some contracts only kick in at 2 inches, some at 1.)
- What is the guaranteed arrival window?
- Does the contract include sidewalks, or just the driveway?
- What happens after a second or third pass of snow in the same day?
- Does the company carry liability insurance, and will they provide a certificate?
- How is damage to landscaping or concrete handled?

Do not rely on a verbal answer to any of these. Get it in the contract. For a detailed walkthrough of what to ask and what to watch for, read [Hiring a Snow Removal Service](/guides/hiring-snow-removal).

## Per Push or Seasonal Contract?

Once you decide to hire, you will face a choice: pay per visit (per push) or pay a flat seasonal rate. Neither one is automatically better. It depends on how much snow falls and how risk-tolerant you are.

Here is the basic tradeoff:

- **Per push** means you pay each time the crew comes. Light winters cost you less. Heavy winters cost you more.
- **Seasonal** means you pay one flat amount regardless of how many times they show up. Heavy winters are covered. Light winters mean you paid for visits that never happened.

In Sioux Falls, where snowfall can vary significantly from one winter to the next, some homeowners prefer the predictability of a seasonal contract. Others would rather pay for what they actually use.

The details inside each contract type matter as much as the structure. What counts as a "push"? Does the seasonal price include ice treatment or just snow? Is there a cap on visits? Read [Per Push or Seasonal Snow Contract?](/guides/per-push-vs-seasonal) before you commit to either model.

## Protecting Your Concrete and Lawn from Snow Removal Damage

Snow removal that is done wrong or too aggressively can cost you more in spring repairs than you saved on the service. The two most common victims are lawn edges and concrete surfaces.

For lawns and landscaping:

- Mark driveway edges with stakes or driveway markers before the first snow. This is especially important for homeowners in neighborhoods like McKennan Park or All Saints where driveways sit close to mature plantings.
- Ask your contractor whether they use stakes and what their policy is if they damage a lawn edge.
- Document the condition of your driveway edges and landscaping in October with photos. If damage happens, you will have a baseline to reference.

For concrete:

The wrong ice melt product can destroy concrete, especially surfaces that are newer or already showing wear. Rock salt is widely used but it accelerates freeze-thaw damage. Some products are safer for concrete but harder on vegetation or pets. The specific product matters, the application rate matters, and the age of your concrete matters.

New concrete is especially vulnerable. If you have had work done recently, check with the contractor who poured it before applying any chemical treatment. For a full breakdown of product types and what to avoid, read [Ice Melt Without Ruining Your Concrete](/guides/ice-melt-concrete). And if your driveway or sidewalk already has damage from previous winters, [Concrete Work in Sioux Falls](/guides/concrete-guide-sioux-falls) covers what repair looks like.

## Reviewing Your Contract Before You Sign

A snow removal contract that is light on specifics is a contract that will disappoint you at the worst time, usually during the second storm of a busy week. The details that matter most:

- Trigger depth (what amount of snow starts the clock)
- Response window (how many hours after trigger)
- What surfaces are included
- Whether ice treatment is included or billed separately
- What constitutes a "return visit" for multi-day storms
- Damage policy and who pays for what
- Payment schedule and what happens if you need to cancel mid-season

Do not sign until you can answer all of those questions from the contract document itself. [What a Good Snow Removal Contract Says](/guides/snow-removal-contract-checklist) walks through each one with the right questions to ask.

## Helping Neighbors Who Cannot Shovel

The City's 48-hour rule is real and the penalties are real, which puts older neighbors and anyone with health limitations in a genuinely difficult position. If you have a neighbor who cannot safely clear their own sidewalk, there are organized ways to help beyond just showing up with a shovel.

Sioux Falls has informal and formal networks for this, including neighborhood groups and volunteer "snow angel" programs. Coordinating on a block level, even casually, makes it easier to make sure no one is left with an uncleared sidewalk and a mounting fine. For practical ways to organize and what services exist, read [Helping Neighbors Who Cannot Shovel](/guides/helping-neighbors-snow).

## Shoveling Safely When You Do It Yourself

If you are doing the work yourself, the physical risk is real. Shoveling after a heavy wet snow is demanding cardiovascular work, and the combination of exertion and cold is hard on the body.

The basics:

- Warm up before you go out.
- Use a pusher shovel when you can instead of lifting.
- Lift with your legs, not your back, and do not twist.
- Take breaks. Do not try to clear everything in one pass.
- If you have a heart condition or back condition, talk to your doctor before shoveling season, not after a painful incident.

[Shoveling Without Hurting Yourself](/guides/shoveling-safely) covers all of this in detail, including when to stop and call someone else.
