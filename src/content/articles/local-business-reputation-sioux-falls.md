Sioux Falls is a city where reputation travels fast. According to SiouxFalls.Business reporting in January 2026, the city added 5,088 new residents in a single year, bringing the total to 224,676. Those newcomers do not have a trusted plumber, dentist, or electrician yet. They are going to look at your reviews before they ever look at your phone number.

Reputation is Pillar 3 in [The 4 Pillars of a Growing Sioux Falls Business](/guides/four-pillars-local-business-growth-sioux-falls). It sits right at the moment a customer has found you and is deciding whether to call. Visibility brought them to your door. Authority gave them a reason to stay. Reputation is what actually makes them reach for the phone.

## How Customers Read Your Reviews (and What They Are Really Looking For)

Most people do not read every review you have ever received. They scan.

They look at your overall star rating first, then flip to the most recent few reviews to see if you are still doing good work. After that, they look at your lowest reviews to understand what went wrong for someone else.

What they are really asking is: "Is this business safe to hire?" They want evidence that you show up, do what you said, and treat people fairly when something goes wrong.

A handful of old five-star reviews and nothing newer than two years ago does not answer that question well. It raises it.

This is why a steady, ongoing flow of honest reviews matters far more than a one-time push. A business that collects two or three real reviews a month, month after month, looks alive and trustworthy. A business sitting on 40 reviews that all came in during one week three years ago looks like it ran a campaign and forgot about it.

Reviewers also read your replies. If you have never responded to anything, that sends a signal too.

## Earning a Steady Flow of Honest Reviews

The biggest reason local businesses have too few reviews is not that customers dislike them. It is that nobody asked.

Most satisfied customers will not leave a review unless the moment is easy and the ask is direct. Here is what that looks like in practice:

- Ask at the right moment, right after a job is complete, an appointment ends, or a customer expresses satisfaction in person.
- Give them a direct link, not "go find us on Google." A short URL or a QR code on a card, receipt, or follow-up text removes all friction.
- Keep the ask human. "Would you mind leaving us a quick review? It really helps a small local business." is better than a generic automated blast.
- Build it into your process. If you send a follow-up text or email after every job, that is the right place to include the review link.

Google is explicit about what it does not allow: you cannot pay for reviews, offer discounts or gifts in exchange, or screen customers before asking (sending only happy customers to the review page while steering unhappy ones elsewhere). Any of those shortcuts can get your reviews removed and your profile flagged.

What Google does encourage is simply asking your customers and making it easy. That is the whole playbook, done consistently.

## Replying to Every Review, Not Just the Bad Ones

According to Google's own guidance on Business Profile, replying to reviews shows customers that you value their feedback. That matters for two reasons.

First, it shows the person who left the review that a real human being read it. Second, it shows every future customer who reads your profile that you are engaged and responsive.

Replying to positive reviews does not need to be elaborate. A sentence or two, using the customer's first name if they included it, acknowledging something specific they mentioned, is enough. Do not copy and paste the same reply to every review. It reads as automated and undoes the warmth you are trying to create.

For positive reviews, some simple approaches:

- Thank them by name if possible.
- Reference the specific service or visit they mentioned.
- Invite them back or mention something coming up.

Replying to your reviews is also a small signal to Google. The platform notes that businesses that engage with their profiles are more likely to show up in local results, because completeness and activity are part of how prominence is measured.

## Handling a Bad Review Without Making It Worse

A bad review will happen. Even businesses that do excellent work eventually get one from a miscommunication, a bad day, or someone who was difficult from the start.

The goal of your reply is not to win the argument. It is to show every future reader how you handle a problem.

Here is a process that works:

1. Wait at least a few hours before replying. Writing when you are frustrated rarely ends well.
2. Acknowledge the experience without admitting fault you do not believe you have. "I'm sorry this visit didn't go the way either of us hoped" is honest without being an admission.
3. Move it offline. Invite them to call you directly or email you to work it out. That shows good faith and stops a public back-and-forth.
4. Keep it short. A long defensive reply looks worse than the original review.

What you should never do: accuse the reviewer of lying, post personal details about the customer, or argue with their account of events. Readers notice how businesses behave when things go wrong far more than they notice the original complaint.

If a review clearly violates Google's policies (it is fake, from a competitor, or contains prohibited content), you can flag it for removal through your Business Profile. That process is not fast, and it is not guaranteed, but it is the right step when a review is genuinely illegitimate.

## Reputation Offline and Online Are the Same Thing in Sioux Falls

This is worth saying plainly: online reviews are just what your reputation looks like to a stranger.

Sioux Falls is a city where people know each other. Neighborhoods like McKennan Park and All Saints have active community networks. The same contractor someone praises at a backyard gathering is the one they tag in a local Facebook group that night. The restaurant someone complains about to a friend is the restaurant that gets a one-star review the next morning.

Your online reputation and your offline reputation are not two separate things you manage separately. They are reflections of the same underlying reality: how you treat people.

This means the most reliable reputation strategy is not a software tool or a clever campaign. It is doing the work well, communicating clearly, following through on what you said, and fixing things when they go wrong. Reviews are just the evidence of that.

Sioux Falls is growing fast. Mayor Paul TenHaken's office reported the metro is projected to reach 360,000 people by 2030, according to SiouxFalls.Business. Those future residents will search for local businesses the same way residents do today, and your review profile will either earn their call or send them to someone else.

The online reputation management of a small business is not a marketing tactic. It is an extension of how you do business, made visible to people who have not met you yet.
