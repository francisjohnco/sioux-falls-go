Hiring a snow removal service sounds simple until the first big storm hits and your driveway still isn't clear at 7 a.m. Getting it right means knowing what to ask before you sign anything, not after. This article covers the full checklist, from verifying insurance to understanding your City obligations. For the full picture on managing snow at your home, start with [Snow Removal in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/snow-removal-guide-sioux-falls).

## What to Ask Before You Hire Anyone

The wrong hire costs you twice: once for the contract, once for fixing whatever goes wrong. These are the questions that actually matter.

**Licensing and insurance first:**

- Ask for proof of general liability insurance and confirm it covers property damage to driveways and lawns.
- Ask whether their workers are covered by workers' compensation. If a crew member is injured on your property and they aren't covered, you could be exposed.
- South Dakota does not require a state license specifically for snow removal contractors, so insurance is your main protection. Ask to see the certificate, not just a verbal yes.

**Trigger depth and arrival time:**

- A "trigger" is the snow accumulation depth at which the company will come to your property. Common triggers are one inch, two inches, or three inches.
- Ask what their trigger is and whether it matches your needs. If you need to leave for work by 6:30 a.m., a service that starts at 8 a.m. is not the right fit.
- Ask how they define "arrival time" and what happens during back-to-back storms. Services that return for second pushes are not all the same.

**What exactly is included:**

- Does the price cover the sidewalk, or only the driveway? Many services treat these separately.
- Does it include salting or ice melt, or is that an add-on?
- What about the apron at the bottom of the driveway where the city plow leaves a ridge?

## The City's Sidewalk Rule and Why It Matters

If you hire a service that skips your sidewalk, the City's clock is still running on you, not on them.

According to City Ordinance 96.100, sidewalks must be cleared edge to edge, down to the concrete, within 48 hours after snow stops falling. Corner lots must also clear curb ramps. The City does not investigate reports filed before that 48-hour window closes, but once it passes, you are exposed.

As reported by Dakota News Now and KSOO in 2025, an uncleared sidewalk can bring a $100 penalty. Beyond the fine, the City can hire its own contractor to clear your walk and charge the cost back to you as a special assessment under City Ordinance 96.104.

This is a real concern for older homeowners who rely entirely on a hired service. If the crew skips a run after a light second storm, you may not know until the 48 hours are almost up. Make sure the contract spells out sidewalk coverage explicitly and that the service handles the curb ramp if you are on a corner lot.

For the full breakdown of the ordinance and how the reporting process works, see [The 48-Hour Sidewalk Rule, Explained](/guides/sidewalk-snow-48-hours).

## Snow Piles, Lawn Edges, and Property Damage

Where the snow goes matters as much as when it gets moved.

Under City ordinance, you cannot place snow on the street, on sidewalks, or in the right of way. That applies to your contractor too. Snow deposited in the street violates City ordinance 96.103. A good contractor knows this and will push or blow snow onto your lawn or into an agreed-upon area on your property.

Common complaints from Sioux Falls homeowners include:

- Snow piles blocking the mailbox or blocking sight lines at the driveway exit.
- Plow stakes cutting up lawn edges, especially on curved driveways.
- Gravel or decorative rock getting mixed into the snow and scattered.

Before the season starts, walk your property with the contractor or at least agree in writing on where snow will be piled and what happens if that area fills up.

If a plow does tear up your lawn or crack a driveway edge, document it with photos right away. What the contract says about damage determines what happens next. The article [When a Plow Damages Your Lawn or Driveway](/guides/snow-removal-damage) covers exactly what to look for in that language and how to handle spring repair.

## Reading the Contract Before You Sign

A verbal agreement disappears in the first dispute. A written contract protects you both.

The contract should spell out all of the following before you sign:

- **Trigger depth.** The specific accumulation that starts service, not "when it snows."
- **Arrival window.** The latest the service commits to arriving after the trigger is met.
- **Scope.** Exactly which areas are covered (driveway, sidewalk, apron, steps, curb ramp).
- **Pile placement.** Where snow will go, and what happens when that area fills up.
- **Damage clause.** Who is responsible, how damage is reported, and the timeline for repair.
- **Pricing structure.** Whether you are paying per push or on a seasonal flat rate, and what happens in an unusually heavy winter under a seasonal contract.
- **Cancellation terms.** How much notice you need to give, and whether there is a refund if you cancel mid-season.

If the contract is vague on any of these points, ask for clarification in writing before signing. A contractor who won't put specifics in writing is showing you something important.

For a deeper look at how per-push and seasonal pricing actually work and which one makes sense for your situation, see [Per Push or Seasonal Snow Contract?](/guides/per-push-vs-seasonal).

## Red Flags to Watch For

A few patterns show up often enough to be worth naming.

**Before you hire:**

- No proof of liability insurance, or they hesitate when you ask.
- A quote that is significantly lower than everyone else with no explanation.
- No written contract offered, only a handshake deal.
- Unclear or missing trigger language. "We come when it snows" is not a trigger.

**Once service starts:**

- Consistent late arrivals that fall outside what the contract specifies.
- Sidewalks left undone after runs where the driveway was cleared.
- Snow regularly pushed toward the street or piled against the mailbox.
- No response when you call or message after a storm.

If problems happen, start by putting your concern in writing (a text or email creates a record). Give the contractor a reasonable chance to correct it. If the issue repeats and the contract includes a cancellation clause, use it. Keep photos of every problem in case you need to dispute a charge.
