Carbon monoxide is invisible, odorless, and produced by every gas furnace in Sioux Falls every single heating season. Most of the time it vents safely outside. The problem is when it doesn't, and in a climate where your furnace can run continuously for days during a cold snap, the risk of a slow, undetected leak is real. If you want the broader picture of heating your home safely, start with the [Heating and Cooling in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/hvac-guide-sioux-falls) and come back here for the specifics on CO.

## How Carbon Monoxide Forms in a Gas Furnace

Your furnace burns natural gas to create heat. That combustion produces carbon monoxide as a byproduct, along with water vapor and carbon dioxide.

Under normal operation, those combustion gases travel through the heat exchanger and exit through the flue pipe to the outside. The air your family breathes never touches them.

The danger starts when that system breaks down. The most common causes in a residential furnace include:

- A cracked or corroded heat exchanger
- A blocked or damaged flue pipe
- Insufficient combustion air (a sealed house with no fresh air intake)
- A backdrafting furnace, where exhaust gets pulled back inside instead of venting out

Sioux Falls homes built before the 1990s are more likely to have aging heat exchangers and older venting setups that weren't designed for today's tighter building envelopes. That age factor matters when you're deciding how urgently to schedule an inspection.

A cracked heat exchanger is the single most common furnace-related CO source in residential homes. It can be invisible to a homeowner and sometimes invisible even to an undertrained technician without the right tools. This is one reason the words "cracked heat exchanger" in a repair quote deserve a careful second look, and potentially a second opinion. See [When to Get a Second Opinion on an HVAC Repair](/guides/hvac-second-opinion) for how to handle that conversation.

## CO Detector Placement in Your Home

A CO detector you can't hear from the bedroom is not doing its most important job, which is waking you up at night.

The Consumer Product Safety Commission recommends at least one CO detector on every level of your home, including the basement, and one outside each sleeping area. That is the minimum. For most Sioux Falls homes with a basement furnace and bedrooms on the main floor, that means at least two units placed thoughtfully.

Placement details that matter:

- Mount detectors at roughly knee height or at table height on a wall, since CO is close to the same weight as air and distributes throughout a room rather than rising or sinking
- Do not place a detector directly next to the furnace, water heater, or range, since normal exhaust smells can cause nuisance alarms
- Plug-in models with battery backup handle power outages, which happen during the same storms that stress your furnace hardest

Replace CO detectors every five to seven years. The sensing element degrades over time even if the unit seems to be working. Check the manufacture date on the back of your existing detectors today.

## Warning Signs Before an Alarm Sounds

A CO alarm going off is the clearest warning. But sometimes CO accumulates slowly enough that the alarm threshold isn't reached before people start feeling sick.

Know these symptoms of CO exposure:

- Headache, dizziness, or nausea that improves when you leave the house
- Fatigue or confusion that seems worse in the morning after a night with the heat running
- Multiple people or pets in the household feeling unwell at the same time
- A yellow or flickering burner flame instead of a crisp blue one (visible through your furnace's sight glass if it has one)

The pattern of "feels better outside" is a classic signal that something in the home is producing CO. If you notice it, don't dismiss it as a coincidence or a winter cold.

You may also notice physical signs around the furnace itself: soot or black marks near the burner compartment, excessive condensation on windows near the unit, or a persistent smell of exhaust. None of these mean CO is definitely present, but all of them are reasons to call a technician before running the furnace another day.

## What to Do When a CO Alarm Sounds

The only right response to a CO alarm is to treat it as a real emergency until proven otherwise.

Do not stop to investigate. Do not try to find the source yourself.

Here is exactly what to do:

1. Get everyone out of the house immediately, including pets
2. Leave the door open behind you as you exit
3. Call 911 from outside or from a neighbor's home
4. Do not go back inside until emergency responders have cleared the building
5. After the all-clear, call a licensed HVAC technician before you turn the furnace back on

The City of Sioux Falls Building Services (605-367-8672) can confirm whether your HVAC contractor holds a current mechanical license before you let anyone work on your system. That check takes two minutes and is worth doing, especially after an emergency when you may be calling unfamiliar companies.

If your detector chirps or displays an error code rather than a full alarm, check the manufacturer's manual first. A chirp often means a low battery or end-of-life alert, not a CO reading. But if you are unsure, treat it as a real alarm.

## Annual Inspections and Why They Matter Here

A fall furnace tune-up is not just about efficiency. It is the primary way a cracked heat exchanger, a deteriorating flue, or a blocked combustion air intake gets caught before it becomes a CO problem.

Sioux Falls furnaces run hard from roughly October through April. That is six or more months of daily combustion cycles, and the freeze-thaw conditions here can accelerate corrosion on older flue pipes and heat exchangers. An inspection before that season starts is the right time to catch problems.

A real inspection for CO safety should include:

- Visual and electronic check of the heat exchanger
- Flue and venting inspection for blockages, corrosion, or improper slope
- Combustion air verification
- Carbon monoxide measurement at the supply registers while the furnace runs
- A look at the burner flame and ignition components

If a technician completes a "tune-up" in under 30 minutes and doesn't mention any of these items, ask specifically whether the heat exchanger was inspected and how. The [Is a Furnace Tune-Up Before Winter Worth It?](/guides/furnace-tune-up-before-winter) article explains what a thorough inspection should actually look like, so you know what to ask for before the technician arrives.

New furnaces with sealed combustion and direct PVC venting are significantly safer than older atmospheric-vented units because combustion air is drawn from outside rather than from the room. If your furnace is 20 or more years old and vents through a metal flue pipe into a chimney, that design is worth discussing with a technician during your next inspection.
