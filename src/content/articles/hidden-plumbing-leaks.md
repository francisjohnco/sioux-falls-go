A hidden water leak can quietly cause thousands of dollars in damage before anyone notices a single wet spot. The tricky part is that the most damaging leaks are often the ones you cannot see, behind drywall, under a slab, or inside a wall cavity. Sioux Falls homeowners deal with an extra layer of complexity: expansive clay soil shifts under foundations, freeze-thaw cycles stress pipe joints every winter, and hard water from the Big Sioux Aquifer leaves mineral deposits that can eventually weaken fittings. If you want the full picture of how plumbing works in a Sioux Falls home, start with [Plumbing in a Sioux Falls Home, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/plumbing-guide-sioux-falls), then come back here for the specifics on leak detection.

## Warning Signs You Might Have a Hidden Leak

The signs of a hidden water leak are easy to dismiss one at a time. Together, they tell a clear story.

**Watch for any of these:**

- A water bill that jumped with no obvious explanation
- A meter that keeps spinning after you have shut off every fixture in the house
- Soft or discolored spots on walls, ceilings, or floors
- A musty or mildew smell in a room that is not normally damp
- Warm patches on a cold floor (a sign of a hot water line leak under a slab)
- Paint that is bubbling or wallpaper that is peeling without an obvious cause
- Low water pressure that appeared gradually

None of these guarantees a leak on its own. But Sioux Falls homeowners who have been through this often describe the same pattern: they noticed something small, ignored it for a few weeks, then found a much bigger problem later.

The musty smell is worth taking seriously. In a climate that gets plenty of snow every winter and sees hard freeze-thaw cycles, moisture that gets into a wall cavity during winter can stay trapped there long after the temperature warms back up. Mold can establish itself faster than most people expect.

## The Meter Test: A Step You Can Do Right Now

Before you call anyone, run this test. It costs nothing and takes about 15 minutes.

**What you need:**

- Access to your water meter (usually in a pit near the street or in the basement)
- A pen and paper or a phone photo

**Step by step:**

1. Make sure no water is running anywhere in the house. That means no dishwasher, no ice maker, no irrigation, no one in the shower.
2. Find your water meter and look at the current reading. Write it down or photograph it.
3. Also look for a small triangle or dial on the meter face. This is a low-flow indicator. If it is spinning right now, water is moving somewhere in the system.
4. Wait 15 to 30 minutes without using any water.
5. Check the reading again. If it changed, you have a leak somewhere between the meter and your fixtures.

If the low-flow indicator was spinning with everything off, that alone is enough reason to call a licensed plumber. Do not wait to see if it fixes itself.

One thing to know: a meter that shows no movement does not always mean no leak. Very slow seeps may not register on an older meter. The absence of movement is reassuring but not a guarantee.

## How Plumbers Actually Trace a Hidden Leak

When a homeowner cannot find the source, a plumber has several tools available. The right one depends on where the leak is suspected.

**Pressure testing** is often the first step. A plumber isolates sections of pipe and pressurizes them to see where pressure drops. This narrows the location before any wall or floor gets opened.

**Acoustic listening equipment** picks up the sound of water moving or dripping inside walls and under slabs. Experienced technicians can often pinpoint a leak location within a few inches this way, which keeps the amount of drywall or concrete that needs to be opened to a minimum.

**Thermal imaging cameras** detect temperature differences in walls and floors. A leaking hot water line shows up as a warmer patch. This is particularly useful for slab leaks, which are more common in homes where the clay soil underneath has shifted over time.

**Pipe cameras** are used when a drain or sewer line is suspected. A small camera on a flexible cable travels through the pipe and sends video back to a monitor. Homeowners who have been through tree root issues in older Sioux Falls neighborhoods often first learn the full extent of the damage this way.

A plumber who explains what tool they are using and why, and who shows you what they found before cutting anything open, is doing the job right. That kind of transparency before starting work is something Sioux Falls homeowners consistently praise when they talk about good service.

## Why Waiting Always Costs More

A leak that runs for weeks does more than waste water. It saturates framing, feeds mold growth, and in winter can freeze inside a wall and cause a pipe to split. Sioux Falls winters are hard on any moisture that gets into a structure.

There is also the question of your water bill. The City of Sioux Falls bills on metered usage. A leak that runs continuously adds up quickly, and that water is gone regardless of whether it reached a fixture you used.

The other cost of waiting is structural. Sioux Falls soils have a significant clay content, and water pooling under or near a foundation changes how that soil behaves. Persistent moisture can contribute to the kind of foundation movement that turns a plumbing repair into a much larger project.

If you discover the leak involves your hot water line, it is worth reading [Water Heater Repair or Replace? How to Decide](/guides/water-heater-repair-or-replace) before assuming the worst. Local homeowners have reported being told a water heater was done when a second opinion found a simple fix. Know your options before agreeing to a full replacement.

## Permits, Licensing, and What to Verify Before Work Starts

Once a plumber locates a hidden leak and proposes a repair, ask two things before they start.

**First, is a permit required?** The City of Sioux Falls requires permits for most plumbing work beyond simple fixture repairs. Work done without a permit can create problems when you sell the house, and unlicensed repairs may not meet the city's inspection standards.

**Second, is the contractor licensed in Sioux Falls?** The City requires a local plumbing contractor license and does not accept licenses from other states. You can verify any contractor on the city's currently licensed contractors list at siouxfalls.gov/licensed-contractors. This takes less than two minutes and is worth doing.

If you are still figuring out whether your problem needs a licensed plumber or just a drain company, [Plumber or Drain Cleaner? How to Call the Right One](/guides/plumber-vs-drain-cleaner) walks through exactly that question. The wrong call can mean paying twice.

Homeowners who own and live in a single-family home can pull their own plumbing permits for their own residence, but they are responsible for passing city inspections. For a hidden leak repair that involves opening walls or working near the foundation, most people are better served by a licensed contractor who handles the permit process as part of the job.
