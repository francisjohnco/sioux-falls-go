Spring in Sioux Falls has a way of announcing itself in your basement before you ever feel it outside. Snowmelt, frozen ground that cannot absorb water fast enough, and weeks of rain all arrive at once. For a lot of homeowners, the result is the same thing every year: water in a corner, a wet floor along one wall, or a damp smell that shows up in March and lingers into May.

If you want the full picture of how to protect your basement long term, start with [A Dry Basement in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/basement-waterproofing-guide-sioux-falls). This article focuses specifically on why spring is so hard on Sioux Falls basements and what to look at first before you call anyone.

## Why Spring Is Especially Hard on Sioux Falls Basements

The ground here does not thaw evenly. The top few inches warm up while deeper soil stays frozen, which means snowmelt has almost nowhere to go. It saturates the surface and migrates sideways toward your foundation.

Sioux Falls sits on expansive clay soil. Clay holds water instead of draining it, and it swells when wet. When that saturated clay presses against your foundation walls for weeks at a time, even a wall with no visible cracks can let in moisture.

The other factor is the freeze-thaw cycle. Water that worked its way into a small crack all winter has expanded and contracted dozens of times. By the time spring arrives, what started as a hairline can be wide enough to let in a steady seep. Many homeowners notice for the first time in spring a crack they didn't know existed.

## The Most Common Entry Points for Spring Water

Knowing where water gets in helps you narrow down what to check. Here are the most typical spots:

- **The cove joint.** This is the seam where your basement floor meets the wall. It is not welded together. When groundwater builds up outside, hydrostatic pressure can push it through that joint, often showing up as a thin line of water running along the base of the wall.
- **Wall cracks.** Poured concrete walls crack as they cure and again as the ground shifts. Stair-step cracks in block walls often open up more in spring. See [Foundation Cracks, Which Ones Matter](/guides/foundation-cracks) for help reading what you are looking at.
- **Window wells.** A window well that fills with snowmelt and has no working drain sends water directly toward your window frame and into the basement. This is one of the most overlooked spring entry points.
- **The floor itself.** Floor cracks can seep when the water table is high. A puddle in the middle of the floor with no wall source nearby is often coming up through the slab.
- **Tie rod holes.** In poured concrete walls, the metal rods used during forming are removed and the holes are patched. Those patches can fail, especially after years of freeze-thaw pressure.

## Snowmelt, Grading, and Gutters: What to Check Outside First

Before anything else, walk around your house on the first warm day and watch where the water goes. These outdoor problems cause or worsen the majority of spring basement leaks, and some of them cost nothing to fix.

**Grading.** The ground directly against your foundation should slope away from the house. If it has settled flat or is sloping back toward the wall, water pools right against the foundation during snowmelt. A few inches of slope over the first several feet matters a great deal.

**Downspouts.** A downspout that empties right at the foundation is pumping thousands of gallons of water directly into the soil next to your basement every time it rains. Extensions that carry water at least four to six feet away from the house are inexpensive and often make a noticeable difference by the following spring. For a detailed look at both grading and downspouts, [Grading and Downspouts, the Cheapest Basement Fix](/guides/grading-and-downspouts) walks through the whole process.

**Gutters.** Clogged gutters overflow and dump water along the foundation line instead of routing it away. Check them in late fall and again after the first thaw. Ice dams can also block gutters and cause overflow that you will not see until the snow melts.

**Window well drainage.** Window wells should have gravel at the bottom and, ideally, a drain that connects to a drainage system or daylight. If yours are full of leaves and debris, water has no way out except through your window frame.

Fixing grading and extending downspouts will not solve every wet basement problem, but they are the right first step because they reduce the volume of water arriving at your foundation in the first place. Many homeowners who invested in full waterproofing systems later said they wished someone had told them to try the cheap fixes first.

## What the Cove Joint and Hydrostatic Pressure Actually Mean

You may have heard a waterproofing contractor use the phrase "hydrostatic pressure" and wondered if it was a sales term. It is a real thing, and spring is when you feel it most.

When saturated clay soil surrounds your foundation and the water table rises after snowmelt, pressure builds against the outside of your walls and under your floor. Water finds the path of least resistance, which is usually the cove joint, an existing crack, or a patched hole.

Water appearing at the base of the wall, along the cove joint, is not a sign that your wall has failed structurally. It is a drainage problem. Interior drainage systems, when they are the right solution, work by intercepting water at the cove joint and routing it to a sump pump before it can spread across your floor.

Water pushing through mid-wall cracks, or cracks that are growing or bulging, is a different situation. That can indicate soil pressure on the wall itself, which is a structural concern worth having evaluated. If your walls look bowed or if doors on the main floor are sticking in spring, do not wait on that. The [Bowing Basement Walls](/guides/bowing-basement-walls) guide explains what to watch for.

## What to Try First and When to Call Someone

If you have water in one corner every spring, here is a reasonable sequence before you commit to anything expensive:

1. Fix grading within two to three feet of the foundation if it slopes toward the house.
2. Add downspout extensions so water discharges well away from the foundation.
3. Clean window wells and check whether they have functional drainage.
4. After the next rain, trace the water to its exact entry point. Write it down. Take a photo.
5. If the entry point is a visible crack, have it looked at. See what the contractor says about the cause before agreeing to any repair.

If you do those things and still get water in the same spot the following spring, you have ruled out the cheap fixes and you have better information for any contractor conversation.

When you do call a contractor, the City of Sioux Falls requires a Residential Building Contractor's License for all contractors working on residential homes within city limits, and the City does not accept licenses from other states. You can verify any contractor on the currently licensed list at siouxfalls.gov/licensed-contractors before anyone sets foot in your home.

Look for a contractor who tells you what is causing the water before recommending a fix. Homeowners who have been through this process consistently say the best experiences came from crews who explained the cause first, not the ones who arrived with a price for a full perimeter system before looking at anything.
