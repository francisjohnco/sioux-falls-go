If you have one cold upstairs bedroom, one room that never catches up no matter how high you set the thermostat, or wet spots on a ceiling every January, the attic is almost always where the story starts. Heat rises, and in a Sioux Falls winter that physics works against you constantly. Before you replace a furnace or add a space heater, read [Insulating a Sioux Falls Home, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/insulation-guide-sioux-falls) to understand the whole picture, then come back here for the deep dive on why the attic gets fixed first and what that actually involves.

## Why Heat Goes Up and Not Sideways

Warm air is lighter than cold air, so it always moves toward the highest point in your home.

Your ceiling is not a solid barrier. It has light fixture boxes, recessed cans, the attic hatch, top plates where interior walls meet the ceiling, and every pipe or wire that passes through.

Each one of those gaps is an escape route. The house literally exhales warm air through those holes and pulls cold air in from every low gap in return, a cycle called the stack effect.

The result is predictable: upstairs rooms lose heat fastest, heating bills climb, and in a cold enough winter you get frost on the attic framing or wet spots on the ceiling below. Local homeowners have described seeing ice on the framing in January after noticing damp patches in an upstairs bedroom first. That is not a roof leak. That is an air leakage problem in the attic floor.

## Air Sealing the Attic Floor Before You Add Insulation

Adding more insulation on top of a leaky attic floor is like putting a warm blanket over a window you left open.

The blown-in cellulose or fiberglass will slow radiant heat loss, but it will not stop air movement. Air sealing has to come first.

A contractor doing this job right will pull back or work around existing insulation to seal:

- Top plates on all interior and exterior walls
- Recessed light housings (using fire-rated boxes or covers)
- The attic hatch with weatherstripping and a rigid insulated cover
- Plumbing and electrical penetrations
- Any ductwork that passes through the attic floor

Ask any contractor you call whether air sealing is included in their quote, or whether it is a separate line item. These are different scopes of work and you need to know which one you are buying. The article [Air Sealing, the Cheapest Comfort Upgrade](/guides/air-sealing-basics) covers where leaks hide and what the sealing process looks like in more detail.

## Depth, R-Value and What "Enough" Means

Once the floor is sealed, the insulation itself needs to be deep enough to actually work.

Depth matters because every insulation material has a rated R-value per inch. Blown-in fiberglass and blown-in cellulose are the two most common choices for attics in this region. Your contractor should leave depth marker rulers standing upright in the insulation so you can verify the installed depth without guessing.

What depth is enough? That depends on what you already have and what you are adding. Ask your contractor to tell you the existing R-value, the target R-value, and the installed depth you should see when the job is done. Get those numbers in writing before work starts, not after.

A good contractor will also show you photos from inside the attic before and after, especially of the air sealing work, since you cannot see that once insulation covers it. Sioux Falls homeowners who have praised blown-in attic jobs specifically mention crews who showed up, explained what they found, and provided photos of the work. That transparency is a reasonable thing to expect.

## Keeping Vents Open Is Not Optional

One of the most common mistakes made during attic insulation work is burying the soffit vents under blown-in material.

Soffit vents are the intake side of your attic's ventilation system. If they get blocked, the attic traps moisture instead of venting it out. That moisture causes mold, rot, and in cold weather, frost on the rafters.

Proper attic insulation includes vent baffles (sometimes called rafter baffles or wind baffles), which are cardboard or foam channels installed at each rafter bay to hold a clear air path from the soffit up to the ridge vent.

When you are comparing quotes, ask specifically whether vent baffles are included. If a contractor skips this step, you can end up with a well-insulated but poorly ventilated attic, which creates a new set of problems. If you want to understand how that connects to ice dam risk, [Insulation, Ventilation and Ice Dams](/guides/insulation-and-ice-dams) explains the relationship clearly.

## What to Expect on Install Day

A blown-in attic job is typically completed in a single visit, which is one reason homeowners find it less disruptive than other insulation projects.

Here is roughly how the day goes:

- The crew arrives and sets up a hose from the blowing machine (usually in a truck or trailer) through a window, door, or directly into the attic hatch
- If air sealing is included, that work happens first, before any insulation is blown in
- Baffles are installed or checked at the soffits
- The insulation is blown in, with depth markers placed as they go
- The crew does a walkthrough and cleans up any material that ended up in the living space

Before the crew leaves, ask them to walk you through the depth markers and confirm the installed depth matches what was written in your quote. If you have an attic hatch, ask whether it was insulated and weatherstripped as part of the job.

One thing that can complicate install day: old insulation with signs of pest activity. If your attic has mouse droppings or damaged insulation from animals, that is a separate conversation. The article [Mice, Squirrels and Old Insulation](/guides/insulation-and-critters) covers what to look for and when removal needs to come before anything new goes in.

## The Practical Next Step

If you are not sure what your attic currently has or how much air sealing has ever been done, a home energy assessment is the most direct way to find out.

A blower door test and thermal camera will show exactly where air is moving and where insulation is thin. That report gives you something specific to bring to any contractor, rather than relying on a visual estimate from someone selling you a job.

Ask your contractor for a written quote that lists the existing R-value, the target R-value, the installed depth, whether air sealing is included, whether vent baffles are included, and what cleanup looks like. Those six items separate a real proposal from a vague one.
