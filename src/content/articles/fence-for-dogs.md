A fence built for people and a fence built for dogs are not always the same thing. The height that satisfies your HOA may not contain a determined Lab. The gap that looks fine to you is an escape route for a Beagle. Getting this right means thinking about your specific dog, your specific yard, and a few Sioux Falls realities before you spend a dollar.

Start with [Building a Fence in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/fence-guide-sioux-falls) for the full picture on permits, materials, and contractors. This article focuses entirely on what changes when dogs are the reason you are building.

## What Height Actually Contains Your Dog

Height is the first question most people ask, and the right answer depends entirely on the dog.

A six-foot fence will contain most breeds. A four-foot fence will not contain a large dog that is motivated or athletic. Some dogs clear five feet without touching it.

Before you decide on height, think honestly about your dog:

- Does it jump for fun or only when excited by something specific?
- Has it escaped a fence before, and how?
- Is it a known escape artist breed (Siberian Husky, Belgian Malinois, standard Poodle)?

The City of Sioux Falls allows fences up to four feet tall in front yards. In rear and side yards the limit is higher, but many Sioux Falls neighborhoods have covenants that restrict height beyond City rules. Check your covenants before you assume six feet is allowed. See [Fence Height Rules by Yard](/guides/fence-height-rules) for the full breakdown by yard zone and lot type.

If your dog is a jumper and your covenant caps you at four feet in a certain area, you have a problem to solve before construction starts, not after.

## Gaps, Pickets, and Keeping Dogs In (and Out)

A fence gap that a child would not squeeze through can still swallow a small dog.

Standard picket spacing on wood and vinyl privacy fences runs roughly three to four inches. That is fine for most medium and large dogs but tight enough matters for small breeds or puppies. Ask your installer specifically what the picket gap will be, not just the style name.

Chain link is worth a separate conversation. It contains dogs reliably if the bottom is secured and the mesh size is appropriate, but small dogs and puppies can push through or under loose bottom edges.

Things to confirm with your installer before signing anything:

- Exact gap measurement between pickets or mesh openings
- How the bottom of the fence is finished (flush to grade, buried a few inches, or bottom rail only)
- Whether the quote includes any grading work if your yard is uneven

Uneven ground is a real issue. A fence that looks solid on flat ground can have gaps of six inches or more where the terrain dips. Walk your yard before the installer measures and point out every low spot.

## Digging: The Problem You Cannot See Until It Is Too Late

Some dogs dig for fun. Others dig with purpose, and a fence is a goal.

A standard fence installation sets posts in the ground but leaves the bottom of the fence itself sitting at or just above grade. That is enough for most dogs. It is not enough for a Dachshund, Terrier, or any breed with strong digging instinct.

Options that actually work:

- **Buried wire or hardware cloth** along the inside base of the fence line, extending outward underground, so digging hits a barrier
- **A concrete or gravel trench** along the bottom of the fence line
- **L-footer design**, where the bottom of the fence or an attached wire apron lays flat on the ground pointing inward

These are not standard inclusions in most fence quotes. Ask for them specifically and confirm whether they are in the written estimate or not.

Sioux Falls clay soil can make a buried L-footer more effective because heavy clay compacts back firmly around it. That same clay also heaves posts in winter. If you are concerned about post movement, [Why Fence Posts Heave and Lean](/guides/fence-posts-frost-heave) explains what causes it and what to ask your installer about footings.

## Gates: Where Dogs Escape Most Often

Gates are where dog fences fail most often, and the failure usually happens months or years after installation, not the first week.

A gate that hangs correctly on day one can sag after one or two winters of freeze-thaw movement in the soil. A sagging gate stops latching. A gate that does not latch is a gate your dog will figure out.

Sioux Falls homeowners in public reviews of local fence installers frequently mention sagging gates as one of their top frustrations, sometimes within the first year. It is a real and common problem, not a fluke.

What to look for and ask about before you hire:

- **Post size for gate posts.** Gate posts carry more load and should be heavier than line posts. Ask what size the installer uses.
- **Gate hardware.** Heavy-duty hinges and a true self-latching mechanism matter more for a dog fence than a decorative latch.
- **Double gates.** If your gate spans more than four feet, ask whether a double gate with a drop rod is a better option than a single wide swing.
- **Self-closing hinges.** Worth asking about if you have kids who may leave the gate open.

For gates that are already sagging, [Fixing a Sagging Fence Gate](/guides/sagging-fence-gate) covers what causes it and when a repair is enough versus when the gate post needs to come out and be reset.

## Permits, Property Lines, and Not Skipping Steps

A dog fence is still a fence, and in Sioux Falls, every fence needs a permit.

According to the City of Sioux Falls, a permit is required to build, move, replace, or repair a fence. The permit fee listed in the City's fence permit pamphlet is $20, and a final inspection is required after construction. Permits expire six months after issue, though extensions are available. You can reach the Zoning Office at 605-367-8254.

Skipping the permit to move faster is not worth it. The inspection catches problems, and an un-permitted fence can create issues when you sell.

Property lines matter especially for a dog fence, because you want the fence where your dog actually is, not a foot inside your property. The City of Sioux Falls says property line location should be done by a licensed surveyor. If your installer is guessing at your lot line, that is a problem. See [Property Lines, Surveys and Your Neighbor](/guides/property-line-fence) for how to handle this correctly before construction starts.

A few other things to confirm before you build:

- Pull your neighborhood covenants and confirm height and material restrictions
- Check whether your HOA requires approval before construction
- Get everything in writing, including gate hardware, gap sizes, and any digging prevention work

A dog fence is a real investment in your pet's safety. Taking the extra week to do the permit, survey, and covenant check correctly is worth it every time.
