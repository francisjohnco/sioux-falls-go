Seeing moisture on your windows in January does not automatically mean you need new glass. In Sioux Falls, where winter temperatures routinely drop well below zero and indoor air stays sealed tight for months, some condensation is normal. The question is where the moisture is forming and what it is telling you.

There are three distinct situations, and they have three different causes and three different fixes. Before you call anyone, it helps to know which one you are actually looking at. For a full picture of when window problems cross into replacement territory, start with [Replacing Windows in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/window-replacement-guide-sioux-falls).

## The Three Types of Window Condensation in Sioux Falls

**Inside surface condensation** is moisture on the room-side face of the glass. Touch the glass and your finger comes away wet. This is almost always a humidity and airflow problem, not a window problem.

**Frost on the inside of windows** is the same thing, further along. When the glass surface is cold enough and indoor humidity is high enough, condensation freezes instead of dripping. Sioux Falls homeowners with older single-pane storms or low-quality double-pane windows sometimes see this in January and February during cold snaps.

**Fog between the panes** means moisture is trapped inside the sealed glass unit itself. You cannot wipe it off because it is not on a surface you can reach. This is a failed seal, full stop.

**Outside condensation** forms on the outer surface of the glass on cool mornings. It is actually a sign the window is doing its job, keeping heat inside so efficiently that the outer pane stays cold. No action needed.

## Inside Condensation and Frost: What to Adjust First

Indoor humidity is the first dial to turn. The rule of thumb for a Sioux Falls winter is to keep relative humidity lower as outdoor temperatures fall:

- Above 20 degrees F outside: indoor humidity up to about 35 percent is usually fine
- Between 0 and 20 degrees F: aim closer to 30 percent
- Below zero: 25 percent or lower helps prevent frost on inside of windows

A basic digital hygrometer (under twenty dollars at any hardware store) tells you exactly where you stand.

Airflow matters just as much. Heavy drapes, cellular shades pulled all the way down, and furniture pushed against windows all trap cold air against the glass. That trapped layer gets colder than the rest of the room, and condensation forms faster.

Try pulling window coverings back a few inches from the glass and running a ceiling fan on low to circulate warm air toward the windows. Many Sioux Falls homeowners are surprised how much this helps before any repairs are needed.

If you run a whole-house humidifier, check whether it has an outdoor temperature sensor. Models without one will keep pumping humidity into the house even when it is minus ten outside, which is a direct path to heavy frost on interior glass surfaces.

## When Frost on Inside of Windows Points to a Bigger Problem

If you adjust humidity and airflow and still see heavy frost or pooling condensation on a window that is supposed to be a modern double-pane unit, the window itself may be underperforming.

A well-made double-pane window with a low-emissivity coating keeps the interior glass surface much warmer than a window without it. In Sioux Falls winters, the difference between a window with a U-factor of 0.22 and one rated at 0.35 is noticeable in both comfort and condensation. (ENERGY STAR Version 7.0, effective October 23, 2023, sets a U-factor of 0.22 or lower as the Northern climate zone threshold, which covers Sioux Falls.)

If your windows are older and you have never seen a label or rating on them, [Window Ratings for a Sioux Falls Winter, U-Factor and SHGC Explained](/guides/window-ratings-cold-climate) walks through exactly how to read those numbers and what they mean for your comfort in January.

Frost concentrated at the corners and edges of a pane, rather than spread across the whole glass, can also point to a thermal bridge in the frame or spacer bar between panes. This is worth noting and mentioning to an installer when you get an assessment.

## Fog Between the Panes: A Failed Seal

Fog or haze trapped inside the glass unit is a different problem entirely. The sealed airspace between panes contains either dry air or an inert gas like argon. When the perimeter seal fails, that gas escapes and humid outside air moves in. Heat and cold cycle the moisture in and out until the inside surfaces become permanently cloudy or streaked.

Sioux Falls homeowners have flagged this in reviews of local window work, describing fog and condensation between panes on windows that were only a few years old. That is a product or installation quality issue, not a humidity problem in the home.

A failed glass unit can sometimes be replaced without replacing the entire window frame. Whether that makes sense depends on the frame condition, the age of the window, and whether a replacement glass unit is still available for that model. [Repair or Replace? What Your Windows Are Telling You](/guides/window-repair-or-replace) covers how to think through that decision without committing to full replacement before you have to.

One thing to ask any contractor who quotes you: is this a glass-only replacement, a sash replacement, or a full-frame job, and what does the warranty cover on the new sealed unit?

## What a Bad Install Can Do

Window condensation problems are not always about the glass itself. A window that was installed without proper insulation around the frame, or without a good air seal at the rough opening, can let cold air infiltrate around the edges. That cold zone creates exactly the conditions for condensation and frost even on a good window.

Sioux Falls homeowners have described in public reviews feeling like their new windows were still drafty, or that they were happier with their original windows after a poor install. If condensation is concentrated around the perimeter of the glass rather than across the whole pane, and if you feel any draft near the frame, ask about the air sealing at the rough opening before assuming the glass is at fault.

This is also a reason to verify that whoever installed your windows pulled a building permit. According to the City of Sioux Falls Building Permit FAQs, window replacement requires a building permit. The City publishes a Currently Licensed Contractors list at siouxfalls.gov/licensed-contractors where you can confirm a contractor holds the right license. A permitted job gets an inspection that catches air-sealing gaps a homeowner cannot easily see.
