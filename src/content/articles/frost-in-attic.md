If you have climbed into your attic on a cold January morning and found ice crystals on the framing or dark wet spots spreading across your bedroom ceiling, you are not alone. Sioux Falls homeowners report this every winter, and it is one of the more alarming things to discover. The good news is that it almost always has a diagnosable cause, and fixing it is not a mystery.

Before going further, the [Insulating a Sioux Falls Home, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/insulation-guide-sioux-falls) lays out the full picture of attic performance for local homes. This article focuses specifically on what causes frost and attic condensation in winter, and what to do about it first.

## What You Are Actually Seeing

Frost in an attic is frozen condensation. Warm, moist air from inside the house finds a way into the attic space, hits the cold roof sheathing, and the moisture deposits there as frost or condensation.

When temperatures swing back above freezing, that frost melts. The water runs down framing and can soak through the ceiling drywall below, showing up as wet spots or water stains in upstairs bedrooms.

In Sioux Falls, those freeze-thaw cycles happen repeatedly through January and February. A single cold snap followed by a mild day is enough to turn an attic frost problem into a wet ceiling problem inside the house.

## The Two Main Causes: Air Leaks and Exhaust Fans

Almost every case of attic frost comes down to one or both of these:

**Air leaks through the attic floor**

Gaps around light fixtures, plumbing chases, top plates, and attic hatches let warm house air pour into the attic continuously. That air carries moisture with it.

A poorly sealed attic hatch is one of the most common culprits. So are recessed lights in the ceiling below the attic, especially older ones without airtight housings.

**Bath fans and kitchen exhaust venting into the attic**

This is surprisingly common in older Sioux Falls homes. The fan pulls humid air from your shower or kitchen and dumps it directly into the attic instead of routing it outside. That concentrated moisture load is more than most attic spaces can handle.

If you can see a flexible duct in your attic that just ends, or one that is disconnected from its roof cap, that is almost certainly a major source of your frost problem. The article on [Bath Fans Venting Into the Attic](/guides/bath-fans-and-attic) covers this in detail, including how to tell if yours is the culprit and what the correct fix looks like.

## How to Check Your Attic Safely in Winter

You do not need to hire anyone to take a first look. If you can safely access your attic, here is what to look for:

- Frost or ice on roof sheathing, especially concentrated in one area rather than everywhere evenly
- Dark staining on wood framing (older moisture that has dried and left a mark)
- Flexible exhaust ducts that end without connecting to a roof or soffit cap
- Visible daylight around attic hatch edges, recessed lights, or top plates
- Compressed or missing insulation directly around exhaust fan locations

Uneven frost patterns matter. If the frost is heaviest near a specific duct termination or a specific area of the ceiling, that tells you a lot about where the moisture is coming from.

Take photos while you are up there. Local homeowners who have had contractors do attic work frequently mention appreciating when the crew showed them photos and explained what they found. Doing it yourself first gives you something to compare.

## Ventilation Is Not the Whole Answer

A common misconception is that adding more soffit or ridge vents will solve an attic frost problem. Ventilation helps move moisture out once it is in the attic, but it cannot keep up if warm moist air is being pumped in continuously through unsealed penetrations or a disconnected bath fan.

The fix has to start with stopping the moisture source. That means air sealing the attic floor and fixing any exhaust fan routing issues before worrying about vent ratios. [Air Sealing, the Cheapest Comfort Upgrade](/guides/air-sealing-basics) explains where leaks hide and what a thorough air sealing job actually covers.

Once air leaks are addressed and exhaust fans are properly routed to the exterior, the existing ventilation in most Sioux Falls homes is often adequate. If a contractor tells you the problem is purely a ventilation issue without mentioning air sealing or exhaust fans, ask them to explain their reasoning.

## What to Fix First and When to Call Someone

If you have confirmed a disconnected bath fan, that is the highest priority fix. Reconnecting or replacing it is often a straightforward job for a licensed contractor and immediately removes a concentrated moisture source.

After that, air sealing the attic floor penetrations is the next step. This is work that can be done in combination with adding insulation, and many homeowners have it done in a single visit.

If you are seeing wet spots on the ceiling already, check whether there is active water damage to the drywall or any sign of mold growth on the framing. Staining that is old and dry is different from framing that feels soft or shows active mold. The latter warrants a closer look before insulation work begins.

If you are not sure what you are dealing with after your own inspection, a home energy assessment with a blower door test can locate exactly where air is moving between the living space and the attic. That gives you a prioritized list rather than guesswork. See [What a Home Energy Assessment Shows](/guides/energy-assessment) for what to expect from that process.

A few questions worth asking any contractor you bring in:

- Will you check and document where exhaust fans are terminating?
- Will you air seal before or as part of adding insulation, or is that a separate scope?
- Can you show me photos of what you find before and after?
- Is there any sign of mold or moisture damage that needs to be addressed first?

Getting clear written answers to those questions before any work starts will tell you a lot about whether a contractor actually understands attic moisture or is just there to blow in more insulation.
