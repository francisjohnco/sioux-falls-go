Cabinets take up more visual space than almost anything else in a kitchen, and they eat the biggest share of a remodel budget. Before you commit to tearing everything out, it is worth knowing that there are three real paths: refinish what you have, reface cabinets by replacing the fronts and hardware, or replace kitchen cabinets entirely. Each one makes sense in different situations, and choosing wrong costs time and money you cannot get back. For a broader look at how the cabinet decision fits into an entire project, see [Remodeling a Kitchen in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/kitchen-remodel-guide-sioux-falls).

The right choice depends on the condition of your cabinet boxes, how happy you are with the layout, and how long you plan to stay in the home. This article walks through each option so you can go into contractor conversations knowing what questions to ask.

## What Refinishing Actually Means and When It Makes Sense

Refinishing means stripping or sanding the existing doors and drawer fronts, then applying new paint or stain. The boxes, hinges, and layout stay exactly as they are.

This option makes the most sense when:

- The box structure is solid with no water damage, warping, or broken joints
- You like your current layout and storage
- The doors are real wood or MDF that can hold a new finish cleanly
- You want a fresh look without a major disruption

Refinishing is the least disruptive path. Most of the work happens on-site or the doors are taken to a shop and returned, so your kitchen is out of service for a shorter stretch than the other two options. Sioux Falls homeowners have noted in public reviews that living without a functional kitchen longer than expected is one of the more stressful parts of any remodel, so a shorter timeline matters.

The honest limitation is that refinishing does not change anything structural. If a door is warped, a hinge is stripped, or the cabinet box itself is soft from moisture exposure, paint will not fix those problems. Ask a contractor to open every door and push on every box before you decide. Surface condition and structural condition are two different things.

## What Refacing Means and When It Makes Sense

Refacing cabinets means keeping the existing boxes in place and replacing the fronts: the doors, drawer fronts, and hardware, plus a veneer or laminate applied to the visible face frames. The footprint, the internal shelving, and the box walls all stay.

This is worth considering when:

- The boxes are structurally sound but the doors are dated, damaged, or just ugly
- You want to change the door style without changing the layout
- You want new hardware as part of the refresh
- A full gut-out feels like more than the kitchen needs

Replacing fronts and hardware is a meaningful visual transformation. From inside the kitchen, nearly everything you see changes: the door profile, the finish, the pulls. What does not change is the interior capacity, the shelf spacing, or how the kitchen works day to day.

The limitation here is that refacing makes sense only if the boxes are worth keeping. If the interiors are dingy, the shelves sag, or the layout frustrates you every morning, putting new faces on the same bones will not solve those problems. A good designer will tell you this directly. Sioux Falls homeowners who have worked with designers describe the value of someone who planned every inch of the kitchen rather than just swapping parts.

One practical thing to know before you hire anyone: according to the City of Sioux Falls, cabinets and countertops do not require a building permit. If your refacing project does not touch plumbing or electrical, you are not in permit territory for the cabinet work itself. If you are adding under-cabinet lighting or a new outlet as part of the refresh, that electrical work does require a permit and must be done by a licensed electrical contractor or under a homeowner permit if you live in and own a single-family home.

## What Replacing Actually Means and When It Is the Right Call

Replacing kitchen cabinets means removing everything down to the walls and installing entirely new boxes, doors, hardware, and often new interior storage systems. It is the most disruptive option and the one that takes the longest.

It makes sense when:

- The existing boxes are water-damaged, structurally soft, or infested
- You want to change the layout, move a wall, or add an island
- You need more or different storage than the current footprint provides
- The cabinets are so old that refacing materials cannot be matched or sourced

Full replacement also gives you a chance to address anything behind the walls, including old plumbing supply lines or wiring that a licensed trade contractor would need to touch anyway. If you are already opening walls for a layout change, it often makes financial sense to handle those upgrades at the same time rather than do them in a second project later.

The tradeoff is timeline. A full replacement involves ordering, lead time, delivery inspection, installation, and then countertop templating after the boxes are in. If any of that sequence gets delayed, the kitchen is unusable longer than you planned. Ordering cabinets carefully to avoid delays is its own subject worth understanding before you get started, and [Ordering Cabinets and Avoiding Delays](/guides/cabinet-lead-times) covers exactly that.

## The Questions to Ask Before You Decide

No article can substitute for someone physically examining your cabinet boxes. Here are the questions to bring to that conversation:

- Are the box joints solid, or is there any softness, swelling, or delamination?
- Are any boxes out of square in a way that would cause new doors to hang badly?
- Is there any evidence of past water leaks at the base cabinets under the sink?
- If we reface, can you source door styles and veneers that will match the age of the boxes?
- If we refinish, what prep work is included, and what is the warranty on the finish?
- If we replace, what does the demo and haul-away include, and who patches the drywall?

Sioux Falls homeowners consistently say in public reviews that a detailed estimate and timeline that actually stayed on target is what separated good experiences from frustrating ones. Ask every contractor for a written estimate that breaks down each stage and spells out what you are responsible for during the project, such as clearing out the cabinets or being home for delivery.

## How the Cabinet Decision Connects to the Rest of the Remodel

Cabinets do not stand alone. The decision you make on cabinets shapes what comes next.

If you are refinishing or refacing, countertops can often stay in place or be swapped without touching the boxes. If you are replacing, countertop templating cannot happen until the new boxes are fully installed and shimmed level, which adds time to the schedule.

New cabinet doors also change how your hardware reads. If you are only refreshing the fronts and hardware, you have flexibility on pulls and knobs. If you are doing a full replacement, the hardware selection often happens during the design phase with the cabinet order.

It is also worth thinking about what the cabinet work will reveal. Opening up the under-sink area during a base cabinet replacement sometimes uncovers supply line or drain issues that a licensed plumber should address. The City of Sioux Falls is clear that plumbing work in a kitchen requires a permit and a licensed plumber, or a homeowner permit if you own and occupy a single-family home and are doing the work yourself.
