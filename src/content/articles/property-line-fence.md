A fence built six inches onto your neighbor's yard is not just awkward, it can become a legal problem that costs far more to fix than the fence itself. Getting the property line right before anything goes in the ground is one of the most important steps in the whole project. The [Building a Fence in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/fence-guide-sioux-falls) walks through the full process from permits to post depth, but this article focuses on exactly one thing: knowing where your lot ends before a single hole is dug.

## Why Sioux Falls Homeowners Get This Wrong

The most common assumption is that the fence everyone has always used marks the actual property line. Sometimes it does. Often it does not.

Old fences drift. They get replaced and rebuilt slightly off. Lots get subdivided. Driveways and landscaping create visual cues that feel like boundaries but were never surveyed.

In established neighborhoods like McKennan Park or All Saints, some lots date back decades and the original corner pins may be buried, rusted, or long gone. If you build on where you think the line is, and it turns out to be wrong, you may have to move or remove the fence at your own expense.

## Finding Your Property Pins

Property pins are small iron or steel stakes driven into the ground at each corner of your lot when it was originally surveyed. Finding them is your first step.

A few ways to locate them:

- Check your plat map (available through the Minnehaha County Auditor's office or Lincoln County if you're in that jurisdiction).
- Use a metal detector along the edges of your lot, especially at corners and where lot lines meet.
- Look at your property deed, which often includes a legal description of the boundary.

If you find a pin, mark it temporarily with a stake or flag. Then look for the others and measure to make sure they match the dimensions on your plat.

If you cannot find the pins, do not guess. The City of Sioux Falls residential fence guide states clearly that locating property lines should be done by a licensed surveyor. That is not a suggestion you should skip to save time.

## When to Hire a Licensed Surveyor for a Fence Survey

If any of the following apply, hire a licensed surveyor before you finalize your fence line:

- You cannot locate all four corner pins.
- Your neighbor disputes where the line is.
- Your lot is an irregular shape or was subdivided at some point.
- There is an existing fence and you are not sure it is on the actual line.
- Your fence will run close to the front yard, where the City of Sioux Falls says fences on both corner and interior lots can be no more than four feet tall, and the exact line determines what counts as front yard.

A licensed surveyor will set or verify pins and can provide a written survey you can keep with your home records. This document protects you if a dispute comes up later.

Survey costs vary. Get a written quote from two or three licensed South Dakota surveyors and ask specifically what is included, whether they will stake the line for you, and whether a written plat or report is part of the price.

## Talking to Your Neighbor Before You Build

A conversation with your neighbor before construction starts prevents most fence disputes. Most people respond well to a heads-up. Most problems come from surprises.

What to cover in that conversation:

- Tell them where you plan to put the fence and show them the property line or your survey.
- Ask whether they have any concerns about style, height, or which side of the fence faces their yard.
- Find out whether they might want to share in the cost if the fence benefits both properties. This is common and worth asking directly.
- Ask if they know of any existing agreements or easements on the shared boundary.

If your neighbor disagrees about where the line is, do not build until you have a licensed survey in hand. Building during an active dispute almost always makes the situation worse.

## Setbacks, Covenants and What You Must Confirm

Your property line is not automatically where your fence goes. City of Sioux Falls rules, neighborhood covenants, and utility easements can all require your fence to sit back from the actual line.

The City of Sioux Falls requires a permit for any new, moved, replaced, or repaired fence, with a $20 fee and a required final inspection (the Zoning Office number is 605-367-8254). Permits expire six months after issue, though extensions are available. See [Fence Permits in Sioux Falls, What to Know](/guides/fence-permit-sioux-falls) for the full permit process.

Many Sioux Falls neighborhoods have HOA or covenant rules that go beyond City minimums on height, material, or placement. Those rules can require a fence to sit inside your property line by a set distance. Check your covenants before you plan the fence location, not after. The article [Fences, Covenants and HOA Approval](/guides/fence-and-hoa) covers exactly how to find and navigate those rules.

Utility easements are another common issue. Even if the survey confirms where your line is, a utility easement along that line may restrict what you can build there or require the fence to be removable. Your plat map will show recorded easements.

## Putting It in Writing With Your Neighbor

If you and your neighbor agree on something, write it down. This is not about distrust, it is about protecting both of you if one of you moves or if memories differ a few years from now.

A simple written agreement can cover:

- The agreed location of the fence relative to the property line.
- Which side of the fence faces which property.
- Who is responsible for maintenance.
- How any shared cost will be handled.

Both parties sign and each keeps a copy. It does not need to be a legal document to be useful.

If you are building exactly on the property line (sometimes called a partition or boundary fence), South Dakota has statutes about shared fences and cost sharing. For anything beyond a simple friendly agreement, consult a real estate attorney familiar with South Dakota law before you build.
