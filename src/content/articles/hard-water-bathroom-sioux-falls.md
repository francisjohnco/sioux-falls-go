If you have lived in Sioux Falls for more than a season, you have probably noticed the white film that builds up on faucets and the haze that creeps across a shower door. That is hard water at work. According to the City of Sioux Falls 2020 finished water quality report, total hardness is about 15 grains per gallon for water treated at the City's own plant and about 10 grains per gallon for Lewis and Clark water. Which source feeds your home can vary by neighborhood and sometimes by street.

Before you pick a tile, a faucet finish, or a shower enclosure, it is worth understanding what that mineral load will do to your choices over time. This article is one part of a larger series. If you are still figuring out the full scope of your project, start with [Remodeling a Bathroom in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/bathroom-remodel-guide-sioux-falls) and come back here when you are ready to think about materials.

## What Hard Water Actually Does to a Bathroom in Sioux Falls

Hard water is water that carries dissolved calcium and magnesium picked up as it moves through rock and soil. The Big Sioux Aquifer, which feeds much of the city's water supply, runs through limestone and dolomite formations that load it with minerals.

When that water evaporates off a surface, it leaves the minerals behind. Do that a few hundred times and you have visible scale.

The effects show up in a few different ways depending on the surface:

- **Glass shower doors:** Calcium deposits etch into glass over time, leaving a haze that cleaning can slow but rarely fully reverse once it sets.
- **Showerheads:** Mineral buildup clogs the small spray holes, reducing pressure and changing the spray pattern.
- **Faucets and handles:** Scale collects around the base of the spout and under the aerator screen, eventually affecting flow.
- **Grout lines:** Hard water leaves deposits in the texture of grout that make lines look dingy and can mask early signs of staining or damage.
- **Toilet bowls:** The ring that forms just below the waterline is almost always calcium scale, and it is stubborn.

None of these are catastrophic, but they all mean your brand-new bathroom will start showing its age faster unless you plan for them.

## Shower Glass: The Most Visible Hard Water Problem

The hard water shower glass problem is one of the most common complaints from Sioux Falls homeowners after a remodel. A frameless glass enclosure looks beautiful on day one. By the end of the first winter, the etching starts.

Glass that has a factory-applied hydrophobic coating sheds water more completely, so minerals have less time to bond to the surface. That coating does wear over time, but it buys you years of easier cleaning. Ask your remodeler or glass supplier specifically whether the glass includes a surface treatment, not just whether it is "easy clean."

Thicker glass also matters. Heavier panels flex less, which means the gaskets and hardware stay tight longer, which means less water sneaking out to the floor or the wall behind the frame.

If a frameless enclosure is not in the budget, a semi-frameless door with minimal metal framing is easier to clean than a fully framed door, because there are fewer channels and corners for scale to pack into.

One more option worth knowing: a tiled walk-in shower with no door at all eliminates the glass problem entirely. The tradeoff is a larger footprint and a carefully designed entry angle to control splash. ## Faucets, Finishes, and Fixtures That Hold Up Better

Finish choice matters a lot in a hard water bathroom. Some finishes show scale immediately; others hide it or resist it.

Polished chrome and high-gloss nickel look crisp in a showroom but show every water spot. You will be wiping them down constantly, or they will look neglected within weeks.

Finishes that tend to perform better in hard water conditions:

- **Brushed or matte nickel:** The texture diffuses light, so spots are less visible.
- **Matte black:** Hides scale well, though white mineral deposits on a dark surface do stand out if you let them build up.
- **Brushed bronze or champagne bronze:** A warm, slightly textured surface that is forgiving.
- **Unlacquered or living finishes:** These develop a patina over time, which can either appeal to you or drive you crazy, so look at real samples before committing.

For showerheads specifically, models with rubber spray nozzles that you can rub with a finger to break off mineral deposits are much easier to maintain than fixed metal nozzles. Aerators on faucets should be easy to unscrew by hand so you can soak them in vinegar when the flow starts to slow.

Toilets and tank fittings are worth thinking about too. The fill valve and flapper are the parts that mineral buildup hits hardest inside the tank. Ask your plumber whether the fixtures being installed have serviceable internal parts, or whether replacing them later will mean replacing the whole unit.

## Grout, Tile, and Wall Surfaces in a Hard Water Bathroom

Grout is porous. In a hard water bathroom, that porosity works against you, because dissolved minerals seep in with every splash and leave deposits behind as the water evaporates.

Epoxy grout is significantly less porous than cement-based grout and resists both staining and mineral penetration much better. It is also harder to install and costs more in labor, so not every remodeler pushes it. Ask specifically whether epoxy grout is an option for shower walls and floors.

If you go with standard cement grout, sealing it matters. But sealer is not permanent. It needs to be reapplied periodically, and in a high-use shower, that interval is shorter than most homeowners expect.

Larger tile formats have fewer grout lines, which is a real maintenance advantage in a hard water bathroom. A large-format porcelain tile on a shower wall means less total grout surface to clean and seal.

For shower floors, smaller mosaic tile has more grout, which catches more mineral deposit, but it also provides more texture for slip resistance. That is a real tradeoff, especially for households planning ahead for safety as family members age. The article [Aging in Place Bathroom Updates That Make Sense](/guides/aging-in-place-bathroom) covers slip resistance and threshold design in more detail.

Avoid very light grout colors if you do not want to spend time cleaning. Mid-tone grays and tans show scale far less than white or cream grout.

## Cleaning Habits That Actually Work

The single most effective thing you can do in a hard water bathroom is squeegee or wipe glass and tile surfaces after every shower. It takes about 20 seconds. It prevents the deposits from bonding to the surface in the first place.

For existing scale, plain white vinegar is the most reliable household option. The mild acidity dissolves calcium carbonate. Apply it, let it sit for several minutes, then scrub and rinse. For heavy buildup around faucet bases or in showerhead nozzles, soaking works better than wiping.

Things to avoid:

- Abrasive scrubbers on glass or polished finishes (they create microscopic scratches that trap more mineral deposit)
- Letting scale sit for weeks before addressing it (the longer it sits, the more it bonds)
- Bleach-based cleaners for hard water scale (bleach kills mold, but it does nothing to dissolve calcium; you need an acid, not a disinfectant)

Build a cleaning routine into the first week you use the new bathroom, before habits form around the old one. It is much easier to maintain a clean surface than to restore one.

## When to Ask a Plumber About a Water Softener

A water softener treats the problem at the source rather than at the surface. It exchanges calcium and magnesium ions for sodium ions, which do not leave scale.

Installing a softener during a bathroom remodel is worth discussing with your plumber, especially if the project involves opening walls anyway. Routing a softened water line is simpler when things are already open.

A few honest notes on softeners:

- They require a drain line and a salt supply, so placement matters.
- Softened water has a noticeably slippery feel that some people dislike.
- Some households choose to soften hot water only, which handles most of the scale problem in showers and sinks without softening drinking water.
- Confirm with your plumber whether your home's existing plumbing layout makes installation practical and what permit or licensing requirements apply.

According to the City of Sioux Falls, a plumbing contractor license is required for anyone doing plumbing work within city limits, and the city does not accept licenses from other states. If a remodeler is suggesting softener work, verify that the person doing the plumbing holds a current City license. The City publishes a Currently Licensed Contractors list you can check directly.

A whole-house softener is a meaningful investment. Get a written quote that separates the equipment cost from the installation labor, and ask what routine maintenance the system requires.
