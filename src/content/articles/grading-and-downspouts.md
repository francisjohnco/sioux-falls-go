Before you spend thousands on interior drainage or a sump upgrade, walk around your house on a dry day and look at where the ground slopes and where your downspouts drop water. For many Sioux Falls homeowners, those two things are the entire cause of a wet corner every spring. The fix can cost less than an afternoon of work and a few bags of topsoil. If water has already gotten inside or you want the full picture of what causes basement moisture here, start with [A Dry Basement in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/basement-waterproofing-guide-sioux-falls).

This checklist covers yard grading, downspout extensions, window wells, landscaping choices, and how to confirm it all worked after the next rain.

## Yard Grading and Your Basement, the Basics

The ground around your foundation is supposed to slope away from the house, not toward it.

When a home settles over time, or when landscaping gets added and regraded by previous owners, low spots form right next to the foundation wall. Water pools there, saturates the soil, and finds its way in through the cove joint or a small crack.

Sioux Falls sits on expansive clay soil. Clay holds water instead of draining it, which makes a negative grade especially damaging here compared to sandier soils.

The standard recommendation from most building guides is a slope of at least six inches of drop over the first ten feet away from the foundation. You do not need a laser level. A long board and a cheap line level from a hardware store will show you what you have.

What to look for during your walk-around:

- Soil that has pulled away from the foundation, leaving a gap or moat
- Mulch beds that slope toward the house
- Settled concrete sidewalk sections that now tilt inward
- Low spots in the lawn within about ten feet of the wall

If you find a gap between the soil and the foundation, fill it with compacted clay-heavy topsoil, not garden mix, which drains too fast. Pack it down and slope it away. This is one of the most common fixes that costs almost nothing.

## Downspout Discharge, Where Your Water Actually Goes

A downspout that dumps water one foot from your foundation is a slow drip onto the same soil your basement wall sits behind. During a heavy spring rain or a fast snowmelt, that concentrated discharge is a real problem.

Downspout extensions are sold at every hardware store in Sioux Falls for a few dollars each. The rigid plastic kind you snap together can get water four to six feet away from the house. Flexible corrugated pipe can go farther if you bury it slightly or run it along the ground.

Where the water lands also matters:

- It should discharge onto a slope that keeps carrying it away
- It should not aim at a neighbor's property or a city easement in a way that creates runoff problems
- Underground extensions that connect to a dry well or daylight outlet work well but need occasional flushing to stay clear

Check every downspout on your house. A common mistake is fixing the one on the problem corner and forgetting the one on the opposite end that feeds a low spot in the yard.

If you have splash blocks, make sure they are still sloping outward and have not settled backward over time. Flip them around or replace them if they have.

## Window Wells and the Water They Collect

A window well that fills with water is a direct path into the basement, and it is easy to overlook until it overflows.

The well itself should have a layer of gravel at the bottom, at least a few inches deep, so water can drain rather than pool. Many older Sioux Falls homes have wells with no gravel, or the gravel has silted over completely.

For more detail on what can go wrong and how to fix it, the [Window Wells That Leak](/guides/window-wells) guide covers drains, covers, and egress requirements in depth.

Quick checklist for window wells:

- Gravel present and not compacted into mud
- Drain at the bottom not clogged with debris
- Top of the well extends at least a couple of inches above grade so surface water does not run straight in
- Cover in place (a dome or flat cover keeps rain and leaves out without blocking egress)

If a well drain runs down to a gravel pit below the footer, check that the pit has not filled with clay fines over the years. A clogged pit turns the well into a bucket.

## Landscaping Choices That Help or Hurt

Mulch is the hidden troublemaker in a lot of wet basement situations. A thick mulch bed holds moisture right against the foundation for days after rain. Over time, mulch also breaks down and settles, creating exactly the backward slope you do not want.

A few better practices:

- Keep mulch at least six inches back from the foundation wall itself
- Use river rock or crushed stone near the house where mulch is tempting, since stone drains instead of holding water
- Avoid planting shrubs or trees with aggressive roots close to the foundation wall
- Do not let ivy or ground cover grow up against the foundation, since it traps moisture and hides cracks

Deep-rooted perennials planted farther out can actually help by pulling water from the soil. The goal is to keep the zone right next to the house as dry and well-drained as possible.

## How to Confirm It Worked After Rain

Making changes and then never checking is a common mistake. The only real test is a wet event, and you have to be watching.

After the next significant rain or during spring snowmelt, go outside while it is still raining or immediately after:

- Watch where water flows on the ground around your foundation
- Look for any pooling within ten feet of the house
- Check each downspout discharge point and confirm water is moving away
- Look inside at the basement corners and wall base for any new moisture

Also check your window wells during the rain, not just after. If a well is filling faster than it drains, you will see it in real time.

If you corrected the grade, extended the downspouts, and cleared the window well drains and water is still getting into the same corner, the problem is likely coming from a source that surface work cannot fix. That usually means hydrostatic pressure through the foundation wall itself, or a crack that needs attention. The [Foundation Cracks, Which Ones Matter](/guides/foundation-cracks) guide can help you figure out what you are looking at.

## What to Do If Surface Fixes Are Not Enough

Grading and downspouts solve a lot of problems, and they should always be the first thing you try because they cost almost nothing. But they only control surface water and roof runoff.

If your basement gets wet from groundwater rising through the floor or seeping through the wall at the cove joint, that is a different mechanism entirely. It means the water table or subsurface drainage is the driver, not what falls on your yard.

In that case, surface fixes will help at the margins but will not solve the problem. That is when interior drainage systems, exterior waterproofing, or a properly sized sump pump enter the conversation.
