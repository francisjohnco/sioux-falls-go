Choosing the right insulation type is not just a budget decision. It depends on where in your home you are insulating, what your existing structure looks like, and what problems you are actually trying to solve. The full picture of how these materials fit together in a Sioux Falls home is in [Insulating a Sioux Falls Home, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/insulation-guide-sioux-falls). This article focuses on one specific question: which material, in which situation, and why.

There is no single best insulation type. Each of the three main options (blown-in, batts, and spray foam) does something the others do not, and in many homes the right answer is two of them used together.

## How the Three Types Actually Work

Blown-in insulation (also called loose-fill) is exactly what it sounds like: small particles of cellulose or fiberglass blown through a hose until they fill a space.

It conforms to joists, pipes, and odd angles without cutting, which is why Sioux Falls homeowners often praise blown-in attic jobs as fast and thorough. A crew can fill an attic floor in a single visit, and the material settles into gaps that batts would bridge over.

Batts are pre-cut blankets of fiberglass or mineral wool fitted between studs or joists. They are the material most people picture when they hear "insulation."

Batts work well when framing is regular and accessible, but they depend heavily on a tight fit. A batt that is compressed, torn, or missing even a small section at the edge loses a significant portion of its rated R-value. That gap at the rim joist or the corner of a knee wall matters more than most people expect.

Spray foam comes in two forms: open-cell and closed-cell. Open-cell is softer and vapor-permeable. Closed-cell is dense, rigid, and acts as both an air barrier and a vapor retarder.

Both types expand to fill the space they are sprayed into, which is what makes spray foam the most reliable air sealer of the three. That property is also what makes it the most expensive and the most difficult to remove or update later.

## Where Each Type Fits Best in a Sioux Falls Home

**Blown-in** is the standard choice for attic floors, and for good reason.

It is easy to add depth over existing insulation, it handles irregular joist bays, and it does not require removing drywall. When homeowners describe rooms that stay cold no matter what the thermostat says, the attic floor is often the first place to check. Adding blown-in depth on top of properly air-sealed attic floor penetrations is one of the most reliable fixes.

**Batts** are common in walls during new construction or during a gut renovation, where studs are exposed and spacing is predictable. Mineral wool batts are denser and more resistant to moisture than fiberglass, which matters in areas prone to occasional condensation. They are also the typical choice for floors over unconditioned garages when the floor joists are accessible from below.

If you have a room over your garage that never feels warm, the floor assembly is usually the problem. See [Warming a Room Over the Garage](/guides/room-over-garage) for what to look for and what to ask a contractor.

**Closed-cell spray foam** is the material of choice for rim joists, crawl spaces, and any location where you need air sealing and vapor control in one application. Rim joists sit right at grade level on a Sioux Falls home, exposed to cold outside air and subject to the freeze-thaw cycling that is a fact of life here. Spray foam bonds to the framing and foundation, creates no gaps, and does not sag or shift over time.

Open-cell spray foam is sometimes used in attic rafters (a "conditioned attic" approach) or in interior wall assemblies where vapor permeability is desired. This is a more specialized application that depends on your home's specific construction.

## Air Sealing Comes Before the Insulation Type

Here is the thing most homeowners do not realize until they have already added insulation and still have cold rooms: insulation slows heat movement, but it does not stop air movement.

Air leaks around recessed lights, plumbing penetrations, and attic hatches carry far more heat out of your home than conduction through the insulation itself. Adding more blown-in on top of an attic floor full of open penetrations is like adding a thicker blanket while leaving the window cracked.

The right sequence is:
- Seal the air leaks first (caulk, canned foam, rigid blocking)
- Then add insulation depth on top

Spray foam is the one exception, because it handles both steps at once. For everything else, air sealing is a separate step and needs to happen before the insulation goes in. [Air Sealing, the Cheapest Comfort Upgrade](/guides/air-sealing-basics) explains where those leaks typically hide and what the sealing process looks like.

## Mixing Types: When It Makes Sense

Many well-insulated Sioux Falls homes use more than one material, and that is completely normal.

A common combination is spray foam at the rim joists, blown-in on the attic floor, and batts in the wall cavities. Each material is doing the job it is best suited for, and there is no conflict between them.

What does not work is layering materials that fight each other's moisture behavior. Closed-cell spray foam on the inside of an exterior wall, for example, can trap moisture in the wrong location depending on how the wall is constructed. If a contractor is proposing a mixed assembly you are not sure about, ask them directly: where does the vapor drive go in winter, and where does moisture exit this assembly?

The question of wet spots on upstairs ceilings or ice on attic framing in January is usually a sign that moisture is moving through air leaks, not just conducting through the insulation. That is a different problem than R-value, and it needs a different fix first. [Frost and Wet Spots in the Attic in January](/guides/frost-in-attic) covers exactly that situation.

## What to Ask Before Any Crew Starts

Before you agree to any insulation job, get clear answers to these questions:

- Will air sealing be done before insulation is added, and what exactly will be sealed?
- Are you proposing to remove any existing insulation, and if so, why?
- For attic work: how will you keep soffit vents clear?
- For spray foam: is this open-cell or closed-cell, and why is that the right choice for this location?
- Can you show me photos of the existing conditions and the finished work?

Homeowners who are most satisfied with insulation projects consistently mention two things: crews who explained what they found and showed photos before and after. That transparency is not a luxury. It is how you know the work was actually done correctly in the places you cannot see.
