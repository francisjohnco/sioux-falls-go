Most people start a kitchen remodel with a number in mind and a vague sense that cabinets and countertops will eat most of it. That is roughly true, but the details matter a lot. Understanding where every dollar category goes, before you get quotes, is what separates a smooth project from one that blows past your budget and leaves you eating takeout for three extra months. This article breaks down the real line items so you can read a bid intelligently and ask the right questions. For the full picture of how a kitchen remodel works from start to finish, see [Remodeling a Kitchen in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/kitchen-remodel-guide-sioux-falls).

## Cabinets Take the Biggest Slice of Any Kitchen Budget

Cabinets typically represent the largest single cost category in a kitchen remodel, often accounting for a third to nearly half of the total budget depending on what you choose.

Stock cabinets (the ones a big-box store stocks on the shelf) cost the least but offer limited sizes and finishes. Semi-custom cabinets are built to order in standard increments, giving you more options. Full custom cabinets are built to your exact dimensions and details, and priced accordingly.

What drives cost inside each tier:

- Box construction (plywood vs. particleboard)
- Drawer boxes and slides (soft-close vs. standard)
- Door style and finish
- Interior fittings like pull-out shelves and drawer organizers
- Hardware, which is easy to underestimate

One thing Sioux Falls homeowners mention repeatedly in reviews of local cabinet shops is that a designer who plans every inch of the kitchen saves money, because there are no awkward gaps or wasted corners that require filler pieces or afterthought fixes. If your contractor does not offer a layout drawing with measurements, ask for one before anything is ordered. See [Ordering Cabinets and Avoiding Delays](/guides/cabinet-lead-times) for what to confirm before the order is placed.

Also worth knowing: if your existing cabinet boxes are solid, you may not need to replace them at all. Refacing the doors and hardware can dramatically change the look at a fraction of the cost. [Refinish, Reface or Replace Kitchen Cabinets?](/guides/reface-or-replace-cabinets) walks through when that trade-off makes sense.

## Countertops, Appliances, and Finishes

Countertops are the second-largest visible expense for most kitchens. Material choice drives cost more than size does, in most cases.

Common options range from laminate on the low end, through butcher block and ceramic tile, up to quartz, granite, and quartzite at the top. Each has real trade-offs around heat resistance, seam visibility, maintenance, and how it holds up to everyday use. [Choosing Countertops You Will Still Like](/guides/countertop-materials) covers those trade-offs in detail without pushing any particular product.

Appliances are a separate line item that homeowners sometimes forget to include until they are already deep in the planning. A new range, refrigerator, and dishwasher add up quickly, especially if you are upgrading from builder-grade to something with more features.

Other finish items that belong in your budget from day one:

- Backsplash tile and installation
- Sink and faucet (more than you might expect, especially if you want something durable)
- Lighting fixtures and under-cabinet lighting
- Flooring, if you are replacing it at the same time

Each of these can be modest or extravagant. The mistake is treating them as "small stuff" and not budgeting for them until the contractor asks.

## Labor Is Often Underestimated, Especially for Trades

Labor is where kitchen budgets surprise people most, partly because it is less visible than a beautiful quartz countertop.

General labor covers demolition, installation, and trim work. But the trades are separate: a licensed electrician for new circuits or moved outlets, a licensed plumber if you are moving the sink or adding a dishwasher line, and an HVAC contractor if your ventilation needs to change. According to the City of Sioux Falls, projects involving electrical, mechanical, or plumbing systems require permits, and those permits require licensed contractors to do the work (unless you own and live in the home and pull a homeowner permit for the work you do yourself).

That permit reality has a budget consequence: if your design moves the sink even a few feet, or if you are adding an island with a sink, you are adding licensed plumbing work, a plumbing permit, and possibly new drain and supply runs. That is not a reason to avoid the move, but it is a reason to know about it before you fall in love with a layout.

## The Permit Line Item Most Homeowners Skip

Permits are not glamorous, but skipping them can cost you far more than the permit fee when you sell your home or file an insurance claim.

According to the City of Sioux Falls, cabinets, countertops, flooring, and painting do not require a permit. But electrical, plumbing, and mechanical work does, full stop. If your quote does not include permit costs as a line item, ask the contractor how permits are handled and who pulls them.

A contractor who pulls permits is taking responsibility for the work passing inspection. One who suggests skipping permits to "save money" is shifting legal and financial risk onto you as the homeowner. ## Surprises, Contingency, and Reading an Itemized Bid

Every experienced remodeler will tell you to hold back a contingency of roughly 10 to 20 percent of your total budget for surprises. This is not pessimism; it reflects what actually happens in kitchen projects.

Common surprises in Sioux Falls homes include:

- Water damage or mold behind the existing sink cabinet
- Outdated wiring that cannot support modern appliances and needs upgrading
- Subfloor damage that only appears after old flooring is removed
- Cabinets that are not square to the walls (older homes in neighborhoods like McKennan Park and All Saints often have walls that have shifted over decades)

An itemized bid is your main protection against vague surprise costs. A good bid breaks out materials from labor, lists each trade separately, and specifies what is and is not included. If a bid just gives you one total number, ask for the line items. If a contractor refuses, that tells you something important. One thing homeowners consistently say after a good remodel experience is that a detailed estimate and a timeline that stayed on target made all the difference. The contractors who provide those things upfront are showing you how they work. The ones who cannot or will not give you that level of detail are showing you something too.
