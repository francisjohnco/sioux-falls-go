A sagging gate is one of the most common fence complaints from Sioux Falls homeowners, and it rarely fixes itself. Left alone, a gate that drags or stops latching puts stress on the whole frame and usually gets worse each season. The good news is that most sagging gates have one or two fixable causes, and you can often spot them in an afternoon. If you want the full picture on fencing around your home, start with [Building a Fence in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/fence-guide-sioux-falls).

## Why Gates Sag: The Real Causes

A gate is essentially a heavy frame hung on a post, and anything that shifts the post or weakens the connection will cause a sag.

The most common culprits in Sioux Falls are:

- **Frost heave.** The ground here is clay-heavy and freezes deep. A gate post that wasn't set deep enough or poured with enough concrete can lift, tip, or twist over winter. When the post moves, the gate moves with it.
- **Loose or stripped hinge screws.** Wood compresses and expands with humidity and temperature. Over a few seasons, hinge screws can back out of their holes, leaving the gate hanging unevenly.
- **Rotted wood at the hinge block or post.** Once wood starts to rot at the connection point, no amount of tightening will hold.
- **A sagging diagonal brace (or missing one entirely).** A rectangular gate frame with no diagonal support will rack out of square. Gravity and use do the rest.

Understanding which cause you're dealing with tells you whether this is a one-hour fix or a bigger project.

## The Checklist: How to Diagnose Your Sagging Gate

Work through this list before buying any hardware. The diagnosis determines the fix.

**Step 1: Check the gate post.**

Stand at the gate and look down the post from above. Is it plumb, or does it lean toward or away from the gate?

Push the post firmly at the top. Any movement at all is a problem. A solid post should feel like it's part of the ground.

If the post leans, moves, or has heaved visibly above grade, that is your primary problem. No hinge adjustment will fix a compromised post. See [Why Fence Posts Heave and Lean](/guides/fence-posts-frost-heave) for more on what causes this and what your options are.

**Step 2: Inspect the hinges.**

Open the gate fully and look at every hinge. Check for:

- Screws that are backed out or spinning freely
- Hinge plates pulling away from the wood
- Rust or corrosion that has weakened the hardware
- Cracks in the wood directly around the hinge

If the screws spin freely in stripped holes, you can often fix this with longer screws (3 inches or longer, into solid wood) or by filling the holes with hardwood dowels and re-driving.

**Step 3: Check the gate frame for square.**

Measure the gate diagonally, corner to corner, both ways. If the two measurements differ by more than a half inch or so, the frame has racked out of square.

A racked frame will not latch properly no matter how well the hinges are set. This is where a tension rod or turnbuckle brace comes in.

**Step 4: Look at the latch.**

A gate not latching is sometimes just the latch hardware itself, not the gate position. Check whether the latch strike plate has shifted, corroded, or simply needs adjustment.

If the latch bolt is hitting wood or missing the strike entirely, the frame or post has moved. Fixing the latch without fixing the cause is a temporary patch.

**Step 5: Look for rot.**

Press firmly on the wood at the hinge block and at the base of the gate post. Soft, spongy, or discolored wood means decay. Rot that has reached a structural area (hinge block, post base) means replacement, not repair.

## Fixes That Actually Work

Once you know the cause, here is what actually addresses it.

**Stripped hinge screws:** Remove the screws, fill each hole with a wood glue and sawdust mix or a hardwood dowel, let it cure fully, then redrive with longer, heavier screws through the hinge plate. If the wood is otherwise solid, this fix holds well.

**Racked gate frame:** A gate anti-sag kit or a tension rod with turnbuckle runs diagonally from the top hinge corner down to the bottom latch corner. Tightening the turnbuckle lifts the latch side. These kits are widely available at hardware stores. Follow the kit directions for your gate width.

**Heaved or leaning post:** This is the most serious fix. A post that has moved needs to be reset, not shimmed. That typically means digging out, resetting at the correct depth, and re-pouring. Because gate posts carry more load than line posts, some contractors set them deeper or with a wider footing. Ask your contractor what approach they recommend for your soil conditions.

**Rot at the hinge block:** If only the hinge block is rotted, a carpenter can sometimes sister in new wood. If the post itself is rotted at the base, it needs full replacement.

**Latch adjustment:** Most latch strike plates have slotted mounting holes for minor adjustment. Loosen, shift, and retighten before assuming the fix is bigger than it is.

## Permits and What the City Requires

Repair work feels minor, but the City of Sioux Falls does require a permit to repair or replace a fence, according to the City's fence permit pamphlet.

The permit fee is $20, and a final inspection is required after construction. Permits expire six months after issue, with extensions available. To confirm what applies to your specific repair, contact the Zoning Office at 605-367-8254.

If your post replacement involves digging new holes, call 811 before you dig. See [Call 811 Before You Dig a Fence Line](/guides/call-811-before-digging) for how the locate process works and how far in advance to call.

If your property is in a neighborhood with an HOA or covenants, check those rules before making any changes to gate hardware, style, or height. Changes that look minor can still require approval.

## When to Rebuild Instead of Repair

Some gates are past the point where repair makes economic sense. Consider rebuilding if:

- The post is rotted at or below grade
- The gate frame has multiple rotted members
- The hinges have failed repeatedly in the same spots
- The gate has never latched correctly since installation

A fresh gate hung on a properly set post, with a diagonal brace built in from the start, will outlast a series of patchwork repairs on a compromised frame.

When getting quotes for a rebuild, ask contractors to specify post depth, footing type, and what hardware they use for gate hinges. Gate-specific hardware (heavier gauge, adjustable) costs more than standard hinge sets but makes a real difference in long-term performance.
