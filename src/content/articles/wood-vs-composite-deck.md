Choosing between wood and composite decking is one of the first real decisions you'll make when planning a new deck, and it's worth getting right before you talk to a single contractor. The material you pick affects how the deck looks, how much work it needs every few years, how it handles a Sioux Falls winter, and how long it's likely to last. If you haven't read [Building a Deck in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/deck-guide-sioux-falls) yet, start there, then come back here for the deep dive on materials.

## How Sioux Falls Weather Affects Both Materials

Sioux Falls has a freeze-thaw climate, which means your deck surface cycles between frozen and thawed dozens of times in a single winter. That movement is hard on any material, but it hits wood and composite differently.

Wood expands and contracts with moisture and temperature. Each freeze-thaw cycle works at any crack, check, or spot where water has gotten in, and over time that adds up to cupped boards, cracked grain, and fastener pop.

Composite boards also expand and contract, but they're engineered to handle moisture better than natural wood. The tradeoff is that cheap or older composite products can sag over long spans if the spacing between joists isn't right for the product, so your builder needs to know what joist spacing the manufacturer requires.

Both materials can get slippery in winter. Ask any contractor you're considering how they handle that, specifically whether the decking profile has a textured surface grip.

## Wood Decking: What It Actually Takes to Maintain It

Wood is the material most Sioux Falls homeowners grew up with, and it's genuinely beautiful when it's cared for. The honest answer is that it needs regular attention to stay that way.

A wood deck needs to be cleaned, then stained or sealed on a regular cycle. Skipping even one cycle lets moisture in, and once moisture gets into end grain or a fastener hole, rot can follow. For more detail on that process, [Staining and Sealing a Wood Deck](/guides/deck-stain-seal) walks through timing, prep, and what to ask about products.

What maintenance looks like in practice:

- Clean the deck surface each spring after snowmelt
- Inspect for cracked boards, raised fasteners, and soft spots
- Re-stain or re-seal on the schedule your contractor or the product label recommends
- Pay attention to where boards butt against the house, railings, or stairs, because those joints hold moisture

The payoff for that work is a natural look that's hard to replicate, and wood is repairable in a way composite isn't. A single bad board on a wood deck can be pulled and replaced without a color-match puzzle.

## Composite Decking: Lower Upkeep, Different Trade-Offs

Composite decking is not maintenance-free, but it is significantly lower maintenance than wood. You won't stain it, seal it, or sand it. Most homeowners clean composite with soap and water in the spring and call it done.

Where composite earns its keep in Sioux Falls specifically is in freeze-thaw resistance. Quality composite products don't absorb water the way wood does, so the cycle of freezing and thawing has less to work with. That matters when you're looking at a deck that sits through five or six months of cold.

The honest trade-offs:

- Composite gets hotter in direct afternoon sun than wood does, which matters on south-facing decks
- Surface texture varies by product, and some profiles hold dirt and pollen in the grooves
- Color fades over time on most products, though capped composite fades more slowly than older uncapped products
- Repair is harder because matching color exactly years later is not guaranteed

Composite also has specific installation requirements. Joist spacing, fastener type, and ventilation under the boards all matter and vary by manufacturer. A contractor who doesn't follow those specs can void the product warranty, so this is worth asking about specifically when you [compare quotes](/guides/deck-quotes).

## Snow, Ice, and the Surface You're Standing On

Snow removal is a real Sioux Falls consideration. Both wood and composite can become slippery when icy, but the textured surface of most composite products gives them an edge over smooth-finished wood.

A few things worth knowing:

- Plastic or rubber snow shovels are gentler on both surfaces than metal
- Ice melt products with salt can accelerate corrosion on metal fasteners and hardware in wood decks
- Some ice melt formulas can discolor composite, so read the product label before you use anything
- Composite boards with deeper grooves can trap ice in those channels, which refreezes overnight

For more on managing your deck through cold months, [Caring for a Deck Through Winter](/guides/deck-winter-care) covers shoveling, ice melt, furniture storage, and what to look for in spring.

## Lifespan and What Makes a Deck Last

Neither material lasts if the structure underneath it fails. The decking boards, whether wood or composite, sit on top of a frame, and that frame is what determines whether you're rebuilding in fifteen years or still using the deck in thirty.

A well-built wood deck with proper drainage, good flashing at the ledger, and regular staining can last decades. A wood deck that was built without proper ledger flashing, or whose footings don't go deep enough to stay below the frost line, will fail well before the material gives out. The City of Sioux Falls Building Services can tell you the required footing depth for your project; don't assume, and don't let a contractor skip that conversation.

Composite boards themselves typically outlast wood boards because the material resists rot and insect damage. But composite boards on a poorly built frame still fail early.

Questions to ask any builder before you commit to a material:

- What joist spacing do you use for this product, and does that match what the manufacturer requires?
- How do you handle the gap between boards for drainage and expansion?
- What fastener system do you use, and is it rated for this product?
- What does the warranty cover, and who backs it, the manufacturer or you?

The most important thing is that whoever builds your deck holds a current Residential Building Contractor's License from the City of Sioux Falls. The City does not accept out-of-state licenses, and you can verify any contractor at siouxfalls.gov/licensed-contractors before you sign anything.
