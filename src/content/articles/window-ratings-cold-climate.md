If you have ever seen frost creeping across the inside of your window glass on a January night in Sioux Falls, that is not just a cosmetic annoyance. It is a heat-loss problem you can measure, and the number that measures it is called U-factor. Understanding U-factor and its partner rating, SHGC, is the fastest way to tell whether a window quote is actually good for a South Dakota winter or just sounds good.

This article is part of a series. If you are still deciding whether replacement is the right move at all, start with [Replacing Windows in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/window-replacement-guide-sioux-falls) before diving into the ratings below.

## What U-Factor Actually Measures

U-factor tells you how quickly a window loses heat from inside your home to the cold air outside. The scale runs roughly from 0.15 to 1.20. A lower number means the window holds heat better.

Think of it this way: a single pane of clear glass has a U-factor around 1.0. A well-built triple-pane window can reach 0.15. The difference on a minus-15-degree Sioux Falls night is not subtle.

According to ENERGY STAR Version 7.0, effective October 23, 2023, windows in the Northern climate zone need a U-factor of 0.22 or lower to qualify. Sioux Falls sits in that Northern zone, so that 0.22 ceiling is the number to keep in front of you when you review any quote.

A window rated 0.27 might look fine on paper and might even be marketed as energy efficient windows South Dakota installers commonly carry. But it does not meet Northern zone ENERGY STAR criteria, and you will likely feel the difference near the glass on the coldest nights of the year.

## What SHGC Means and Why It Still Matters Here

SHGC stands for Solar Heat Gain Coefficient. It measures how much of the sun's heat passes through the glass into your home, on a scale of 0 to 1.

A higher SHGC lets in more solar warmth. A lower SHGC blocks more of it, which is useful in a hot climate but can work against you in South Dakota, where free solar heat in winter is genuinely valuable.

ENERGY STAR Version 7.0 sets the Northern zone floor at an SHGC of 0.17 or higher. That lower limit exists precisely because windows that block too much solar gain are a poor trade for a cold climate. The spec also allows what ENERGY STAR calls equivalent-performance options, where a slightly higher U-factor may qualify if the SHGC is higher, capturing more passive solar heat to offset the modest difference in conduction loss.

For most Sioux Falls homes, a window U-factor at or below 0.22 paired with an SHGC somewhere in the 0.25 to 0.40 range is a reasonable target. But check the label on the exact window in your quote, and confirm it matches your county on the ENERGY STAR climate zone map, since counties just outside the city limits can occasionally fall in a different zone.

## Air Leakage, the Rating Nobody Talks About

U-factor and SHGC get most of the attention, but air leakage (AL) is what explains why some homeowners describe their new windows as still drafty, or say they can still hear highway traffic after a full replacement.

Air leakage is measured in cubic feet per minute per square foot of window area. A rating of 0.30 or lower is generally considered acceptable. Better windows are closer to 0.10.

Here is what that number does not tell you: even a window with an excellent air leakage rating will be drafty if it is installed poorly. Gaps between the frame and the rough opening, missing or compressed insulation, and inadequate sealant at the perimeter are installation failures, not product failures. When you read reviews where a homeowner says the new window feels worse than the old one, that is almost always an install issue, not a glass issue.

Ask any installer what air leakage rating the quoted window carries, and ask specifically how they handle the gap between the window frame and the existing framing or jamb. Those two questions will tell you a lot.

## How to Compare Two Quotes Using the Numbers

Homeowners frequently describe wanting quotes that compare the same window and the same install method. That is exactly the right instinct, and the label data makes it possible.

Every NFRC-rated window ships with a label that lists U-factor, SHGC, visible transmittance (VT), and air leakage. When you have two quotes in front of you, do this:

- Confirm both quotes list the NFRC label ratings, not just marketing phrases like "triple pane" or "low-E glass."
- Check that both windows meet U-factor 0.22 or lower and SHGC 0.17 or higher per ENERGY STAR Northern zone Version 7.0.
- Compare air leakage ratings if provided. Lower is better.
- Ask whether the quoted price is for a full-frame replacement or an insert (pocket) replacement. The install method affects both performance and long-term durability.
- Ask what insulation material goes in the gap around the frame and how it is sealed at the interior and exterior.

## Reading the ENERGY STAR Label Before You Sign

The ENERGY STAR label on a window is a third-party certification, not a marketing claim. It means the product was tested by an accredited lab and the ratings are verified.

When a salesperson says a window is "ENERGY STAR certified," ask them to show you the label and confirm it is certified for the Northern climate zone, not just one of the warmer zones. ENERGY STAR has four climate zones and the thresholds are different for each. A window certified only for the North-Central zone does not meet the tighter Northern zone standard that applies to Sioux Falls.

Also confirm that the label reflects the exact configuration you are buying. Ratings can change with different glass packages, frame materials, or spacer types within the same product line. The window in the showroom and the window in your quote should have matching model numbers and matching label data.

## One More Thing About the Federal Tax Credit

For several years, the federal Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit (Section 25C) gave homeowners a reason to prioritize high-rated windows. According to the IRS, that credit is not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025.

If a salesperson mentions a federal window tax credit as part of a pitch, verify the current status directly with a tax professional or IRS.gov before factoring it into your decision.

The ratings still matter, the credit situation just no longer sweetens the deal the way it once did.
