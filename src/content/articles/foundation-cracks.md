A crack in your basement wall is one of those things you notice once and then can't stop thinking about. Some cracks are genuinely harmless. Others are early warnings of a serious structural problem. Knowing the difference saves you from both unnecessary panic and from missing something that actually needs attention. This article is a companion to [A Dry Basement in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/basement-waterproofing-guide-sioux-falls), focused specifically on reading the cracks you find.

Sioux Falls homeowners have a particular reason to pay attention. The expansive clay soil common throughout this region swells when wet and contracts when dry, putting cycles of pressure on foundation walls that other parts of the country simply don't experience the same way.

## The Crack That's Usually Fine: Hairline Shrinkage Cracks

Poured concrete shrinks as it cures. That is not a defect, it is chemistry.

The result is often a network of very fine, shallow cracks, usually vertical or slightly diagonal, that appear in the first few years after a home is built. These are called shrinkage cracks or settlement cracks.

What makes them low concern:

- They are hairline-thin, meaning you can barely fit a credit card edge into them
- They are consistent in width from top to bottom
- They are dry, with no staining, efflorescence (white mineral deposits), or moisture around them
- They haven't changed in months or years

The honest answer is that most hairline cracks in older Sioux Falls basements are cosmetic. That said, any crack is a potential water entry point. If yours is staying dry and stable, monitor it. If water starts finding it, that changes the conversation.

A simple way to monitor: draw a small pencil mark at each end of the crack and date it. Check back in three to six months. If the crack has grown past your marks, something is moving.

## Cracks That Leak: Why Water Changes the Math

A crack that leaks is a different problem than a crack that doesn't, even if the crack itself is small.

Water moving through a foundation crack is under pressure. Over time it carries fine particles of soil with it, which can hollow out voids behind the wall. It also deposits minerals on the inside face, which is what causes that chalky white efflorescence you see on older basement walls.

Sioux Falls homeowners frequently describe water in one corner every spring. That seasonal pattern usually means snowmelt or rain is saturating the soil against the foundation and finding a path through a crack or the cove joint (where the floor meets the wall). The crack may look minor but it is acting as a drain for a much larger water problem outside.

If a crack is leaking, the right question is not just "how do I seal this crack" but "why is water reaching this wall in the first place." Grading, downspouts, and soil saturation all play a role. [Grading and Downspouts, the Cheapest Basement Fix](/guides/grading-and-downspouts) covers the outside factors worth checking before you spend money on interior repairs.

A leaking crack that gets ignored tends to widen over time. Freeze-thaw cycles do real work on wet concrete, expanding water trapped inside the crack each winter.

## Cracks That Signal Structural Movement: The Ones to Take Seriously

Some crack patterns are not about concrete shrinkage or minor water infiltration. They indicate that the wall itself is moving.

**Horizontal cracks** are the most serious type in a basement wall. A horizontal crack, especially one running across a block or poured concrete wall at mid-height, means the wall is bending inward under lateral soil pressure. This is a structural issue, not a cosmetic one. Do not wait to get a structural engineer's opinion if you find one.

**Stair-step cracks** run diagonally through the mortar joints of a block wall, following the pattern of the blocks. They indicate differential settlement, meaning one part of the foundation is sinking faster than another. The severity depends on how wide the crack is and whether it is actively growing. A narrow, stable stair-step crack may be minor. A wide or growing one needs professional evaluation.

**Diagonal cracks from corners** are common and often caused by soil settlement or shrinkage, but a diagonal crack that is wider at one end than the other suggests active movement. Width variation matters.

**Widening cracks** of any type are more concerning than stable ones. If your pencil-mark monitoring shows the crack is growing, that is worth a professional opinion regardless of the crack type.

For more on what happens when soil pressure causes walls to visibly bow or lean, [Bowing Basement Walls](/guides/bowing-basement-walls) goes deeper on that specific situation, including the signs that often show up elsewhere in the house like sticking doors.

## When to Call a Structural Engineer (Not Just a Waterproofing Company)

This is the question Sioux Falls homeowners get wrong most often, usually because the first phone call goes to a waterproofing contractor instead of an engineer.

Waterproofing companies are good at keeping water out. Structural engineers are trained to tell you whether your foundation is safe and what repair approach is actually appropriate. They are different skills.

Call a licensed structural engineer, not a contractor, when you see:

- Any horizontal crack in a basement wall
- A crack that is wider than about a quarter inch
- A crack you can see daylight or feel outside air through
- A wall that looks bowed, tilted, or out of plumb
- Stair-step cracking that is growing or uneven in width
- Cracks accompanied by sticking doors, sloping floors, or gaps at window frames upstairs

An engineer will give you an independent diagnosis. That matters because a waterproofing contractor has a financial interest in recommending waterproofing. An engineer's only job is to tell you what is actually happening and what needs to happen next. Many Sioux Falls homeowners have expressed frustration with being sold a whole system when their actual problem was simpler. Getting an independent structural opinion first protects you from that.

If you do move forward with a contractor after an engineer's report, verify they hold a current Residential Building Contractor's License with the City of Sioux Falls. The City publishes its list of currently licensed contractors at siouxfalls.gov/licensed-contractors and does not accept licenses from other states. [Questions to Ask a Waterproofing Contractor](/guides/choosing-waterproofing-contractor) covers what else to verify before you sign anything.

## How to Document and Monitor Cracks Before You Call Anyone

Good documentation makes every conversation with a contractor or engineer more productive.

Steps to take now:

- Photograph each crack with something for scale (a coin, a ruler)
- Note the location on a simple sketch of your basement walls
- Record the date
- Mark the ends of each crack with pencil and date the marks
- Note whether the crack is dry, damp, or actively wet
- Note whether it is consistent in width or wider at one end

Recheck in three months and again in six. Bring these photos to any professional you consult. An engineer especially will want to know whether a crack is new or has been stable for years.

If you are seeing moisture around any crack, also note the weather conditions when it appears. Spring-only leaking points to surface water. Leaking after any rain points to drainage issues. Leaking that seems unrelated to weather could indicate groundwater pressure, which is a different and more serious situation.

Keep these records even if nothing turns out to be wrong. If you sell the home, buyers will ask about foundation cracks, and documented evidence of stability is genuinely reassuring.
