A bowing basement wall is one of those things you spot once and cannot stop thinking about. The curve is subtle at first, then you notice a horizontal crack running across the block, and suddenly the word "structural" is running through your head. For Sioux Falls homeowners, this is not a rare worry. The expansive clay soil across much of the city pushes hard against foundation walls, and that pressure builds over years.

This article focuses specifically on bowing walls. For a broader look at keeping your basement dry overall, start with [A Dry Basement in Sioux Falls, the Homeowner's Guide](/guides/basement-waterproofing-guide-sioux-falls).

## Why Sioux Falls Soil Pushes So Hard on Basement Walls

The clay-heavy soil common throughout Sioux Falls absorbs water and swells. When it dries out, it shrinks. That repeated cycle, season after season, builds lateral (sideways) pressure against your foundation walls.

This is not abstract. Neighborhoods like McKennan Park and All Saints have older homes where clay soil has been cycling through wet and dry periods for decades. The cumulative pressure on those walls adds up over time.

Poured concrete walls handle lateral loads differently than concrete block (CMU) walls. Block walls are more vulnerable because the mortar joints between blocks are weak points. Horizontal cracks in block walls are almost always a sign that the wall is bending at that joint, and they deserve immediate attention.

Poured concrete walls tend to crack vertically or at corners first, but they can still bow. Either way, the shape of the crack and the direction it runs tells you something important, which is why a foundation engineer rather than just a contractor should be the first set of eyes on a serious situation.

## Warning Signs to Watch For

Some of these you can see from across the basement. Others need a closer look.

- A visible curve or lean in the wall (hold a long level or straight board against it)
- A horizontal crack running across a block wall, especially near the middle third of the wall height
- Stair-step cracks running diagonally through block mortar joints
- Doors or windows on the floor above that suddenly stick or won't latch
- Gaps opening up where the floor joists meet the top of the foundation wall
- White mineral deposits (efflorescence) or new moisture streaks near a crack

Sticking doors upstairs matter here because a foundation wall that moves pushes the structure above it. When Sioux Falls homeowners describe bowing walls and sticking doors in the same breath, they are describing the same problem showing up in two places at once.

If you see a horizontal crack that is wider than about the thickness of a nickel, or if the crack has shifted so the two sides are at different heights, stop waiting and call an engineer.

## When to Bring in a Structural Engineer

A waterproofing contractor can identify a bowing wall and recommend a repair product. A structural engineer can tell you how much the wall has already moved, whether it is still moving, and whether the repair approach actually matches the severity of the problem.

Those are different questions. You want both answered before spending money on a fix.

Ask the engineer to give you a written report that documents the degree of deflection (how far the wall has moved from plumb) and their repair recommendation. That report protects you when you go to sell the house, and it gives you an independent check on any contractor proposal.

If a contractor offers a free inspection but discourages you from also paying an engineer, that is a reason to pause. Homeowners who later said they felt oversold consistently described situations where no independent diagnosis ever happened.

## Repair Approaches in Plain Language

There is no single fix for a bowing wall. The right approach depends on how far the wall has moved, the wall material, and what is accessible on the exterior side.

**Wall anchors**
Wall anchors are one of the most common repairs for a bowing basement wall. A steel plate is attached to the inside of the wall, connected by a steel rod that runs through the soil to an anchor plate buried in the yard.

The idea is that the anchor in the yard resists the soil pressure, and over time, the rod can be gradually tightened (usually once or twice a year) to slowly pull the wall back toward plumb. That tightening process takes years, not weeks. It requires yard access and works best when the wall has not moved too far.

**Carbon fiber straps**
Carbon fiber straps are bonded vertically to the inside face of the wall with epoxy. They are strong in tension and resist further inward movement.

They do not pull the wall back toward straight. They stabilize it where it is. If the wall has moved significantly, stabilization may be acceptable, or it may not be enough. That is a judgment call for an engineer, not a sales brochure.

**Steel I-beams**
Steel beams can be installed floor-to-ceiling against a bowing wall to brace it. They transfer the lateral load to the floor slab and the ceiling structure.

This approach works when wall anchors are not possible (no yard access, buried utilities, finished landscaping) and can handle more severe deflection than carbon fiber alone.

**Excavation and rebuilding**
When a wall has moved too far or is severely cracked, the most reliable fix is to excavate the exterior, remove pressure from the soil, repair or replace the wall, and waterproof the exterior before backfilling. This is more disruptive and costly than interior methods, but sometimes it is the right answer.

Whatever method a contractor proposes, ask them to put in writing how much movement is acceptable before they recommend a different approach. Ask whether the repair is designed to stabilize or to restore the wall toward its original position. Those are different outcomes.

## Permits, Licensing, and What to Confirm

Structural foundation repairs in Sioux Falls require permits and inspections. The City of Sioux Falls requires a Residential Building Contractor's License for all contractors working on residential one and two family homes within city limits, and the City does not accept licenses from other states.

You can confirm any contractor's license on the City's publicly available list at siouxfalls.gov/licensed-contractors before anyone starts work.

Do not let a contractor skip the permit. A permit creates an inspection record. Without one, the repair does not appear in your home's permit history, which matters when you sell and when your insurance carrier has questions.
